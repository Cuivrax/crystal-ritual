Crystal & Ritual

About Crystal & Ritual

Crystal & Ritual is an independent encyclopedia of crystals and stones, written in US English. Every page tells you what a stone really is, where its meaning comes from and what you can actually do with it in everyday life. Beliefs are labeled as beliefs, facts are checked against the sources listed at the end of each page, and nothing here promises that a stone will heal you.

Who Writes Crystal & Ritual

Iris Holloway is the pen name of the founder and editor of Crystal & Ritual. It is a pen name, and we say so up front. The site claims no gemology diploma and no healer's certificate. What it offers instead is a method you can check on every page. The stone comes first, every belief is labeled with who held it and when, and every fact is checked against the sources listed at the end of the page.

Why the Name

Iris is for iris agate, an agate whose fine repeating structure splits light passing through a thin slice into rainbow colors. It is one of the well-known examples of iridescence made by repeating transparent structures, light split by the stone's build rather than by pigment.

A holloway is an English word for a sunken lane, a path worn down into the ground by centuries of feet, hooves and wheels. Rock shaped by small acts repeated over a long time felt like the right picture for a site about stones and everyday rituals.

What the Site Is

Each stone page is built in three labeled layers.

  1. The Stone

    What it is, from its chemistry and hardness to where it is found, how it is treated or imitated, and how to care for it. Every fact is sourced.

  2. The Story

    Where its meaning comes from, from old texts such as George Frederick Kunz's books of 1913 and 1915 to the modern crystal books and today's guides, each belief attributed.

  3. The Ritual

    Two or three realistic ways to use the stone in a normal day, at the desk, on the nightstand, in a coat pocket before a hard talk, with no promise attached.

Around the stone pages sit guides on care, real or fake stones and birthstones, pages on the needs people bring to crystals, and free tools such as the Crystal Care Checker and Tonight's Moon.

What the Site Is Not

  • Not medical advice. No stone treats, cures or prevents anything, and no page says it does. Pages close to health carry a box called When a Stone Is Not Enough, with plain advice to see a doctor, therapist, midwife or vet.
  • Not a shop. We do not sell crystals. No seller or brand pays to appear on a page. If a page ever earns money from a link, that page will say so.
  • Not a lab. No one can confirm what a stone is from a photo, and we will not pretend to. Our real or fake guides, starting with real citrine vs heated amethyst, explain what you can check at home and when only a gem lab can answer.
  • Not a debunking site. We do not mock anyone. Some readers use crystals spiritually, others simply love the colors, and both deserve accurate context.
  • Not fortune telling. Zodiac and birthstone pages tell the history of each link, not your future.

Who We Write For

The curious owner who just got a bracelet or a first tumbled stone and wants to know what it means. The respectful skeptic who enjoys the colors and the ritual but does not believe a rock heals anything. The careful buyer who wants to avoid dyed, glass or lab-made stones sold as natural. The gifter looking for something safe and kind. And the worried parent or pet owner who asks whether a stone is safe around kids, cats, water or sun.

How We Work

Facts about a stone come from reference sources such as GIA, the International Gem Society, the Smithsonian and the USGS. The old lore comes from public domain books, quoted with their page numbers. Today's beliefs are compared across many crystal guides in several languages and rated on our five-facet scale, which shows how widely a belief is held, not whether it works. AI tools help with research and drafting, and every fact is checked against its source before a page goes live. The details are in our editorial policy and on Sources & Methods.

When we test something on our own stones, the page will show the date and our photos. Until a test has actually been run, no page on this site says we tested anything.

Every page ends with its sources and the date it was last reviewed.
