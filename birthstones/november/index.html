November Birthstones

November Birthstone Meaning, History & Honest Buying Tips

The November birthstones are topaz and citrine, the two stones on the modern US list of the American Gem Society. Topaz was November's only stone on the 1912 jewelers' list, and citrine, a yellow quartz, joined it in 1952. Most topaz sold today is colorless topaz turned blue by treatment, and most citrine is heated amethyst, which is fine when the seller says so.

By Iris Holloway

What Is the November Birthstone?

Topaz and citrine, and topaz is the older answer by far, November's stone on all eight old lists George Frederick Kunz compared in 1913.

Modern vs Traditional November Birthstones

"Traditional" usually means topaz, the stone of the 1912 list and of every older list on our birthstone chart. "Modern" adds citrine, and the International Gem Society counts topaz of any color as traditional.

Why Does November Have Two Birthstones?

Mostly price and popularity. GIA's birthstone exhibit says "the more significant reason is that some gemstones are too rare and expensive, and others just aren't as popular." Citrine fits that pattern, sharing topaz's yellow to orange range at a far lower price. Kunz would have objected, writing that "there should be no duplication or triplication of birth-stones for any given month" (1913, pages 320 to 321).

Tradition

Where the November Birthstone Comes From

Month stones grew out of the twelve foundation stones of the New Jerusalem in the book of Revelation, counted from March, when the year was reckoned to begin (Kunz 1913, pages 307 to 308 and 319). The ninth foundation stone is a topaz (page 303), and the ninth month from March is November.

That topaz was probably green. Kunz wrote that "the topaz of the ancients was unquestionably the gem commonly called chrysolite at present," meaning peridot (page 406), and Pliny the Elder wrote that topazos was "held in very high estimation for its green tints" (Natural History, book 37, chapter 32). The names seem to have swapped, since "chrysolite," golden stone, was used for our yellow topaz (Kunz, pages 297 to 298).

Kunz's 1913 calendar of the months gives November a verse that begins "Who first comes to this world below / With drear November's fog and snow / Should prize the topaz's amber hue" (pages 330 to 331).

Mineral Fact

Topaz, the Traditional November Birthstone

The Stone. Topaz is a silicate of aluminum and fluorine, and it is the 8 on the Mohs scale, the ten minerals Friedrich Mohs chose in 1812 to rank scratch resistance. Most natural topaz is colorless, and the reds, pinks and violets owe their color to traces of chromium. Brazil is the principal source.

Hardness is only half the story. Topaz has perfect cleavage, a built-in plane along which a crystal can split, and poor toughness, meaning it chips and breaks easily. A stone that grit cannot scratch can still crack from one hard knock.

Why Most Topaz in Stores Is Blue

Natural blue topaz is extremely rare and usually pale. In the 1960s, treaters found that irradiating colorless topaz turns it brown and that heating then gives a stable blue, so close to nature that a treated stone cannot be told from a natural one. Before the 1970s, topaz in a jewelry case meant yellow, orange or brown. Since the 1970s and 1980s it has mostly meant blue, sold as Sky Blue, Swiss Blue or London Blue, light to dark.

Safety. Freshly irradiated topaz can be slightly radioactive, so US rules require it to be held until safe. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission says there is "no safety reason to stop wearing blue topaz or any other irradiated gems."

Imperial topaz is a prized reddish orange, mostly from Brazil. Mystic topaz is colorless topaz with a thin rainbow coating that scratches easily.

The Story. Some of this lore may belong to peridot, since the old name covered both.

  1. 13th century, India Tradition

    The physician Naharari wrote that whoever wears topaz "will be assured of long life, beauty and intelligence" (Kunz, The Magic of Jewels and Charms, 1915, page 158).

  2. Medieval talismans Tradition

    A falcon engraved on a topaz was said to win "the goodwill of kings, princes, and magnates" (Kunz 1913, page 133).

  3. 15th century, Rome Tradition

    A physician was reputed to cure plague sores with a topaz once owned by two popes, and Kunz suspected faith "may really have helped" (pages 389 to 390).

  4. Renaissance Europe Tradition

    People thought topaz could break magic spells and dispel anger.

Mineral Fact

Citrine, the Modern November Birthstone

The Stone. Citrine is the transparent, pale yellow to brownish orange variety of quartz, the same mineral as amethyst, colored by a trace of iron. Natural citrine is rare. Most citrine on the market is amethyst, or sometimes smoky quartz, heated, usually right at the mine, a stable treatment fully accepted in the trade. Almost all orange citrine geodes started out purple, and our real citrine vs heated amethyst guide shows how to spot them.

The Story.

  1. 1385

    "Citrine," from the French citron, lemon, was already used for yellow gems in English.

  2. 1538, Rome Tradition

    Benvenuto Cellini, jailed in Rome, believed diamond dust was a deadly poison. An enemy gave a gem-cutter a diamond to grind into his food, and the cutter kept it and ground a citrine instead, in Kunz's telling (1913, pages 154 to 155).

  3. A calming stone Tradition

    In ancient times, people believed citrine could calm tempers and soothe anger.

  4. 1952

    Citrine joined topaz when the Jewelry Industry Council of America revised the US list.

Is My Grandmother's Topaz Really Citrine?

It might be. The two were confused for centuries, and old names such as "Madeira topaz," "Brazilian topaz" and "topaz quartz" kept the mix-up alive. A gemologist can settle it. Topaz is much denser, with a specific gravity (weight against the same volume of water) of 3.4 to 3.6 against about 2.66 for citrine, and the two read differently on a refractometer, the tool that measures how a gem bends light. Get the answer in writing before you insure, resize or sell the piece.

Modern Association

What November Birthstones Mean Today

In GIA's birthstone exhibit, topaz "is believed to bring prosperity when worn," and "wearers of citrine may experience heightened awareness."

Modern crystal guides go further. In our rankings, citrine is a Classic stone for money, confidence and protection, mostly because a golden stone reads as gold, and most guides place it on the solar plexus chakra. Topaz is Often Linked to love, focus and confidence. Some sites list health benefits for both stones. Nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports them.

What Is the November Birthstone Color?

Yellow to orange is the classic answer, the "amber hue" of Kunz's verse and the color of every citrine. In practice, most topaz in stores is blue.

November splits between Scorpio, until about November 21, and Sagittarius. Kunz's zodiac verses give Scorpio the beryl and Sagittarius the topaz (page 347), and crystal guides still tie topaz to Sagittarius more than to any other sign.

How to Choose a November Birthstone

Start with how the piece will be worn, then the budget, then the color.

Which Piece Fits Daily Wear

  • A ring worn every day. Citrine is the easier pick, durable enough for any jewelry, though years of dust and sand, mostly quartz too, leave fine scuffs. Topaz is harder but breaks easily along its cleavage, so it has little durability advantage. For a topaz ring, choose a bezel or other protective setting, since some prongs stress the cleavage plane.
  • Earrings or a pendant. Both stones are excellent, since they take far fewer knocks.
  • A first piece for a November baby. Choose a small pendant to keep for later, not to wear now. Citrine is affordable and durable.
  • A gift on a budget. Citrine and treated blue topaz are among the least expensive gems.
  • An inherited piece. Old yellow "topaz" may be citrine and old blue topaz is almost certainly treated, so have it identified before paying to resize or insure it.

What to Ask Before You Buy

  1. What is the mineral name?

    Topaz and citrine are different minerals, and "smoky topaz" is smoky quartz.

  2. Was the color treated, and how?

    Heat and irradiation are stable. A coating can scratch or wear.

  3. Is it natural, lab-grown or simulated?

    The FTC's Jewelry Guides call it deceptive to use "topaz," or even "birthstone," for a lab-made or imitation stone unless a word like "laboratory-created" or "simulated" comes right before it. Lab-made yellow corundum stands in for citrine in some inexpensive birthstone jewelry.

  4. Will you write it on the receipt?

    The same guides call it deceptive to hide a treatment that is not permanent, needs special care or significantly affects value, but they leave open whether a treated gem may be called "natural." A tag that says natural can still mean heated.

What November Birthstones Cost

Treatment drives most of the price. Natural pink, red, orange and blue topaz are rare and valuable, while common colorless topaz becomes the cheap treated blue. Citrine's value rests mostly on color. Unheated citrine can carry a premium, so ask how the seller knows.

Single listings in November 2026 give a feel. Loose yellow citrine from Brazil, treatment not stated, was about $3 for 0.65 carat and $48 for 7.55 carats at one large gem dealer. Irradiated London blue topaz was about $6 for 0.74 carat there, and one 6.10-carat stone listed at $150 on a gem marketplace. Polished citrine points sold as unheated ran about $25 to $105 for 1 to 1.8 inches.

Try This For a November birthday card, copy Kunz's 1913 lines, "Firm friendship is November's, and she bears / True love beneath the topaz that she wears." Add one honest line on what the stone is, and the moon phase of their birth night from our Birthday Moon.

Safety. Small stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

Is My November Birthstone Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone. Treated is not fake, and for November it is the norm.

Real or Fake Topaz

Natural, Untreated

Usually colorless, pale yellow or brown.

Natural, Treated

Nearly all blue topaz. Some yellow to brown topaz is heated to pink or red, a common, stable and undetectable treatment. Mystic topaz and some vivid pink topaz are coated.

Lab-Grown

Made in labs but not usually sold.

Imitation

Citrine is sometimes passed off as the pricier topaz. Topaz plays the impostor too, when dark treated blue topaz is sold as aquamarine.

Trade Name

"Smoky topaz," "Brazilian topaz" and "Madeira topaz" are names misused for smoky quartz or citrine.

Real or Fake Citrine

Natural, Untreated

Rare, usually pale yellow, often smoky.

Natural, Treated

Heated amethyst or smoky quartz.

Lab-Grown

Grown mainly in Russia, and hard to spot without a gem lab.

Imitation

Lab-made yellow corundum, and Nanosital, a glass-ceramic.

Trade Name

"Madeira" names a reddish orange color, not a place. "Lemon quartz" is irradiated and heated colorless quartz.

Three Checks You Can Do at Home

  1. Check the edges of a mystic or vivid pink topaz

    With a 10x loupe, an inexpensive jeweler's magnifier, look at the facet edges and tips, where a coating wears thin first. A gemologist confirms it by immersion.

  2. Check where a citrine's color sits

    On a cluster, deep orange tips over a pale base fit heated amethyst. This says nothing about a cut or tumbled stone.

  3. Read the color

    Neon greenish yellow points to lemon quartz. It is a hint, never proof.

Topaz or citrine, natural or lab-grown, these need a gemologist. See citrine vs topaz for the two side by side.

Treated blue topaz is rated OK in sunlight, since its color does not fade in light. GIA says topaz is affected only very slightly by chemicals. Our data has no rating yet, so take it off before cleaning the house.

How to Clean a Topaz or Citrine Ring at Home

  1. Fill a small bowl with warm water and one drop of mild dish soap.
  2. Dip the ring briefly and brush around and under the stone with a soft toothbrush.
  3. Rinse under running water just as warm, with the drain closed. A sudden switch to cold can crack either stone.
  4. Pat dry with a soft, lint-free cloth.
  5. Never steam or boil either stone, and keep topaz out of the ultrasonic. Mystic topaz gets mild soap only, because abrasive cleaners and buffing strip its coating.

Do it whenever lotion and skin oil dull the sparkle.

How to Store November Birthstones

  1. Give each piece its own soft pouch. A harder stone scratches a softer one, so topaz can scratch citrine, and diamond or sapphire can scratch topaz.
  2. Keep both away from sunny windows, hot cars and radiators.
  3. Take rings off for steam rooms, hot dishwater followed by a cold rinse, the gym and heavy chores.

Where the beliefs and the care data disagree. Some crystal guides cleanse topaz and citrine in running water or charge them in the sun. A rinse is fine, but soaking is Brief Only for both, and long sun can fade some yellow to brown topaz and some citrine. Moonlight or sound keeps both dry, if you bring them in before the morning sun. Salt has no rating yet for either stone, so leave salt bowls out.
