January Birthstones

January Birthstone Meaning, History & Honest Buying Tips

The January birthstone is garnet, the only stone the American Gem Society lists for the month. Garnet is not one mineral but a family of closely related minerals, with gems in almost every color, though red is the classic. Most garnets are not treated, which makes it one of the simpler birthstones to buy honestly.

By Iris Holloway

January Birthstone at a Glance

Treatments Most are untreated. Rare fracture filling, and a coated type called Proteus
Lab-made Synthetic garnets YAG and GGG, made as diamond simulants

What Is January's Birthstone?

Garnet, on almost every list we checked, old or new. That makes January one of the steadiest months in birthstone history. Today's US lists give garnet alone.

Does January Have Two Birthstones?

No. On today's US lists January has one stone, while months like December have three. GIA's birthstone exhibit explains that months gain extra stones mostly because "some gemstones are too rare and expensive, and others just aren't as popular." Garnet has neither problem. Red garnet is one of the most common and widespread gems, and it comes in almost every color, so one birthstone already offers plenty of choice.

The hyacinth and jacinth on some old lists may not be a rival at all. Those names were used for reddish brown zircon and, historically, also for hessonite, which is a garnet.

Tradition

Where the January Birthstone Comes From

Birthstone lists grew out of the twelve stones on the breastplate of the Jewish high priest, which the historian Josephus tied to the twelve months in the 1st century (Kunz 1913, pages 307 to 310). Kunz thought the fourth stone, the one engraved with the name of Judah, was probably an almandine garnet (pages 292 to 293). That is his reading, not a settled fact. When the stones were later matched to the months, garnet replaced the jacinth for January, which Kunz noted as one of only two departures from the foundation stones of Revelation (page 316).

In August 1912, the National Association of Jewelers met in Kansas City and adopted a new list. January kept garnet, with no alternate (Kunz 1913, pages 317 to 320). The rest of the story, month by month, is on our birthstone chart.

Mineral Fact

What Garnet Actually Is

The Stone. Garnet is the name for a large group of rock-forming minerals. The six main gem species are almandine, andradite, grossular, pyrope, spessartine and uvarovite, and they are never found pure. Every garnet is a mix. Most garnet forms where shale is squeezed and heated deep under colliding plates, while calcium garnets grow where limestone turns to marble.

The colors come from small amounts of other elements. Pure pyrope would be colorless, and its red comes from iron and chromium. Garnet also has a working life. It has been used since the Bronze Age as a gem and as an abrasive.

What Color Is Garnet?

Garnet is typically red, but it can be orange, green, yellow, purple, black or brown, and blue garnets are extremely rare. Some garnets change color from blue to purple in different lighting. Here is the family by color.

Garnet Color Worth knowing

Tradition

Where Garnet's Meaning Comes From

The Story. Garnet's meaning has always followed its color, the red of blood, fire and fruit.

  1. The name

    "Garnet" comes from the medieval Latin granatus, meaning pomegranate.

  2. About 3500 BC, Egypt

    An Old Empire necklace found in 1905 held fragments of almandine garnet with carnelian, shell and turquoise. Kunz thought its turquoise ibex had the quality of a fetish, a protective charm (Kunz 1913, pages 37 to 38). Necklaces studded with red garnets adorned the pharaohs.

  3. Ancient Rome

    Signet rings carried garnet intaglios used to stamp the wax on important documents.

  4. The carbuncle

    The old name for brilliant red stones, from the word for a glowing coal, covered both rubies and certain fine garnets (Kunz 1913, pages 292 to 293).

  5. Middle Ages Tradition

    A lion engraved on a garnet was said to protect its owner's honors and guard him "from all perils in travelling" (Kunz 1913, page 133). The clergy and nobility of the period preferred red garnets.

  6. 1891, Germany

    At the Dusseldorf Exhibition, Kunz saw the "Ring of St. Elizabeth," said to hold a luminous ruby. The stone turned out to be "a large almost flat carbuncle garnet of no great brilliancy" (page 165).

  7. 1892, Kashmir frontier

    Red stones were long believed to protect against wounds, yet the Hanzas, as Kunz calls them, used garnets as bullets against British troops, on the opposite idea that a blood-red stone would wound more deeply (pages 33 to 34).

  8. Victorian era

    Bohemia was the main source of the red pyrope garnets of the period.

  9. 1913 Tradition

    Kunz printed a rhyme for those born in January, promising that garnets "will insure her constancy, true friendship and fidelity" (page 327).

Modern Association

What Garnet Means Today

GIA's exhibit puts it simply. "Garnet is believed to bring a sense of calm, when worn." Modern crystal guides lean warmer. Love and protection are the themes most guides link to garnet, with confidence next, and the root and heart chakras are the links they name most often.

The line from old to new is easy to follow. Protection echoes the amulets against wounds and the lion for travelers, and love fits a stone the color of a heart. Kunz's "constancy" is still a good word for it.

The zodiac is less settled. Guides split between Scorpio, Leo and other signs, although by date January belongs to Capricorn and Aquarius, and Kunz's old zodiac rhyme gives garnet to Aquarius (Kunz 1913, page 345). Some sites list health benefits for garnet. Nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports them.

How to Choose a January Birthstone

  • Pick the color first. Deep red (almandine, pyrope), purplish red (rhodolite), orange (spessartine) or green (tsavorite, demantoid).
  • Match it to the budget. Dark red garnets are affordable, while greens are rare and valuable. Tsavorite and demantoid command premium prices.
  • Match it to the wear. Garnets rate 6.5 to 7.5 on the Mohs scale, which makes them more likely to be damaged than rubies, sapphires or diamonds. They are ideal for earrings, brooches and pendants, since not all garnets suit daily wear. Rhodolite is rated durable for any setting.

Try This Kunz's rhyme promises constancy, so borrow the word for January. Pick one small promise for the month, like a walk after lunch. Keep a tumbled garnet on your desk and touch it each time you keep the promise. The stone keeps count, you do the keeping.

Try This Garnet is also the gem for a second anniversary. Tuck a card in with the gift that says the name means pomegranate, a fruit full of seeds, and leave the metaphor to them.

Safety. Small tumbled stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

Is My Garnet Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone. Garnet scores well on honesty, because most garnets are not treated.

Natural, Untreated

Most garnets you will see.

Natural, Treated

Rarely, surface-reaching cracks are filled with a glass-like substance. Proteus garnets, almandine-pyrope with a thin metallic surface layer, are the only regularly treated garnets, and they look dark gray and metallic in reflected light.

Lab-Made

Synthetic garnets called YAG and GGG were the main diamond simulants before cubic zirconia arrived in the late 1970s, and they are still made in many colors. They are lab materials with a garnet structure, not the same compounds as the natural gem species.

Imitation

Ask the seller to name the mineral, and get it in writing.

Trade Name

Rhodolite, malaia, tsavorite, demantoid and cinnamon stone are honest names for real garnets. Chrome pyrope from Arizona is called "anthill garnet," because ants bring the stones to the surface.

Home checks are limited. Garnet has no cleavage, so a stone that splits cleanly along flat planes is probably something else. Color alone cannot identify a garnet, because the species overlap so much. Naming the exact species or blend takes a gem lab.

Garnet vs Ruby

Ruby is red corundum and rates 9 on the Mohs scale, harder than any garnet. Chrome pyrope can rival a fine ruby's red, but it is typically very dark in tone, which separates it from ruby. Even Kunz's St. Elizabeth "ruby" was a garnet. More on telling them apart in garnet vs ruby.

Three questions for the seller. Which garnet is it? Has it been filled or coated? Where was it mined?

How to Care for Garnet

Here are the basics for garnets in general. Warm soapy water and a soft brush are always safe. Ultrasonic cleaners are usually safe, except for stones with fractures or fillings, and steam cleaning is not recommended. Garnets should not take hard blows or rough wear.

Where the beliefs and the care data disagree. Several crystal guides suggest a short spell of sun to recharge garnet.
