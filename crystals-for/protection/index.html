Crystals for Protection

Crystals for Protection & What People Use Them For

Crystals for protection are stones people wear, carry or set by the front door as a sign of safety. The ones modern crystal guides name most often are amber, black agate, carnelian, obsidian and pyrite, and many shops now put black tourmaline first. Protection is a belief, not a shield, and it grew out of a very old habit of wearing amulets.

By Iris Holloway

Tradition

Where Protection Beliefs Come From

The Story. Long before crystal shops, protection took the form of amulets, small objects worn to keep harm away.

  1. Stone Age, northern Europe Tradition

    George Frederick Kunz describes rough pieces of amber marked with small hollows, found in Stone Age deposits in Prussia, Schleswig-Holstein and Denmark. He reports the view of the archaeologist Hoernes that they were probably talismans or amulets (The Curious Lore of Precious Stones, 1913, page 55).

  2. Ancient Egypt Tradition

    The Book of the Dead has a chapter for a carnelian buckle placed on the neck of the dead, so that "the virtue of Isis protects him" (quoted by Kunz, 1913, page 226).

  3. Engraved Gems Tradition

    Kunz lists protective meanings for pictures cut into stones. A man with a sword cut into a carnelian was said to guard a place from lightning and storms, and a bear cut into an amethyst was said to put demons to flight (Kunz, 1913, page 134).

  4. 1800s, Germany and Africa Tradition

    Agate cutters at Idar and Oberstein in Germany were kept busy making Evil Eye amulets for buyers in the Sudan, from brown or black agate with a white ring that stood for an eye (Kunz, 1913, page 54).

  5. Today Modern Association

    Crystal guides describe protection as a shield against "negative energy." They do not agree on how the shield works. One guide says obsidian absorbs negative energy rather than deflecting it, while another says chiastolite deflects it rather than absorbing it. Neither offers a way to check.

Why So Many Protection Stones Are Black

Black stones fill today's protection lists, from obsidian and black agate to black tourmaline, onyx and hematite. Guides usually explain it with color symbolism, dark stones said to soak up what you do not want around you. The older record is less tidy. Kunz notes that red, white and green amulets were in demand on the west coast of Africa, while only white stones were favored in North Africa (1913, page 54). Protection has never had just one color.

Try This

A Doorway Ritual for a New Home

The Ritual. Modern guides often suggest a protection stone by the entrance, obsidian in one guide, black agate in another.

Try This On your first evening in a new place, or any evening you want a fresh start, take one sturdy stone, a small dish, a card and a pen.

  1. Pick the stone

    Black agate, carnelian or an obsidian palm stone. Obsidian chips, so give it the dish, not a hard shelf edge.

  2. Choose the spot

    A shelf by the door, at adult height, out of reach of small children and pets.

  3. Write one line

    On the card, write what this home is for. "Rest after work" is plenty. Tuck it under the dish.

  4. Set it down as you walk in

    Drop your keys in the dish beside the stone every day. The stone marks the line between out there and in here.

  5. Once a month, wipe the dish

    Reread the card and rewrite it if life has changed.

Why it helps. Whatever you believe about the stone, here is what the ritual does for sure. It turns walking through your own door into a moment you notice, and your keys stop disappearing. What Research Says A 2018 review by Nicholas Hobson and colleagues, a narrative look back over earlier ritual research with no participants of its own, proposes that rituals help people steady their emotions, their focus on a goal and their sense of connection to others, through both the physical steps and the meaning people give them (Hobson and colleagues, 2018). It is a framework, not a test, and none of the work it reviews used stones or doorways, so read it as background on why small routines feel steadying, not as proof.

Safety. Small tumbled stones are not toys. Keep the dish where young children and pets cannot reach it.

When a Stone Is Not Enough

A stone by the door is a symbol. It is not a lock, a smoke alarm or a person who can help. If you feel unsafe in your home or with someone in it, tell someone you trust, and in an emergency call 911.

The Protection Stones, One by One

  1. Classic
  2. 1

    Amber & Baltic Amber

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Amber is fossilized tree resin, not a mineral, and Baltic amber is the most common source worldwide. Kunz also records the centuries-old belief that amber necklaces protect children from colds, and suggests a sober reason, a bare throat that never overheats and then chills (The Magic of Jewels and Charms, 1915, preface, page viii). Today the gentlest way to carry it is a small piece in a coat pocket as a lucky charm, as one German crystal shop suggests.

    Care. Very soft (Mohs 2 to 2.5) and easily scratched, so pendants and earrings fare better than rings. Use a damp cloth and mild soap only, since solvents, alcohol and heat can damage it for good. The International Gem Society's toxicity table rates amber a high risk without saying why, so leave it out of anything you drink.

  3. 2

    Black Agate

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Black chalcedony is rare in nature, so most black agate and "black onyx" is chalcedony dyed black. Its protective reputation echoes the Evil Eye amulets above, and modern guides suggest a black agate pendant or a stone near the entryway. Dyed is not fake. On our authenticity scale it is natural, treated.

    Care. The classic sugar and acid process is permanent, but stones colored with organic dyes can fade in direct sunlight. Black onyx is heat sensitive, and polished pieces scratch fairly easily, so store it apart from harder stones. A soak, rinse and soft brush are fine for agate in general.

  4. 3

    Carnelian

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Carnelian is orange to red chalcedony colored by iron. Beyond the Egyptian buckle of Isis, Kunz traces its popularity as a talisman in the Islamic world to the silver ring set with a carnelian seal that the Prophet wore on the little finger of his right hand (1913, pages 63 to 64). Modern guides lean toward courage more than protection, so it suits a ring or bracelet for a day that needs nerve.

    Care. Assume any carnelian has been heated to deepen its red. A dealer guide says dyed chalcedony should skip harsh chemicals, acetone and ultrasonic cleaners, and carnelian is often treated. Our care data has no rating yet for water, sun or salt.

  5. 4

    Obsidian

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Obsidian is volcanic glass, lava that cooled too fast for crystals to form. The ancient Mexicans made images of the god Tezcatlipoca, whose name Kunz renders as "shining mirror," from obsidian, and used obsidian mirrors for divination (1913, pages 204 to 205). Modern guides turned the mirror into a shield, and the usual tip is a piece near the front door.

    Black is its common natural color. Blue, green or red "obsidian" can be man-made glass passed off as a rare natural color.

    Safety The broken edges of obsidian are sharper than any steel knife, so a chipped piece is not a toy.

    Care. It scratches and chips easily, which makes it unsuitable for rings and bracelets. Wipe it with a lint-free cloth or wash it briefly in warm soapy water, and keep it away from heat, which can make inclusions expand.

  6. 5

    Pyrite

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Pyrite is iron sulfide, the brassy mineral nicknamed fool's gold. Kunz records pyrite crystals used as amulets by Native American peoples and kept among the objects medicine men used in their rites (1913, page 99), and a belief in Surrey, England, that pyrite nodules fell from the sky in thunderstorms and had peculiar powers where lightning is concerned (1915, page 112). Today guides set it by the front door as a protection and money stone.

    Wondering why yours went dull? Pyrite often tarnishes to a dull brass color.

    Care. It is very brittle and heat sensitive. Our care data has no rating yet for water, salt or cleaning, so look it up in the Care Checker before you wash it.

  7. 6

    Sugilite

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Sugilite is a rare purple mineral, named in 1976 to honor Ken-ichi Sugi, and it did not reach the gem market until 1979, after a find at the Wessels Mine in South Africa. So its protective meaning is entirely modern, and one crystal guide is typical in calling it a shield of light. The same guide suggests a spot on the nightstand.

    Much cheap "sugilite" is powdered sugilite pressed into a mold with resin, often sold without saying so.

    Care. Warm water and mild soap are fine. It is fragile in rings and bracelets, so protective settings are advised.

  8. 7

    Amethyst

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Amethyst is purple quartz, colored by traces of iron and natural radiation. In the 1400s, Kunz writes, it was believed to control evil thoughts and to have "preserved soldiers from harm" (1913, page 58), and an Egyptian necklace set with amethysts was worn as a set of protective amulets (The Magic of Jewels and Charms, 1915, page 317). Today it is a nightstand favorite.

    Care. Some amethyst can fade with long exposure to intense light, and sudden temperature changes can fracture it. Wash it by hand with warm water, mild soap and a soft brush, and skip the ultrasonic cleaner, since inclusions can shatter.

  9. 8

    Chiastolite

    ClassicHow We Rate

    Classic. Chiastolite is a variety of andalusite with a dark cross inside, and Kunz explains that its name means a mark like the Greek letter chi and that "the divine symbol it bore served to drive away evil spirits" (1913, page 270). Pilgrims on the Camino Primitivo to Santiago de Compostela collected it as protective amulets for the road, according to a 2015 study by Ramón Jiménez Martínez of Spain's Museo Geominero and Matina Prieto Fernández.

    Try This Give one to a friend setting out on a long trip, with a note about the pilgrims.

    Care. Our care data does not cover chiastolite yet.

Safety. Never put amber teething necklaces or bracelets on a baby. The US Food and Drug Administration has received reports of deaths and serious injuries to infants and children, including strangulation and choking, from teething jewelry such as amber teething necklaces.

Is Black Tourmaline the Best Crystal for Protection?

Black tourmaline is the stone many crystal shops now sell first for protection, but "best" is a belief, and no test can rank protection stones. One German shop ranks it as the most important protection stone in modern crystal healing, and one dealer repeats the idea of a piece in each corner of a room. Black tourmaline rates Classic for protection in our ranking.

The Stone. Most black tourmaline is schorl, one species of the tourmaline group, though other species can be black too. It is hard (Mohs 7 to 7.5) and grows as three-sided prisms with fine lengthwise grooves.

The Story. Tradition Victorian jewelers often used it in mourning jewelry. Modern Association Shops often suggest a piece by the Wi-Fi router to absorb electromagnetic radiation, yet the same German shop admits "there is no scientific evidence that stones influence electromagnetic fields."

Care. Tourmaline suits everyday jewelry. Our care data has no water, sun or salt rating for black tourmaline yet.

Smoky Quartz, Too. Smoky quartz also rates Classic for protection. John Dee's crystal ball in the British Museum is smoky quartz, perhaps chosen because of Scottish superstitions about the stone, Kunz suggests (1913, page 190). Heat lightens its color, and strong sunlight is often said to fade it, which is why our care data rates sun as one to avoid. Keep it off a sunny windowsill.

Grounding & Protection Crystals

Obsidian, smoky quartz and hematite rate Classic for both protection and grounding in our ranking, which is why so many guides treat the two ideas as a pair. For the steadying side, see crystals for grounding and its five-sense routine.
