Crystal & Ritual

Sources & Methods

Crystal & Ritual builds every page from three kinds of material. Reference sources tell us what a stone is, old public domain books tell us where its meaning comes from, and today's crystal guides tell us what people believe now. This page lists the sources we rely on, explains how we rate a belief and shows where our images come from.

Reference Sources We Use

  • GIA. The Gemological Institute of America, its gem pages and its journal Gems & Gemology, for chemistry, hardness, treatments, lab-grown stones and care.
  • International Gem Society. The IGS gem guides and care pages.
  • Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History. Specimen records from its Department of Mineral Sciences, many with photos released as CC0 through Smithsonian Open Access.
  • USGS. The US Geological Survey's public reference pages, such as its booklet on gemstones.
  • Handbook of Mineralogy. The mineral data sheets for formulas, crystal systems, hardness and localities.
  • The IMA List of Minerals. The official list of mineral species, kept by the International Mineralogical Association's Commission on New Minerals, Nomenclature and Classification, where the current edition is posted. Our data uses the July 2026 edition, which counts 6,226 valid mineral species. We use it to tell a mineral species from a variety, a rock or a trade name. Calcite is on the list, amethyst is not, because amethyst is a variety of quartz, and moldavite is not, because it is a natural glass.
  • George Frederick Kunz. The Curious Lore of Precious Stones (1913, Project Gutenberg 57978) and The Magic of Jewels and Charms (1915, Project Gutenberg 66583), both in the public domain and always cited by page.

We also use Geology.com, Minerals.net, university geology departments, museum pages and the gem trade press, such as JCK and Gem-A. Scientific papers are linked by DOI whenever one exists.

How We Sort a Source

  • Reference sources, from museums, gem labs and institutes, universities, mineral handbooks and peer-reviewed journals, back the facts we state plainly. We check each fact against them and list them at the end of each page and on this page. Quotes, old lore and studies keep their author in the text.
  • Dealer guides sell what they describe. A fact found only there is written as "according to dealers", or confirmed in a reference source first.
  • Open online encyclopedias are a pointer, never the source. We follow them to the sources they cite and check those.
  • Mindat may be linked as a reference for a mineral species. We never copy its text or its photos.
  • Forums such as Reddit tell us what people really ask. They never tell us what is true.

How We Rate

When a page says a stone is Classic for sleep or Sometimes Linked to the new moon, that word comes from our five-facet scale.

We compare what crystal guides say about each stone today, in English, French, German, Spanish, Portuguese and Japanese among other languages, and read it against the older record, from Kunz's books of 1913 and 1915 to the modern crystal books most of today's meanings come from. For each intention, such as sleep, love or protection, we look at how consistently guides link the stone to it. The more consistently a stone is linked to an intention, the more facets it gets. A stone that only a couple of guides cover cannot reach the top of the scale, however much they agree.

Facets Label In plain words
5 Classic The link turns up in most guides that cover the stone, by a wide margin
4 Widely Linked Around half of the guides or more make the link
3 Often Linked A solid minority of guides make it
2 Sometimes Linked A smaller minority make it, or too few guides cover the stone to rate it higher
1 Rarely Linked Only the odd guide makes it

The scale shows how widely a belief is held, not whether it works. A Classic stone for sleep is a stone many people believe in for sleep. It is not a sleep aid, and the page says so.

Where the Story Comes From

  • Kunz, 1913 and 1915, for ancient, medieval and folk lore, with the page number every time.
  • Pliny the Elder, Natural History Book 37, in the Bostock and Riley translation of 1855 only.
  • Theophrastus, On Stones, in the John Hill translation of 1746.
  • Modern crystal books, named by title and date as the origin of today's meanings, such as the Edgar Cayce readings on gems, compiled by the A.R.E. Press (1979 edition) and later sold as Gems and Stones, Katrina Raphaell's Crystal Enlightenment (1985), Melody's Love Is in the Earth (1991) and Judy Hall's The Crystal Bible (2003). We summarize them in a line and never quote them at length.

We do not quote modern copyrighted translations of old texts.

Studies

Studies are linked by DOI or PubMed ID, with their size and limits. Most research on rituals and habits did not use stones, and we say so when we lean on it.

Moon Times

Moon phases are computed with the method of Jean Meeus in Astronomical Algorithms (1998), checked against US Naval Observatory times and rounded to the minute. Full moon names follow the Old Farmer's Almanac, and eclipse dates come from NASA. The results are on our full moon calendar.

The Stone Lab

Some questions are best answered by trying, carefully, with real stones, such as what water does to selenite. Each Stone Lab note will show the date, the method and our own photos. Until a test has actually been run, no page on this site says we tested anything.

Image Licenses

Every image has a recorded source page, author, license and retrieval date before it is published.

  • Smithsonian Open Access. Specimen photos released as CC0. We credit them anyway, as "Smithsonian NMNH, CC0".
  • Wikimedia Commons. Only public domain, CC0, CC BY and CC BY-SA files, credited with the author and the license as each license requires.
  • USGS. Images made by the USGS are in the public domain and credited "U.S. Geological Survey", following its credit policy.
  • The Met. Open Access images marked public domain, for old amulets and jewels.
  • Stock photo sites. Unsplash, Pexels and Pixabay for everyday scenes only, a desk or a nightstand, never to show what a stone looks like.
  • Illustrations. Labeled "Illustration" and used for moods and rituals, never in place of a real stone.

We never use Mindat or RRUFF photos, and never photos from shops, Etsy, Amazon or Pinterest.

Review Dates

Every page lists its sources and its last reviewed date. How we write and correct pages is in our editorial policy.
