# February Birthstone Meaning, History & Honest Buying Tips

The February birthstone is amethyst, the purple variety of quartz. It is February's only stone on the modern list of the American Gem Society, and it holds the same month in all eight older lists George Frederick Kunz compared in 1913. Its meanings (calm, a clear head, staying sober) are beliefs, but the stone itself is durable and affordable.

## What Is the February Birthstone?

February's birthstone is amethyst, on both the modern and the traditional lists, according to the [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/history-of-birthstones/). The modern list of the [American Gem Society](https://www.americangemsociety.org/birthstones/) gives February amethyst and nothing else.

That makes February one of the steadiest months in birthstone history. Kunz set eight lists side by side, from Jewish, Roman, Arabian, Polish, Russian and Italian sources and from Isidore of Seville to a list of the 15th to 20th centuries, and amethyst is February's stone in every one. Only the last list adds hyacinth and pearl as extra choices (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, pages 315 to 316). When the National Association of Jewelers standardized the list in Kansas City in August 1912, February again got amethyst alone (pages 317 to 319).

One outlier is worth knowing. A Hindu list of gems of the months that Kunz reprints gives February the chandrakanta, a stone he gives only by that name (page 331).

### Why Some Months Have Several Birthstones & February Has One

The 1912 list already gave a second stone to March, June, August, October and December (Kunz 1913, pages 319 to 320). Later updates added more. The Jewelry Industry Council of America added alexandrite to June, citrine to November, pink tourmaline to October and zircon to December in 1952, tanzanite joined December in 2002, and spinel joined August in 2016 ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/history-of-birthstones/)).

None of those changes touched February. Kunz would have approved. In 1913 he argued that there should be "no duplication or triplication of birth-stones for any given month" (pages 320 to 321), a rule February still keeps.

If you want a second February stone anyway, the zodiac offers one route. A February birthday falls under Aquarius or Pisces, and our [Aquarius](/zodiac/aquarius/) and [Pisces](/zodiac/pisces/) pages list the stones each sign is linked to.

## Where the February Stone's Meaning Comes from

**The Story.** The February amethyst is the end point of a long chain of lists. Here is how the stone got the month, and then the meanings.

- **Ancient Greece.** **Tradition.** The name comes from the Greek amethystos, "not drunk," and the Greeks believed the stone prevented intoxication ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/amethyst/gem-overview)).
- **1st century CE.** **Tradition.** The historian Josephus tied the twelve stones of the Hebrew high priest's breastplate to the twelve months, and St. Jerome adapted the idea about three centuries later (Kunz 1913, pages 309 to 310).
- **Early Christianity.** **Tradition.** Amethyst is the twelfth foundation stone of the New Jerusalem in Revelation (Kunz 1913, page 303). The month lists counted the year from March, so the first stone went to March and the twelfth, amethyst, landed on February (page 316).
- **Valentine's lore.** **Tradition.** GIA says St. Valentine wore an amethyst ring carved with Cupid. Kunz, more careful, calls the story somewhat doubtful but "by no means impossible" (1913, page 257). Either way, it gave February's stone a Valentine's Day legend.
- **1700s.** **Tradition.** Wearing a stone for one's birth month seems to have started in Poland in the 18th century, in Kunz's view (1913, page 307). The writer Poujet fils, in 1762, described rings set with the birth-month stone and engraved with the zodiac sign (pages 316 to 317).
- **1913.** **Tradition.** Kunz's calendar of the months gives February the amethyst, the guardian angel Barchiel, the apostle Andrew, the sign of Pisces and the primrose, with a verse. "The February-born may find / Sincerity and peace of mind, / Freedom from passion and from care, / If she an amethyst will wear" (page 327). His zodiac verse for Pisces repeats the promise, "From passion and from care kept free" (page 345).
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** In the crystal guides we compared, amethyst is a Classic stone for sleep, for focus and for protection, and Widely Linked to calm in anxious moments. Most guides place it on the crown chakra. Our [amethyst page](/stones/amethyst/) follows each thread.

Notice how little the core idea has moved. The Greek sober stone, the 1913 promise of "peace of mind" and today's calm stone are close cousins, and that steadiness is part of the appeal.

### American Amethyst

Kunz once drew up a list of American gems to pair with the birthstones, out of patriotism, and February's was amethyst from North Carolina, Georgia and Virginia (1913, page 323). The [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/amethyst-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/) still lists Georgia and North Carolina as sources, and notes that the Smithsonian holds a 202.5-carat amethyst from North Carolina. Today the only commercially run amethyst mine in the United States is the Four Peaks Mine in Arizona, known for reddish purple stones ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/amethyst/)).

## Is My February Birthstone Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone. Here is where amethyst stands.

- **Natural, untreated.** Purple straight from the ground. It is so abundant and cheap that the International Gem Society sees little incentive to buy synthetics.
- **Natural, treated.** Heat and irradiation can change the color. Amethyst heated to yellow or red-orange is, by definition, citrine, and the International Gem Society calls the heat treatment undetectable and very stable.
- **Lab-grown.** Labs grow amethyst hydrothermally, or make it by irradiating specially prepared smoky quartz, and synthetic rough is sometimes sold as natural rough (International Gem Society).
- **Imitation.** Purple chalcedony is often wrongly called "amethyst" or "amethyst chalcedony." Purple or lilac chalcedony are the honest names ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/amethyst/)).
- **Trade names.** "Siberian" is a grade for dark purple with red or blue flashes, not an origin. "Rose de France" is very light, pinkish violet amethyst. "Madeira," "Palmyra" and "Spanish" topaz are heated amethyst, not topaz (International Gem Society).

Three checks you can do at home, with their limits.

1. **Look for color zoning.** Strong zoning is very common in natural amethyst and not usually seen in synthetic stones (Geology.com). It is a hint, not a proof.
2. **Check the luster of a chip.** With a loupe, find a tiny shell-shaped chip at a facet edge or the base of a cabochon. Quartz looks glassy there, chalcedony looks dull (Geology.com). This rules out chalcedony, not lab-grown amethyst.
3. **Look at any break.** Amethyst has no cleavage and breaks with a curved, shell-like fracture (Geology.com). A purple stone that splits along flat planes is probably something else.

For natural versus lab-grown, the International Gem Society points you to a professional gemologist or a gem lab.

### Is Cheap Amethyst Fake?

Not by itself. Amethyst is one of the most affordable gems, and enormous deposits in South America and Africa keep the price low (Geology.com). A low price is normal for this stone, so judge the piece in front of you, not the tag. The opposite holds too. Pale stones that have faded in a sunny window are not proof of a fake, because GIA says some amethyst color can fade with prolonged exposure to intense light.

Wondering whether a very dark stone was dyed? Ask the seller in writing whether the color is natural or treated.

## Honest Buying Tips for a February Birthday

**Mineral Fact.** Amethyst rates 7 on the Mohs scale and does not break by cleavage, so it is durable enough for rings, bracelets, earrings and pendants (Geology.com). GIA calls it "beautiful enough for crown jewels yet affordable enough for class rings."

- **Pay for color, not size.** Value depends almost entirely on color, with the dark "Siberian" grade at the top, though light Rose de France stones are back in fashion ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/amethyst-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)).
- **Do not pay extra for a bigger stone alone.** Price per carat does not rise dramatically with size ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/amethyst/gem-overview)), and it climbs gradually, not exponentially (International Gem Society).
- **Skip visible flaws.** Most amethyst has no inclusions you can see without magnification (GIA), and the stone is plentiful, so there is little reason to pay top dollar for visible inclusions or poor cutting (International Gem Society).
- **Know where it comes from.** Brazil gives large stones of generally moderate color, and many gem lovers prefer the smaller, richer stones from Zambia and Uruguay (International Gem Society).
- **Read the label twice.** "Green amethyst" is heated quartz called prasiolite, and "amethyst chalcedony" is chalcedony. The [citrine guide](/real-or-fake/citrine/) covers the yellow side of heated amethyst.

## How to Use Amethyst for a February Birthday

**The Ritual.** The stone will not do the work, but a small object tied to a date is an easy anchor for a habit.

### A Birthday Card with the 1913 Verse

**Try This.** Wrap a tumbled amethyst and copy Kunz's February lines inside the card, "The February-born may find / Sincerity and peace of mind." Add one sentence on what the stone is (purple quartz, colored by iron and natural radiation in the rock). The card does half the work.

### A Valentine's Week Desk Stone

**Try This.** Set a small amethyst point by your keyboard on February 14. Each time you notice it that week, send one short note to someone you love, friend or partner. The Cupid ring story is doubtful, and the notes are the point.

### A Birthday Morning Letter

**Try This.** On the morning of your February birthday, hold the stone and write three lines to yourself about the year ahead, one thing to keep, one to drop and one to try. Fold the letter around the stone and put both in a drawer. Next February, read it before you write the new one. The 1913 verse promised "peace of mind," and a yearly check-in is a realistic version.

**Safety.** Small tumbled stones and bead bracelets are a choking hazard, so keep them away from small children and pets.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Amethyst is often chosen for sleep, stress and cutting back on drinking. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If poor sleep, anxiety or worries about drinking last or feel heavy, talk to a doctor, a therapist or a counselor, and never use a stone in place of treatment.

## How to Care for a February Birthstone

**Mineral Fact.** Amethyst is quartz with good toughness, and GIA calls it suitable for all jewelry. These ratings come from our care data, the same data as the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/).

- **Water.** OK. Warm water, mild detergent and a soft brush (International Gem Society). Keep soaks brief, since the guidance covers washing, not long soaks.
- **Sun.** Brief only. Some amethyst color can fade with prolonged exposure to intense light ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/amethyst-care-cleaning)), so a sunny windowsill is the wrong home for it.
- **Heat.** Avoid. The International Gem Society says amethyst remains sensitive to extreme heat, and its [care guide](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gemstone-care-guide/) warns it can crack as well as fade.
- **Chemicals.** Avoid. GIA lists hydrofluoric acid, ammonium fluoride and alkaline solutions.
- **Salt.** Unknown. Our care data has no salt guidance yet, so skip salt baths for now.
- **Cleaning machines.** Skip them. Inclusions can shatter in an ultrasonic cleaner (International Gem Society).
- **Storage.** Keep it apart from harder gems, and take amethyst jewelry off for strenuous activity (International Gem Society).
- **Toxicity.** OK to wear and handle. The silica risk the International Gem Society lists concerns dust from cutting and polishing, not intact stones.
