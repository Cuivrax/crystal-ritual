# How to Tell If a Crystal Is Real

To tell if a crystal is real, first decide what real means, then read the label, look at the stone under a loupe and ask the seller three questions. Many crystals people call fake are real stones that were heated, dyed, stabilized or coated, while outright fakes are usually glass or plastic. Home checks can rule a stone out, but only a gem lab can confirm what it is.

## What Real Means, the Five Labels We Use

"Real" hides five different answers. We use the same five labels on every page of this site, and the [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) uses them too, with two extra tags for label words and for names that cover several things.

| Label | What it means | Common examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural Untreated** | Mined, then only cut or polished | Iris agate, rainbow obsidian, Blue John fluorite |
| **Natural Treated** | A real stone whose look was changed by heat, radiation, dye, a filler or a coating | Citrine made from heated amethyst, dyed howlite, stabilized turquoise, mystic topaz |
| **Lab-Grown** | The same material, made in a laboratory | Lab-created sapphire, lab-grown opal |
| **Imitation** | Something else that looks the part | Goldstone, most opalite, reconstituted turquoise |
| **Trade Name** | A selling name that may hide what the stone is | Rainbow moonstone, Herkimer diamond |

Two of these trip people up. A treated stone is not fake. Heated citrine is still quartz, and the heating is a stable treatment the trade fully accepts. A lab-grown stone is not an imitation either. GIA describes a synthetic gem as one made in a laboratory that "shares virtually all chemical, optical, and physical characteristics of its natural mineral counterpart." An imitation, also called a simulant, has a very different chemistry and structure from the gem it copies, and it can be natural or man-made.

## Fake Crystals You Are Most Likely to Meet

These are the labels that most often hide something else. Each row gives the first clue to look for and the reason that clue cannot settle it on its own.

| On the label | What it usually is | Our label | First clue, then its limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Citrine](/real-or-fake/citrine/) | Heated amethyst or heated smoky quartz, in most cases | Natural Treated | Deep orange with a reddish tint and color packed in the tips. Some natural citrine is reddish too |
| Turquoise | Stabilized turquoise, filled with wax, oil, resin or epoxy | Natural Treated | A very even color and a glassy shine. Magnification alone can miss it |
| Blue howlite, howlite turquoise | Howlite dyed blue, the most common turquoise fake | Natural Treated | Dark web veins and a flat, even blue. Real turquoise has veins too |
| Reconstituted or block turquoise | Ground turquoise pressed with plastic or resin | Imitation | A very even color with no natural pattern. It can look like the real thing |
| Black onyx | Usually chalcedony dyed black | Natural Treated | A deep, even black with no bands. The dye is stable and hard to see |
| Brightly colored agate | Dyed agate | Natural Treated | Bright, even color that pools in cracks and edges. Stable dye can be invisible |
| Goldstone | Man-made glass with tiny copper crystals | Imitation | Coarse, flat glitter spread very evenly. Natural aventurine glitters too |
| Opalite | Man-made glass or plastic | Imitation, in most cases | Bubbles or swirl marks and a very even milky look. A few sellers use the word for natural common opal |
| Moldavite | Natural impact glass, though much moldavite sold online is man-made glass | Natural Untreated, or Imitation | Real moldavite holds bubbles and swirly flow lines. A faceted stone over two carats, or a piece with no bubbles, is likely fake |
| Magnetic hematite | Hematine, which contains no hematite at all | Imitation | A strong pull to a magnet. Some real hematite holds enough magnetite to be pulled too |
| Mystic topaz | Colorless topaz with a thin coating | Natural Treated | Rainbow colors that shift with the angle, and plain topaz showing at worn edges. A fresh coating shows no wear |
| Blue topaz, Swiss blue, London blue | Topaz that got its blue from treatment, almost always | Natural Treated | An even, clean blue. Natural and treated blue topaz look the same |
| Lab-created sapphire | Real corundum grown in a lab | Lab-Grown | Curved lines inside under magnification. Modern lab stones can look very natural |
| [Rainbow moonstone](/stones/rainbow-moonstone/) | White labradorite | Trade Name | Flashes in several colors. A blue flash alone proves nothing |
| Herkimer diamond | Quartz | Trade Name | A clear crystal with a point at both ends. Similar quartz comes from other places |
| Indian jade | Green aventurine, a quartz | Imitation | Fine glitter inside a green stone. Real jade has none |

Several of these names have their own guide, including [cherry quartz](/real-or-fake/cherry-quartz/), [aura quartz](/real-or-fake/aura-quartz/), [goldstone](/real-or-fake/goldstone/), [opalite](/real-or-fake/opalite/) and [moldavite](/real-or-fake/moldavite/).

## What the Words on a Label Promise

A US seller's words carry some weight. Under the Federal Trade Commission's [Jewelry Guides](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-23), it is unfair or deceptive to use "real," "genuine," "natural" or "precious" for a product that is "manufactured or produced artificially" (16 CFR 23.27). A lab-grown stone or an imitation should carry a word such as "laboratory-grown," "laboratory-created," "synthetic," "imitation" or "simulated" right before the stone's name, just as visible as the name, and the Guides add that "faux" is not enough (23.25). A treatment should be disclosed when it is not permanent, when it creates special care needs, or when it has a significant effect on value (23.24).

Notice what "natural" does not promise. It rules out a stone made in a factory. It does not rule out heat or dye. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Safe Checks at Home, and What They Cannot Prove

Every check below is safe for you and for the stone. Each one can raise a doubt. None of them can prove a stone is natural.

| Check | What it can show | What it cannot show |
|---|---|---|
| Read the whole label and the receipt | Words like heated, dyed, stabilized, coated, lab-created or simulated | Anything the seller left out |
| Look with a loupe in daylight | Bubbles and swirl marks point to glass. Color pooled in cracks points to dye. Curved lines point to lab-grown sapphire | Natural glass has bubbles too. Stable dye and modern lab stones can look clean |
| Hold a fridge magnet to it | A strong pull on "hematite" points to hematine | Some real hematite is magnetic too |
| Compare its weight with a known stone of the same size | A gross mismatch, such as a "topaz" that feels as light as citrine | Small differences, which need a gemologist's scale |
| Look at the price and the pile | A large, clean, bright stone at a very low price | Price alone proves nothing either way |

A word on bubbles. They say glass. They do not always say fake, because moldavite and obsidian are natural glasses, and genuine moldavite usually holds far more bubbles than factory glass.

For a stone you cannot name at all, start with [the crystal identification guide](/identify/crystal-identification-guide/), which walks through color, luster, hardness and the other safe tests in order.

## Tests to Skip

Some popular "real or fake" tests damage the stone or put you at risk, and most of them prove little.

- **A flame or a lighter.** Heat can make citrine fracture after an abrupt temperature change, and opal is sensitive to temperature changes. A hot needle that smells of burnt plastic does expose plastic, but it is a destructive test.
- **Acid or vinegar.** Geologists use dilute acid to make powdered calcite fizz. The fizz is the stone reacting, which is why it has no place on a polished piece or near your skin.
- **Nail polish remover.** A swab can lift some dyes, but it is a destructive test. It damages the stone it is meant to judge.
- **Scratching glass or steel with a finished stone.** The International Gem Society says to scratch test "only as a last resort and never on a finished gem." Glass and nails also vary from 4 to 7 in hardness, so the result means little.
- **A water bath.** Hydrophane opal absorbs water, and Ethiopian hydrophane opal can crack.
- **A lick or a taste.** Some minerals are toxic, and toxic gems do not belong in anyone's mouth.

## Can You Tell If a Crystal Is Real from a Photo?

No. A photo shows color and shape, and color shifts with the light, the camera and the screen. The clues that matter most, a bubble, a pooled dye line, the weight in your hand, need the stone itself, and a lab-grown stone shares the look of the natural one. A photo can make a guess likely, for example a citrine cluster with orange tips over a white base. It can never prove a stone is natural. The same goes for apps, which work from the same picture.

## When to Ask a Gem Lab

Ask a lab when the price depends on the answer. That covers natural citrine sold at a premium, untreated turquoise, jade sold as untreated jadeite, opal priced by the carat and any moldavite costing more than a souvenir. For a tumbled stone from a bowl, the checks above are usually enough to decide what you are happy to pay.

A lab sees what you cannot. GIA's gemologists use a refractometer, a polariscope, magnification up to 50x and a range of spectroscopy and chemical tests, and its Colored Stone Identification Report identifies the gemstone, including any treatments the lab can detect. Some treatments need that equipment. Plastic in opal usually requires major lab equipment to detect, and smoke-treated Ethiopian opal can be caught with Raman spectroscopy. Testing need not be costly either. Separating natural from lab-grown citrine can often be done for less than $100.

Labs have limits too. The International Gem Society says the treatment that turns colorless topaz blue "so nearly duplicates what happens in the Earth, a treated stone cannot be distinguished from a natural."

## Does It Matter If Your Crystal Is Fake?

It matters for three practical reasons. You should pay a fair price for what you have. A treated or imitation stone can need its own care, which is one reason the FTC Guides expect sellers to disclose treatments that create special care needs. And a seller who labels honestly deserves your repeat business.

**What Research Says.** For meaning, the answer may be less than people fear. In a randomized study of 138 adults, people held real rose quartz or a glass look-alike for 14 days, and anxiety scores fell only in those who believed in crystal healing, with the real stone doing no better than the glass ([Escolà-Gascón and colleagues, CNS Spectrums, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1092852925100515)). It was one trial in adults with low anxiety, scores were self-reported, and some participants could in principle have recognized the glass, so it does not show that either stone treats anything.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** If worry or anxiety is part of why you reached for a crystal, talk to a doctor or a therapist as well. A stone, real or glass, is not a treatment. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/), any time, day or night, in the United States. In an emergency, call 911.

## Fakes Are as Old as Gem Lore

**Tradition.** Glass copies of gems are nothing new. Pliny the Elder, writing in the 1st century, noted that iaspis, a stone read as jasper, was imitated in glass, described an artificial obsidian made of colored glass, and, in his chapters on opal, described a sunlight test for glass fakes (*Natural History*, Book 37, chapters 37 and 22, and Book 36, chapter 67). George Frederick Kunz recorded that black opals were once faked by dipping light-colored stones into ink (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, page 151). GIA still calls manufactured glass "an age-old gem imitation that is still used today."

## Three Questions to Ask the Seller

1. What is the mineral name of this stone, apart from the selling name?
2. Has it been heated, dyed, stabilized, coated or treated in any other way?
3. Is it natural, lab-grown or an imitation, and will you write that on the receipt?

A good seller answers all three without fuss, and "we don't know" is an honest answer too. The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) turns a name on a tag into what it usually means, with the house label beside it, and gives you these questions to copy.

**Try This.** Before you pay for a stone you love, take two photos, one of the tag and one of the stone in daylight, and send the seller the three questions. Keep their reply with the receipt in the box the stone came in. If you ever ask a lab or resell the piece, the paper trail is already there.
