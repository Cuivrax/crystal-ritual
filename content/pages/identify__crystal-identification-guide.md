# Crystal Identification Guide with Simple Home Tests

Crystal identification at home works like a short key. Note the color in daylight, how much light passes through and how the surface shines, then run a few safe tests for hardness, streak, magnetism and weight. That narrows most stones to a few candidates, and only a gemologist with instruments can confirm which one you have.

## What You Need, and What to Leave Out

Everything here fits in a kitchen drawer.

- Daylight near a window and a sheet of white paper.
- A jeweler's loupe or a strong magnifying glass.
- An ordinary fridge magnet.
- A piece of quartz you already know, about the same size as the mystery stone, for comparing weight.
- For rough stones only, a streak plate (a small square of unglazed porcelain sold in rock kits), a real piece of copper and a steel nail.

**Safety.** Leave out lighters, vinegar, acids and nail polish remover. Never lick or taste an unknown stone, since some minerals are toxic and the International Gem Society advises against putting toxic gems in anyone's mouth ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gemstone-toxicity-table)). Keep a stone you cannot name out of water you drink, and keep small stones away from small children, who can choke on them.

We also leave glass out of the kit. Geology.com's table of everyday testing objects gives glass a hardness anywhere from 4 to 7, so scratching it tells you little, and broken glass cuts ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/mohs-hardness-scale.shtml)).

## How to Identify Crystals, Step by Step

Our [Crystal Identifier](/tools/crystal-identifier/) asks the same questions, apart from the streak step, which it leaves out, and returns three to five candidates with what sets each one apart. Work through them with the stone in your hand and write down each answer.

### Step 1. Color, in Daylight

Color is the first thing you notice and the least reliable clue. Quartz alone occurs in almost every color ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml)), while stones of different minerals can share one shade. Check the stone in daylight on white paper, because lamps and screens shift yellows and blues. Note whether the color is even, banded or speckled, and whether it gathers in cracks, which can point to dye.

### Step 2. How Much Light Gets Through

Hold the stone up to the window. Transparent means you could read through it, translucent means light glows through it, and opaque means none does. Quartz is transparent to translucent ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml)), rose quartz is usually translucent to cloudy ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/rose-quartz/gem-overview)), and hematite, pyrite and magnetite are opaque.

### Step 3. Luster, the Way It Shines

Luster is "the light-reflecting characteristics of a mineral specimen," in Geology.com's words. A metallic luster has the brightness of metal and always comes with an opaque stone. Most minerals are nonmetallic, and the common words are vitreous (like glass), waxy (like a candle), silky (like the satin spar form of gypsum), pearly, greasy and dull ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/luster.shtml)).

**The limit.** The same page warns that "luster is not a diagnostic property." Use it to split metallic stones from the rest, then move on.

### Step 4. Shape, Breaks & Flashes

Look at the stone with the loupe and ask what its surface is doing.

- **Long crystals with fine lines running their length** and a rounded triangular cross-section suggest tourmaline ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)). A clear crystal with a point at each end may be the kind of quartz sold as Herkimer diamond, a name for doubly terminated quartz crystals from Herkimer County, New York ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/articles/herkimer-diamonds.shtml)).
- **Curved, shell-like breaks** are conchoidal fracture, typical of quartz, opal and obsidian (Geology.com on [quartz](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml), [opal](https://geology.com/gemstones/opal/) and [obsidian](https://geology.com/rocks/obsidian.shtml)).
- **Flat, stepped breaks** are cleavage. Calcite cleaves in three directions ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/calcite.shtml)), and fluorite is the only common mineral with four directions of perfect cleavage ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/fluorite.shtml)).
- **Bands.** Straight, parallel black and white stripes make onyx, while wavy or curved bands make agate ([JCK](https://www.jckonline.com/magazine-article/black-chalcedony/)).
- **Flashes.** Rainbow play-of-color belongs to opal alone ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/opal-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). Broad flashes of blue, green, yellow, orange and red that move as you turn the stone suggest labradorite ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/labradorite/)).
- **Sparkle inside.** Reflective flakes give aventurine its sparkle, called aventurescence ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/aventurine/)), and flat reflective inclusions do the same in sunstone ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/sunstone/gem-overview)).

**Try This.** Set a clear stone on a printed line and look down through it. If the line doubles, think calcite, which shows strong double refraction ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/calcite.shtml)). It is the safest test on this page and the most fun.

### Step 5. Hardness, Only on a Hidden Spot of a Rough Stone

A scratch is permanent, so the rule comes first. The International Gem Society advises scratch testing "only as a last resort and never on a finished gem" ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/fluorite-jewelry-gemstone-information/)). Never scratch a tumbled stone, a polished tower, a faceted gem or anything set in jewelry. Use a rough piece and a spot you will never see, such as the underside.

Work on a bench or a covered table, not fine furniture. Hold the stone so a slipping tool cannot reach your fingers, and drag the tool away from your body and parallel to the fingers holding the stone ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/mohs-hardness-scale.shtml)). Then try three tools in order.

1. **Your fingernail.** A mark means a very soft stone, like selenite, the gypsum variety at 1.5 to 2 ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gypsum-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)).
2. **A real piece of copper.** If copper marks it and your nail does not, think of a stone near 3, like calcite ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/calcite.shtml)). Use copper wire or sheet, not a coin, since pennies are now made of zinc.
3. **A steel nail.** If the nail marks it and copper does not, you are in the middle of the scale, where fluorite sits at 4 ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/fluorite.shtml)). Quartz, at 7, should resist all three.

Wipe a finger across the spot and look with the loupe. Geology.com puts it plainly, "a scratch will be a distinct groove cut in the mineral surface, not a mark on the surface that wipes away." A soft tool can leave a trail of its own metal, like chalk on a blackboard.

**The limit.** Everyday objects vary. Geology.com's table gives a fingernail 2 to 2.5, copper 3 and nails anywhere from 4 to 7, and it recommends confirming the hardness of every item in a home kit ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/mohs-hardness-scale.shtml)). Treat each result as a range. Our [Mohs hardness guide](/identify/mohs-hardness-scale-for-crystals/) has the full scale.

### Step 6. Streak, for Rough or Broken Pieces

The streak is the color of a mineral's powder. Rub a rough corner across an unglazed porcelain streak plate and look at the line it leaves ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/streak-test.shtml)). Hematite always leaves a reddish streak, even when the stone looks silver or black ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/hematite.shtml)). Pyrite leaves a greenish black to brownish black one, and magnetite a black one ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/magnetite.shtml)).

**The limit.** Geology.com's table rates a streak plate at 5 to 7, and a stone harder than the plate leaves no streak at all, so quartz leaves none. Streaking also grinds off a little of the stone, which is why it is for rough pieces only.

### Step 7. The Fridge Magnet

Magnetite is one of just a few minerals that are attracted to a common magnet ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/magnetite.shtml)). Natural hematite is not magnetic ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/hematite-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)), though many hematite specimens hold enough magnetite to be pulled toward one ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/hematite.shtml)). A shiny bracelet that grabs a magnet hard is often hematine, a magnetic look-alike with no hematite in it at all (IGS). [Hematite vs magnetite](/compare/hematite-vs-magnetite/) goes further.

### Step 8. Weight in the Hand

Hold the mystery stone in one hand and your known quartz in the other. Quartz has a specific gravity of 2.6 to 2.7 ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml)). Hematite runs 5.0 to 5.3, pyrite 4.9 to 5.2 and magnetite about 5.2 (Geology.com on [hematite](https://geology.com/minerals/hematite.shtml), [pyrite](https://geology.com/minerals/pyrite.shtml) and [magnetite](https://geology.com/minerals/magnetite.shtml)), so each feels nearly twice as heavy for its size. Fluorite, at 3.2, is detectably heavier than most minerals ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/fluorite.shtml)), and opal, at 2.0 to 2.2, is lighter than quartz ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/opal/)).

**The limit.** Your hand only notices big differences. The International Gem Society notes that a specific gravity test easily separates calcite from gypsum, two clear stones that look alike, but measuring it precisely is a job for a gemologist.

| Black | [Obsidian](/stones/obsidian/), [black tourmaline](/stones/black-tourmaline/), [onyx](/stones/onyx/), [hematite](/stones/hematite/) | Obsidian breaks in curved, shell-like surfaces. ## What Is This Crystal? Start with Where It Came From

Mystery stones tend to arrive in one of three ways, as a gift, from a shop bowl or from a walk. Each one changes your first guess.

A gift or a shop stone usually came with a name, and that name is a lead, not an answer. Selling names often hide the mineral. "Rainbow moonstone" is technically labradorite ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/moonstone-jewelry-gem-information/)), and "Indian jade" is a name often used for green aventurine ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/jade/)). The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) turns a label into what it usually means. A stone you found outdoors has no label at all, so the key above is your starting point.

Until you know what it is, care for it like a stone with no data. Keep it dry, out of the sun and out of anything you drink. The [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/) has the rating once you have a name.

## Can an App or a Photo Identify a Crystal?

An app or a photo can suggest candidates. It cannot confirm one. A photo carries color, shape and shine, and color is the least reliable of the three, shifted by the light, the camera and the screen it is viewed on. It carries no hardness, no weight, no streak and no pull to a magnet.

Some differences never show in a picture at all. A lab-grown gem "shares virtually all chemical, optical, and physical characteristics of its natural mineral counterpart," says [GIA](https://www.gia.edu/gem-synthetic), and the dye in a dyed agate is stable and hard to see ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/agate/)). A good photo of a cluster with orange tips and a white base can hint at heated amethyst, though it proves nothing.

Use an app result the way you would use a friend's guess. Add it to your list of candidates, then check it against the steps above. A photo or these tests can narrow it down but cannot confirm it. A gemologist can.

## When to Ask a Gemologist

Ask when the answer changes the price, when a stone is sold as rare, or when the piece matters to you. At GIA, for example, gemologists work with a refractometer, a polariscope and a spectroscope, test the specific gravity of loose stones, look at up to 50x magnification and run spectroscopy and chemical analysis that no home kit can match. Its Colored Stone Identification Report identifies and describes the gemstone, including any treatments the lab can detect ([GIA](https://archive.gia.edu/gia-news-research/guide-how-gia-analyzes-colored-stones)).

For a gift from a grandparent or a market find, a jeweler with gemological training is a good first stop. Bring your notes from the steps above. They save time and show which tests you have already run.

**Try This.** Keep a small card in the box with each stone. Write where it came from, the date, the color in daylight, how much light gets through, the luster, and the result of each safe test. Two minutes at the kitchen table now saves guesswork later, and the card goes with the stone if you ever ask a gemologist. When you want to know whether the label told the truth, [how to tell if a crystal is real](/real-or-fake/how-to-tell-if-crystals-are-real/) picks up where this guide stops.
