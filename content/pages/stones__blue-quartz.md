# Blue Quartz Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

Blue quartz is not one stone. The name covers quartz colored blue by another mineral, such as dumortierite quartz from Brazil, natural quartz that looks blue because tiny inclusions scatter light, and quartz that was lab-grown, dyed or coated blue. Crystal shops link it to calm and clear speech. Those meanings are modern, so the first question is which blue quartz you have.

> **Is It Real? Quick Checks.** Ask which blue quartz it is. A transparent crystal in vivid cobalt blue is lab-grown, since that color is not found in natural quartz. Blue that pools in cracks or between grains under a loupe suggests dye. A neon or metallic shine that sits only on the surface suggests a coating. Natural dumortierite quartz can fool even these checks, so only a lab can confirm a valuable piece.

## What Blue Quartz Actually Is

**The Stone.** Quartz is silicon dioxide, "the most abundant mineral in Earth's crust," with a hardness of 7 on the Mohs scale that "makes it very durable" ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml)). Its plain form, rock crystal, is colorless (International Gem Society). When quartz turns up blue, something else is doing the coloring, and that something decides what your stone really is.

### Types of Blue Quartz

Here is what the name can mean on a tag, from most natural to least.

1. **Dumortierite quartz.** "A dense, deep blue to violet material made up of crystalline quartz colored by dumortierite, a complex borosilicate," in the [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/quartz-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)'s words. One crystal shop guide says most blue quartz owes its color to dumortierite.
2. **Light-scattering blue quartz.** Natural quartz grains in some rocks look blue because tiny inclusions scatter light, much as fine particles in the air make the sky blue. It is found as grains in certain rocks rather than as loose crystals (Mineralogical Society of America).
3. **Quartz with blue fibers or flakes.** Hawk's eye is crocidolite turned to quartz that kept its fibrous structure and its original blue color ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tigers-eye-gem-information/)), and blue aventurine is a translucent quartz or quartzite with sparkling inclusions ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/aventurine/)).
4. **Lab-grown blue quartz.** Grown in a lab and colored with cobalt or iron, according to the Mineralogical Society of America.
5. **Dyed or coated quartz.** Natural quartz or quartzite with a blue dye (International Gem Society) or a thin metal film, such as aqua aura ([Minerals.net](https://www.minerals.net/mineral/quartz/aqua-aura)).

### What Is Dumortierite Quartz?

Dumortierite is a separate mineral, named after the French paleontologist Eugène Dumortier (1803 to 1873). When it forms as dense inclusions inside quartz, "this combination results in a natural blue Quartz gemstone," says [Minerals.net](https://minerals.net/mineral/dumortierite). The trade calls it dumortierite quartz. In Macaúbas, Bahia, Brazil, it is known locally as "Bahia Blue Quartz," and in the United States blue and lavender dumortierite in dense fibrous form comes from the Dehesa deposit near Alpine, California (Minerals.net). The International Gem Society calls it "somewhat rare" and lists "California lapis" as a misnomer for it.

A 2013 case in GIA's journal shows how confusing it can look. A gem lab in Jaipur, India, received an opaque blue cabochon submitted as sapphire and first suspected dyed quartzite. Testing showed a natural mix of white quartz grains in a blue dumortierite matrix, with a specific gravity of 2.99 against 2.65 for pure quartz. The author estimated it at roughly 55 percent quartz and 45 percent dumortierite, and noted that identification "would have been difficult in the absence of Raman analysis" ([Gems & Gemology](https://www.gia.edu/articles/spring-2013-gemnews-dumortierite-quartz-rock-sapphire), Spring 2013).

### Natural Blue Quartz That Scatters Light

Some natural blue quartz has no blue mineral in it at all. "The blue color in quartz results from Rayleigh scattering in the quartz," explains the [Mineralogical Society of America](https://msaweb.org/msa/Collectors_Corner/faq/faqquartz.htm), caused by extremely small inclusions of the mineral ilmenite. Such quartz looks blue in reflected light and "orangeish-brown in transmitted light." The society points to blue quartz from the Llano uplift in Texas and the Roseland district of Virginia.

A 2011 study of blue quartz from eight occurrences worldwide reached the same answer. "Rayleigh scattering of light by nano-particulate inclusions best explains the origin of the blue colour," wrote Seifert and colleagues, who found mica, ilmenite and rutile as the most common tiny inclusions, in quartz that formed at roughly 700 to 900 °C ([Mineralogical Magazine](https://doi.org/10.1180/minmag.2011.075.4.2519)). The International Gem Society describes a related effect, Tyndall scattering, in some Madagascar rose quartz, which can show pink and blue in the same stone.

### Is There a Natural Blue Quartz Crystal?

Not as a clear, evenly blue crystal point. Natural blue quartz is usually dense and opaque, like dumortierite quartz, or blue only in reflected light, like the scattering kind, which turns orange-brown when light shines through it. A transparent crystal point in an even, vivid blue does not fit either. The International Gem Society lists cobalt blue among the synthetic quartz colors not found in nature, and the Mineralogical Society of America says synthetic blue quartz colored by cobalt or iron is "not known to occur naturally."

### What Is Siberian Blue Quartz?

Treat it as a name, not an origin. On amethyst, the International Gem Society notes that "Siberian" describes a color and "does not refer to its origin." A see-through quartz in strong cobalt blue is lab-grown by the rules above, whatever the tag says [TO CHECK a reference source on the material sold as Siberian blue quartz and where it is grown]. Lab-grown is not a fake in itself. It is only a problem when it is sold as natural.

### Is Star Blue Quartz Real?

A natural star is possible in principle. The Mineralogical Society of America notes that when the tiny inclusions line up in the same orientation, "star effects can be generated." The International Gem Society knows star quartz as white, rose or gray, so a blue star is the less familiar case [TO CHECK a reference source and locality for natural blue star quartz]. Check the back of any star cabochon, because the IGS lists foil backing and coatings on cabochons, used to boost color or produce a star, and notes that they can be detected on the surface.

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** Blue quartz has no old lore of its own. George Frederick Kunz's two books on gem lore (1913 and 1915) have nothing on it, and one crystal guide says outright that its meanings come from modern crystal practice rather than ancient texts. Its real history is a story of science and manufacturing.

- **Eugène Dumortier (1803 to 1873).** The French paleontologist gives his name to dumortierite, the mineral that colors dumortierite quartz (Minerals.net).
- **World War II.** Quartz becomes one of the first gems grown synthetically on a large scale, to supply crystals for radios (International Gem Society). Today synthetic quartz "comes in every color of the rainbow," cobalt blue included.
- **1980s.** Gold-coated aqua aura quartz is first sold, according to the jeweler [Skyjems](https://skyjems.ca/pages/encyclopedia-aquaaura-quartz).
- **20th century, crystal books.** **Modern Association.** One shop guide credits 20th-century crystal writers with exploring the meanings of each quartz color [TO CHECK book titles and years].
- **2011.** Seifert and colleagues explain rock-forming blue quartz by light scattering from nano-sized inclusions.
- **2013.** GIA's journal reports dumortierite quartz submitted as sapphire, and suggests the material is not routinely seen in the trade "perhaps due to lack of awareness or because it is being misrepresented."
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** Shops tie blue quartz to calm, clear communication, honest speech and the throat chakra.

## What People Use Blue Quartz for Today

**Modern Association.** In crystal shop guides, blue quartz is Often Linked with [anxiety](/crystals-for/anxiety/) and with [focus](/crystals-for/focus/), and Sometimes Linked with love, protection, confidence and grief. The throat chakra comes up most, with the third eye behind it.

The "why" is color. Blue is widely read as a cooling color, tied to sky, water and stillness, as one guide puts it, and shops most often place it at the throat chakra, which they tie to speech and expression. From there come the themes of calm speech, finding the right words and keeping a clear head. Shops place it by the phone, on a desk where emails get written, on a bedside table or in a shared family room where conversations happen.

Some associations are marginal. Zodiac signs scatter across Gemini, Cancer, Scorpio and Pisces, with the ox and the snake in the Chinese zodiac and the number 4 in one guide. Treat any one of them as a single shop's choice.

### What Does Blue Quartz Do?

As a mineral, quartz has electrical properties and heat resistance "that make it valuable in electronic products" (Geology.com), and the IGS notes that "our computer industry is based on synthetic quartz." Everything else people say blue quartz does is belief about the person holding it.

Some sellers go much further and list the lungs, blood clots, the thyroid, depression, fibromyalgia and joint pain. Those are health claims with no mineral basis, and one crystal guide says plainly that blue quartz does not treat, cure or prevent any condition. One shop also suggests a blue quartz elixir to drink. Skip it. No stone belongs in water you drink, and dyed or coated stones least of all.

## How to Use Blue Quartz in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** These ideas do not depend on believing anything about the stone. They use a cool blue object as a cue to slow down before you speak.

### The Pause Before You Send

**Try This.** Keep a tumbled blue quartz next to your keyboard. Before you send an email you wrote while annoyed, pick up the stone and read the message once out loud. If any sentence sounds harsher spoken than written, change it, then send.

### Before a Hard Call

**Try This.** Hold the stone for one slow breath before a difficult phone call or meeting. Decide your first sentence and the one thing you want the other person to understand. Put the stone down when you dial, and pick it up again when you hang up.

### The Talking Stone at the Table

**Try This.** In a family or a shared house, keep one blue quartz in the room where arguments tend to start. Whoever holds it gets to finish their sentence, and the others wait. It works with kids, partners and roommates, and the rule matters more than the stone.

### A Gift With an Honest Label

**Try This.** For someone starting a job where they talk all day, a teacher or a nurse, give a blue quartz with a card that says which kind it is. "Dumortierite quartz, quartz colored blue by another mineral." An honest name is a better gift than a vague one.

**Safety.** Small tumbled stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

## Is My Blue Quartz Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone.

- **Natural, untreated.** Dumortierite quartz, light-scattering blue quartz, hawk's eye and undyed blue aventurine.
- **Natural, treated.** Dyed quartz or quartzite. The International Gem Society says dye shows up as concentrations of color under magnification, and that some dyes fade while most are stable. Quench crackling, which cracks the stone on purpose so dye can soak in, also weakens it. Geology.com adds that aventurine and plain translucent quartz "are often dyed bright colors." Coated quartz, like aqua aura, is "quartz synthetically enhanced with a coating using gold (and sometimes other metals)," says [Minerals.net](https://www.minerals.net/mineral/quartz/aqua-aura).
- **Lab-grown.** Synthetic quartz colored with cobalt or iron. The IGS notes that "the synthetics are always clean," with none of the natural inclusions of mined quartz.
- **Imitation.** Glass. Goldstone, a man-made glass with metal flakes, can have a blue base and imitates aventurine (Geology.com). Crystal sellers also warn about dyed howlite and glass sold as blue quartz.
- **Trade names.** "Blue quartz" itself, "Bahia Blue Quartz" (a local name for dumortierite quartz), "California lapis" (a misnomer) and "Siberian."

Three checks you can do at home, with their limits.

1. **Look at where the blue sits.** Under a loupe, dye gathers in cracks and along the boundaries between grains. The limit is real. In the GIA case, natural dumortierite quartz showed blue "along the boundaries of white to colorless grains, as seen in dyed materials," and only reflected light and lab tests sorted it out.
2. **Look at the surface.** A coating sits on the outside, and the IGS notes that it can be detected on the surface. A neon or metallic shine, or color that stops at a chip, points to a film. A clean, unchipped piece can hide it.
3. **Judge the color and clarity together.** A transparent crystal in vivid cobalt blue with no inclusions fits lab-grown quartz. Natural blue quartz is usually dense and opaque, or blue only in reflected light. A lab report is the only proof.

Three questions to ask the seller. Which blue quartz is this, dumortierite quartz, natural blue quartz or something else? Is the color natural, dyed, coated or lab-grown? Where was it mined? ## How to Care for Blue Quartz

**Mineral Fact.** Quartz rates 7 on the Mohs scale and is chemically inert with most substances (Geology.com). Coated and dyed blue quartz are more delicate than the quartz underneath.

- **Water.** Unknown in our Care Checker.
- **Sun.** Unknown.
- **Heat.** Brief only. Quartz is stable only below 573 °C (about 1,063 °F), the International Gem Society notes, so keep it away from flames and very hot surfaces.
- **Chemicals.** OK for quartz itself. Keep household cleaners off coated and dyed pieces.
- **Salt.** Unknown.
- **Storage.** Brief only. Quartz is durable, but store coated pieces apart, since a film scratches long before the quartz does. See [aqua aura quartz](/stones/aqua-aura-quartz/) for coated stones.
- **Toxicity.** OK in our Care Checker. The International Gem Society rates wearing quartz jewelry and handling an intact stone as negligible risk. The hazard is dust from cutting or grinding quartz ([IGS care guide](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gemstone-care-guide/)), so leave that to a lapidary with proper dust control.

### Can Blue Quartz Go in Water?

Our Care Checker rates water, salt and soaking as Unknown for blue quartz, and the answer also depends on what made it blue. Quartz itself is inert with most substances, but a dye or a coating may not be. One shop suggests a 24-hour saltwater soak. Our Care Checker says skip it for now, and never with coated or dyed stones. If your tag names dumortierite or aura quartz, look that name up in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/) too.

## Blue Quartz vs Look-Alikes

- **Sodalite, lazurite and lazulite.** Compared with dumortierite, they lack the fibrous structure, are usually a more intense blue and are softer (Minerals.net).
- **Sapphire.** Dumortierite quartz has been submitted to a lab as sapphire (GIA).
- **Blue aventurine.** Quartz or quartzite with sparkling inclusions. See [blue aventurine](/stones/blue-aventurine/).
- **Hawk's eye.** Quartz that replaced blue crocidolite fibers and kept their color (IGS).
- **Dumortierite.** The blue mineral itself, which has its own page at [dumortierite](/stones/dumortierite/).

## Price Guide

Quartz is common, and the International Gem Society says even clear rock crystal has "little intrinsic value, except in very large, flawless pieces." Dumortierite quartz is "somewhat rare" by the same source. What drives price is which blue quartz it is, so a fair price starts with an honest name. [TO CHECK US retail ranges]
