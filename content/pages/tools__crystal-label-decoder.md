# Is My Crystal Real? Label Decoder

The Crystal Label Decoder tells you what the words on a crystal label or listing usually mean. Type a term such as "AAA grade", "genuine", "stabilized" or "rainbow moonstone" and it gives you a colored label, what the term usually means and three questions to ask the seller. You can also paste a whole label, such as "Genuine AAA natural rainbow moonstone", and every term it knows gets its own card.

## The Five Labels We Use Everywhere

Most results land on one of these five labels, the same ones we use on every stone page.

1. **Natural, untreated.** Mined, then only cut or polished.
2. **Natural, treated.** Mined, then heated, irradiated, dyed, stabilized or coated. Treated is not fake.
3. **Lab-grown.** The same mineral, made in a laboratory.
4. **Imitation.** Something else that looks the part, such as glass, plastic or another stone.
5. **Trade name.** A selling name that may hide what the stone is.

The tool adds two more cards. **Label Word** is for words on a tag that describe a claim rather than a stone, such as "genuine", "natural" or "AAA". **Varies** is for names that cover several realities at once, for example opalite and citrine, which can be natural, treated or man-made. Some results are marked "Based on seller descriptions, not yet confirmed by a gem lab or reference source", which means we found the description in seller guides and no reference source confirms it yet.

## What the Words on the Label Mean

### Natural, Genuine & Real

These words promise less than they seem to. In plain terms, the Federal Trade Commission's Jewelry Guides call it unfair or deceptive to use words like "real", "genuine" and "natural" for a stone that was made artificially (16 CFR 23.27). They do not promise an untreated stone. Most citrine is heated amethyst ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/citrine/gem-overview)), a treatment the trade fully accepts ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/citrine-jewelry-gemstone-information/)). The same Guides also ask sellers to disclose a treatment that is not permanent, creates special care needs or significantly affects value (16 CFR 23.24). More on that case in [is my citrine real](/real-or-fake/citrine/).

### AAA Grade

There is no shared scale behind the letters. Writing about spinel, GIA notes that individual companies create their own quality descriptions like AAA, AA and A, and that no standard quality-grading scale exists for that gem ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/spinel/buyers-guide)). Crystal sellers use the letters the same way, as their own grading, and we found no industry standard behind them. The FTC Guides add that a seller who states a grade should say which grading system it comes from (16 CFR 23.1, note 1), so ask. Read AAA as the seller's opinion of their own stock, and compare stones side by side.

### Stabilized & Reconstituted

Stabilized means a porous stone was filled with something to hold it together. All but the highest grades of turquoise may be stabilized through pressure infusion with wax, oil, natural resin or epoxy resin, and lower quality malachite may be stabilized with plastic resins ([IGS turquoise](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/turquoise-jewelry-gem-information/), [IGS malachite](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/malachite-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). That is natural, treated. Our care data keeps water contact brief for turquoise.

Reconstituted goes further. Reconstituted turquoise is ground natural turquoise mixed with plastic or other materials, and gem writers debate whether it counts as a synthetic, a hybrid or a simulant (IGS). Pressed amber, sold as ambroid, is small bits of amber fused under heat and pressure ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/amber-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). On our scale neither one counts as a natural stone.

### Lab-Created, Lab-Grown, Synthetic & Created

All four mean the real mineral, made by people. The FTC Guides ask for a qualifier such as "laboratory-grown" or "laboratory-created" right before the stone's name, just as visible as the name (16 CFR 23.25). Lab rubies are old news. Verneuil published his flame fusion method in 1904 ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/ruby-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). Lab-grown opals are real opals made in a laboratory ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/opal-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)), and most quartz used in electronics is grown in labs ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml)).

### Simulated & Imitation

These mean the stone is something else. Opalite usually means a plastic or glass opal simulant (IGS). Goldstone is glass full of small, flat copper crystals and imitates sunstone and aventurine ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/sunstone/buyers-guide), [Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/aventurine/)). Some "strawberry quartz" and the whole "fruit quartz" family, cherry quartz included, is colored glass, according to the dealer [GemSelect](https://www.gemselect.com/other-info/about-strawberry-quartz.php). The FTC Guides ask for "imitation" or "simulated" right before the stone's name (16 CFR 23.25).

### Aura & Mystic

Both mean a coated stone, natural and treated. Aura quartz is quartz with a thin film of metal, gold for aqua aura, and dealers say the coating must be disclosed ([Skyjems](https://skyjems.ca/pages/encyclopedia-aquaaura-quartz)). Mystic topaz is colorless topaz with a multicolored surface coating applied by chemical vapor deposition, and the International Gem Society warns that the coating scratches easily ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/topaz-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). The same kind of film on quartz is sold as mystic quartz ([Gem Rock Auctions](https://gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/mystic-quartz-information)). More on our [mystic topaz](/stones/mystic-topaz/) page.

|---|---|---|

| Herkimer diamond | Quartz crystals from Herkimer County, New York | Geology.com |
| African turquoise | A jasper, usually dyed, per a bead dealer | Fire Mountain Gems |
| White turquoise | Sometimes undyed howlite, a misleading name | IGS |
| Turquenite, blue howlite | Dyed howlite | Gem Rock Auctions |
| Swiss lapis | Dyed jasper | IGS |
| Amazon jade | Amazonite | IGS |
| Green amethyst | Prasiolite, a green quartz | FTC Guides, Geology.com |
| Yellow emerald | Heliodor, a golden beryl | FTC Guides, Geology.com |
| Red emerald | Red beryl | Geology.com |

| Magnetic hematite, hematine | A magnetic look-alike with no hematite in it | IGS |

Not every trade name is a trick. The trade has generally accepted "rainbow moonstone" ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/moonstone-jewelry-gem-information/)), and our [rainbow moonstone](/stones/rainbow-moonstone/) page explains the labradorite behind it. What matters is whether the seller also tells you the mineral.

## Three Questions to Copy for the Seller

1. What is the mineral name of this stone, apart from the selling name?
2. Has it been heated, dyed, stabilized, coated or treated in any other way?
3. Is it natural, lab-grown or an imitation, and will you write that on the receipt?

A good seller answers all three without fuss. A vague answer to the second one is an answer too.

## The Honest Limit

A label is a claim, and the decoder can only tell you what that claim usually means. It is general information, not legal advice, and it cannot say whether a particular seller broke any rule. Photos and home tests rule stones out more often than they prove them. For a valuable piece, a lab report settles it. A GIA colored stone report, for example, identifies whether a stone is natural or laboratory-grown and names any detectable treatments ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/spinel/buyers-guide)).
