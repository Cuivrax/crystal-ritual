# Yellow Jade Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

Yellow jade is jadeite or nephrite, the two materials called jade, naturally colored pale yellow to honey or yellowish brown. Some yellow stones sold under the name are something else, such as quartzite dyed yellow or what one bead seller calls yellow "jade" quartz. Chinese emperors chose yellow jade to honor the Earth, and crystal lovers today link it with joy and optimism.

> **Is It Real? Quick Checks.** Ask whether it is jadeite, nephrite or another stone, and whether it was dyed. A yellow "jade" bead that feels light for its size may be quartz, since jade is noticeably denser. Color pooled along cracks points to dye. Only a gem lab can confirm jade and its treatment.

## What Yellow Jade Actually Is

**The Stone.** Jade is a cultural word for a very durable material shaped into tools, carvings and jewelry for over 5,000 years. In 1863 the Frenchman Alexis Damour showed that it covers two different materials, jadeite and nephrite. Both come in yellow. Our [jade page](/stones/jade/) covers the whole family, and this page is about the yellow.

### Is Natural Yellow Jade Real?

Yes. Jadeite comes in a wide range of colors, many shades of green, yellow and reddish orange among them, and its color is often unevenly spread. Nephrite can be green, yellow, brown, black, gray or white, in tones more muted than jadeite's. So a yellow jade can be natural. The catch is that dyed jadeite and other dyed stones are sold in the same colors.

### What Makes Jade Yellow?

In nephrite, magnesium makes the stone lighter and iron darker and greener, and combinations of these elements with traces of others and tiny inclusions create the yellowish kinds. Some nephrite is greenish to honey yellow with distinct bands. Jadeite boulders can carry a yellow to brown skin from natural weathering.

Traditional jade names describe these tones. Mutton fat jade, usually nephrite, runs from clear off-white to yellowish. Chicken bone jade is off-white with a yellowish discoloration, and tomb jade, buried with the dead, is often reddish, yellowish or brownish, very likely from oxidation during burial.

### Yellow Jade Quartz & Other Stones Sold as Yellow Jade

Quartzite is a rock made mostly of quartz, and perhaps its main use as an ornamental stone is as a stand-in for jade. Quartzite dyed green, pink or yellow has been sold as jade. One bead seller sells yellow "jade" quartz, also called butter quartz, and says its color is often enhanced with dyes. By the seller's own name, it is quartz, not jade.

Other stand-ins include serpentine, sold falsely as "Korean jade," and glass and plastic. GIA has also reported a 30 kg block of marble submitted as jadeite rough, with a yellow surface just like jadeite's weathered skin and dyed green beneath, and notes that quartzite imitations are fixtures in the rough jadeite market. Under the Federal Trade Commission's Jewelry Guides, describing a product with the incorrect varietal name is unfair or deceptive, so a quartz bead should not be sold as plain "yellow jade." See [serpentine vs jade](/compare/serpentine-vs-jade/).

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** Yellow jade's recorded meaning is imperial and ceremonial, and it is about the Earth.

- **Ancient China.** **Tradition.** Colors of jade were matched to the heavens, the earth and the four directions, with yellow standing for the earth.
- **The Manchu dynasty.** **Tradition.** George Frederick Kunz records that the emperors wore yellow girdles set with different stones for each ceremony. For the Altar of Earth, yellow jade was favored, while lapis lazuli served the Temple of Heaven, red coral the Altar of the Sun and white jade the Altar of the Moon (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, page 245).
- **The six tablets.** **Tradition.** Jade of different colors made the six precious tablets used to worship heaven, earth and the four directions. Earth received an octagonal tablet of yellow jade, Heaven a dark green round one, and the South a tablet of red jade (Kunz 1913, page 245, citing the Bishop Collection's *Investigations and Studies in Jade*, 1906).
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** Joy, optimism, steady confidence and wisdom, with luck borrowed from jade in general.

## What People Use Yellow Jade for Today

### Yellow Jade Crystal Meaning & Chakra

**Modern Association.** In modern crystal practice, yellow jade means cheerfulness and calm self-trust. Modern crystal guides make it Often Linked for [money](/crystals-for/money/), [confidence](/crystals-for/confidence/) and anxiety, Sometimes Linked for protection and sleep, and Rarely Linked for focus.

The "why" is color plus jade's long history. Yellow reads as sunlight and warmth, and guides add jade's reputation for luck and balance. A Japanese jewelry guide gives it the "stone words" hope and wisdom and calls it popular for money and work luck. Guides put it on the solar plexus chakra, above the navel, while jade in general sits on the heart. One bead seller assigns yellow "jade" quartz to Leo.

Some pages claim yellow jade aids digestion or speeds metabolism. Gem references and medicine give no support to those claims.

## How to Use Yellow Jade in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** The stone will not bring luck on its own. It can anchor a small habit you repeat.

### Three Good Things Before Bed

**Try This.** Keep a yellow jade pebble and a notebook on your nightstand. Before lights out, hold the stone and write three things that went right today, however small, then set the stone on the closed notebook. Some guides frame jade's luck as appreciating what you have, and this is the practical form of that idea.

### A Pocket Stone Before a Meeting

**Try This.** Carry a tumbled piece in your coat pocket on a day with a meeting or a hard phone call. In the minute before it starts, close your hand around the stone, take one slow breath and decide the one point you want to make.

### A Housewarming Gift

**Try This.** For a new home, give a small yellow jade carving with a card that tells the Altar of Earth story and says what it is, nephrite or jadeite if the seller confirms it. If it is dyed quartz, say so on the card. A yellow jade dragon or any other carving deserves the same honest label.

**Safety.** Small tumbled stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets, and never put a stone of unknown treatment in water you drink.

**What Research Says.** In three randomized studies, of 192 and 157 college students and 65 adults with neuromuscular disease, people asked to list things they were grateful for reported better well-being than comparison groups on several measures, with the clearest gain in positive mood (Emmons and McCullough, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2003, [doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377)). The results were self-reported and did not involve stones, so the nightstand habit above is our extrapolation.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Yellow jade is often chosen for anxiety, low moods and money worries. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If worry or sadness lasts or feels heavy, talk to a doctor or a therapist. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/), any time, day or night, in the United States. In an emergency, call 911.

## Is My Yellow Jade Real?

- **Natural, untreated.** Yellow jadeite or nephrite. "A" jade means untreated jadeite or nephrite, which may have a wax coating but no other treatment.
- **Natural, treated.** "B" jade has been bleached in acid and filled with polymer, and "C" jade is dyed. Acid can leave jadeite brittle, resin can discolor with heat and sunlight, and dye can fade. Jadeite is treated far more often than nephrite, which is rarely dyed.
- **Lab-grown.** General Electric grew jadeite in the lab as an experiment, with no plans for commercial production when *Gems & Gemology* studied it in 1987. We found no lab-grown yellow jade on the market.
- **Imitation.** Dyed quartz and quartzite, serpentine, glass, plastic and dyed marble.
- **Trade name.** "Yellow jade quartz" and "butter quartz" describe quartz. Treat any "jade" with another mineral's name on the tag as a question.

### How to Check Yellow Jade at Home

1. **Read the whole tag.** "Jade quartz," "Malaysian jade" or a place name before the word jade is a reason to ask what the stone is. Check it in the [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/), which explains Type A, B and C jade and names like Korean jade.
2. **Compare the weight.** Jade is dense, about 3.0 to 3.5 times as heavy as the same volume of water, while quartz and quartzite are lighter. Hold a yellow "jade" bead and a clear quartz bead of the same size side by side. A bead that feels no heavier is a reason to ask. Judging weight by hand is rough, nephrite is only a little denser than quartz, and the test rules nothing in.
3. **Look for pooled color.** With a 10x loupe, an inexpensive jeweler's magnifier, look at drill holes and cracks. Dye often collects along fractures and sits unevenly. Some dyes are hard to see, so a clean look proves nothing.
4. **Skip the scratch test.** A dyed marble tested by GIA scratched at Mohs 2 to 5, against 6 to 7 for jadeite, but a scratch marks your piece for good.

Three questions for the seller. Is it jadeite, nephrite or another stone? Is it Type A, B or C, and was it dyed? Will you put that on the receipt? For an expensive carving, ask for a lab report, which usually costs a tiny fraction of the price of a Type A piece.

## How to Care for Yellow Jade

**Mineral Fact.** Jadeite rates 6.5 to 7 and nephrite 6 to 6.5 on the Mohs scale, where talc is 1 and diamond 10, so take some care against scratches. Both have exceptional toughness and resist breaking and chipping.

- **Water.** Not Enough Data. Keep contact to the quick wash below.
- **Sun.** Brief Only. Jade itself is stable to light, but resin in treated jadeite can discolor and dye can fade, so keep it off the sunny sill unless you know it is untreated.
- **Heat.** Brief Only. A jeweler's torch can harm jade, and dyes can fade with heat.
- **Chemicals.** Brief Only. Warm acids can affect jade, and acid treatment makes some jadeite brittle.
- **Salt.** Not Enough Data. Skip the salt bowl.
- **Knocks and storage.** Brief Only. Tough, but it scratches, so store it apart from quartz and other harder stones.
- **Cleaning.** OK with warm water, detergent and a soft brush. Ultrasonic and steam are safe only for untreated jade.
- **Toxicity.** Not Enough Data in our Care Checker, so no elixirs.

### How to Clean Yellow Jade at Home

1. Add one drop of mild dish soap to a bowl of warm, not hot, water.
2. Dip a soft toothbrush and brush the piece for about 30 seconds, rather than leaving it in the bowl.
3. Rinse quickly under warm running water.
4. Pat dry with a soft cloth and let it air dry.
5. Skip ultrasonic and steam unless a lab has called it untreated.

If your piece turns out to be dyed quartz, treat it just as gently, with no soaks and no strong sun, since dyes can fade with heat and light.

### Can Yellow Jade Go in the Sun?

Briefly. Untreated jade is stable to light, so the sun is not a worry for a lab-tested Type A piece. Unless yours comes with a lab report, you cannot know it is untreated, and resin and dye are what fade or discolor, so the Care Checker rates sun as Brief Only. Check your exact piece in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/).

## Yellow Jade vs Look-Alikes

- **Citrine.** Yellow quartz, lighter in the hand than jade. See [citrine](/stones/citrine/).
- **Yellow calcite.** Far softer, at Mohs 3, and dissolves in acid. See [yellow calcite](/stones/yellow-calcite/).
- **Serpentine.** Usually under 5 on the Mohs scale, with a waxy luster, and sold under names such as New jade and Korean jade. See [serpentine](/stones/serpentine/).
- **White jade.** Creamy mutton fat nephrite can lean yellowish. See [white jade](/stones/white-jade/).

## Price Guide

The first question is what the stone is, since a dyed quartz bead and a jadeite carving are different purchases. Within jadeite, Type B pieces normally sell for a small fraction of a similar Type A piece, and Type C for a small fraction of Type B, which should be bought assuming only a few years of use. The best jadeite can cost more per carat than fine diamonds.
