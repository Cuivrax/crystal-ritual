# London Blue Topaz Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

London blue topaz is the trade name for the darkest common shade of treated blue topaz, made by irradiating naturally colorless topaz and then heating it. Its blue is described as stable, and the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission says it has no reason to believe wearing irradiated gems is harmful. Its calm, honest meanings are modern beliefs.

## What London Blue Topaz Actually Is

**The Stone.** London blue topaz is real topaz, a silicate of aluminum and fluorine that rates 8 on the Mohs scale. Most topaz crystals are colorless, and Brazil is the principal source of gem-quality topaz. One documented example is a 2.26-carat London blue cut from colorless Brazilian topaz, then irradiated and heated.

**Trade Name.** "London Blue" is a trade name for one shade of treated blue topaz, not a variety found in nature. There are three shades, each marked "enhanced."

| Name on the tag | What it means |
|---|---|
| Sky Blue | Aqua blue |
| Swiss Blue | Medium blue, bright, light in tone |
| London Blue | Dark blue, moderate to dark in tone and saturation |

On our five-step ladder, London blue is **Natural, treated (irradiated and heated)**, sold under a **Trade name**. One shop blog even calls it a type of beryl. It is not. Beryl is the mineral behind aquamarine and emerald, and London blue is topaz all the way through.

### What Color Is London Blue Topaz?

Dark, inky blue. GemSelect, a gem dealer, describes the range from "a dark, inky blue to a vibrant, electric shade" and calls London blue the deepest and most intense of the blue topazes. It is also described as an inky medium blue, and it may show a subtle green tinge from the heat treatment.

## How London Blue Gets Its Color

**Mineral Fact.** Natural blue topaz is extremely rare and usually pale blue. Almost all blue topaz in stores today is colorless topaz that has been irradiated and then heated.

The process works in two steps. First the rough is irradiated, which turns it brown. Then the brown stone is heated to a stable blue. Kurt Nassau's 1985 study in *Gems & Gemology* names the three radiation sources in use, gamma rays, high-energy electrons and neutrons, with heating needed in most cases to finish the blue. According to one gem dealer, London blue is typically made with fast neutrons in a nuclear reactor, followed by heat to lighten overly inky tones.

Why does radiation change the color? The particles enter the crystal at high speed, knock electrons out of place and damage the crystal lattice. Those defects change which wavelengths of light the stone absorbs. They are called color centers, and topaz is unusual here, since most gems, like sapphire, get their color from trace elements instead.

The timeline depends on the source. The International Gem Society dates the method to the 1960s, while Geology.com says experiments in the early 1970s revealed that cheap colorless topaz could be turned blue. Either way, before 1970 most topaz in moderately priced jewelry was yellow to brown, and natural blue was rare and expensive.

### Is London Blue Topaz Radioactive?

Slightly, right after treatment, and not by the time it reaches a store. Some irradiation leaves topaz slightly radioactive, but the level starts dropping as soon as treatment ends. London blue may need months of storage for its radioactivity to decay to safe levels.

**Safety.** In the United States, the first distribution of irradiated gems requires a license from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, and the licensed distributor surveys the stones before they reach the market, typically after a couple of months of storage while any radioactivity decays. The NRC states that it "has no reason to believe that wearing irradiated gemstones can be harmful" and knows of no reported cases of harm. Blue topaz treated in the United States goes through the same procedure. For imported stones, a 1990 NRC information notice, No. 90-62, says neutron-irradiated gems from foreign reactors must be imported by an NRC or state licensee, that only the first importer or distributor in the US needs that license, that distributors must tell their customers the gems are neutron-irradiated, and that imported blue topaz is subject to random checks by the NRC or US Customs.

### Can Anyone Tell It Was Treated?

Not with routine tests. The process so nearly duplicates what happens in the Earth that a treated stone cannot be distinguished from a natural one, and Nassau wrote in 1985 that no routine gemological test can definitively settle it. The honest rule is simple. Assume a dark London blue is treated, because almost all of it is.

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** London blue's own story is only a few decades old. Everything older belongs to topaz in general, and that older lore was rarely about blue stones, since natural blue topaz was so scarce.

- **Ancient world.** **Tradition.** Pliny told of a Red Sea island called Topazos, from a Greek word meaning "to conjecture," because it was hard to find. His topazius was greenish and usually what we call peridot (Kunz, *The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, page 291). Before the 20th century all yellow, brown and orange transparent gems were called topaz.
- **Medieval talismans.** **Tradition.** A falcon engraved on a topaz was said to win the goodwill of kings, princes and magnates (Kunz 1913, pages 133 to 134).
- **13th century, India.** **Tradition.** The Hindu physician Naharari wrote that anyone who wears topaz "will be assured of long life, beauty and intelligence" (Kunz, *The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, page 158).
- **15th century, Rome.** **Tradition.** A physician was said to cure plague sores by touching them with a topaz once owned by two popes, and Kunz suspected the patients' faith "may really have helped" (1913, pages 389 to 390).
- **1960s to 1970s.** Treaters learn to turn colorless topaz blue, and blue becomes the topaz most people know.
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** Calm and honesty, borrowed from the color rather than from history.

### Why Is It Called London Blue?

The International Gem Society says the name "originated because London houses the largest blue topaz ever discovered," the 9,381-carat Ostro Stone. That stone, found in Minas Gerais, Brazil, and colored by treatment, went on display in the Natural History Museum's Minerals Gallery from October 19, 2016, on loan from Maurice Ostro. The museum's announcement does not mention the trade name, and the stone's 2016 display does not date the name. A jeweler's blog offers a simpler story, that the name honors London itself. We found no reference source that records when the trade name was first used, so the origin of the name is unsettled.

## What People Use London Blue Topaz for Today

**Modern Association.** Calm is the most common theme in London blue guides. One gem dealer links it with "serenity and emotional balance," and one jeweler's blog calls it a symbol of "honesty, courage, peace, and fidelity." Another jeweler adds love, friendship, clarity of mind and self-confidence, and says it helps you "find your lost voice."

The "why" is the color. The same dealer compares London blue to twilight skies and the depths of the ocean, and the guides read that deep blue as calm and steady. The honesty and fidelity themes fit its anniversary role, since blue topaz is the gem of the 4th wedding anniversary.

### Is London Blue Topaz a December Birthstone?

On some lists, yes. The International Gem Society counts blue topaz among the modern December birthstones. The American Gem Society list we follow names [topaz for November](/birthstones/november/) and tanzanite, turquoise and zircon for December. If you are buying for a December birthday, London blue is a reasonable choice, and you can say why on the card.

Some links are marginal, like calling it a "gem of the sun," wealth and prosperity, or chakra activation. The jewelers' blogs also list throat problems, migraine, digestion and the immune system. Gemological references do not support those claims.

## How to Use London Blue Topaz in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** Whatever you believe about the stone, here is what the ritual does for sure. It gives you a moment you chose, at a time you picked, with something blue to mark it.

### The Honest First Sentence

**Try This.** Guides tie London blue to honesty and courage. Before a conversation you have been putting off, touch the pendant or ring and decide on the true sentence you will say first. Say it before anything else. The stone marks the decision, the sentence does the work.

### The Ring Dish by the Sink

**Try This.** Topaz chips and cleaves easily, so keep a small dish by the sink and take your ring off before dishes, cleaning or the gym. Make the moment count. As the ring goes into the dish, take one slow breath out. When it goes back on, you are done with the chore.

### A Fourth Anniversary Gift With a Note

**Try This.** Blue topaz is the 4th anniversary gem. Write on the card what it really is (natural topaz, colored by irradiation and heat, a stable treatment that the NRC says it has no reason to believe is harmful to wear) and one line of lore, like Pliny's island that was hard to find. Telling the truth about the treatment is part of the gift.

**Safety.** Loose stones and small jewelry are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

**What Research Says.** The slow breath out in the ring dish ritual is the part with research behind it. In a remote randomized study of 108 adults, five minutes a day of breathing exercises for a month, especially exhale-focused cyclic sighing, improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than the same time spent in mindfulness meditation (Balban and colleagues, 2023, *Cell Reports Medicine*, [doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). It began as an exploratory study, volunteers knew which exercise they were doing and rated their own mood, and it tested five minutes of breathing, not one breath and not London blue topaz or any other stone. Treat these as personal practices.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Some guides offer London blue topaz for anxiety, depression, heartbreak or divorce. A stone can be a comfort to wear, but it is not care. If sadness or anxiety lasts or feels heavy, talk to a doctor or a therapist. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/), any time, day or night, in the United States. In an emergency, call 911.

## Is My London Blue Topaz Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone.

- **Natural, untreated.** Natural blue topaz exists but is extremely rare and usually pale. A deep London blue is not what nature usually makes.
- **Natural, treated.** This is London blue, natural topaz irradiated and heated.
- **Lab-grown.** Topaz has been synthesized in labs but is not usually commercially available.
- **Imitation.** Dark blue treated topaz is sometimes sold as aquamarine. We found no reference source naming the imitations most often sold as London blue topaz. In general, colored cubic zirconia makes good imitations of darker blue gems, and synthetic spinel can mimic many natural gems.
- **Trade name.** London Blue, along with Swiss Blue and Sky Blue.

### Is London Blue Topaz Natural?

The topaz is natural, the color comes from treatment. Natural blue topaz is rare, and most London blue is created by treating colorless topaz with radiation and heat.

### London Blue Topaz or Aquamarine?

Dark treated topaz is sometimes used as a simulant for aquamarine, a different gem species, and because topaz is usually cheaper, buyers should be wary of vendors who sell treated topaz as aquamarine. The reverse mistake costs you nothing, since paying topaz prices for real aquamarine is a bargain. See [blue topaz vs aquamarine](/compare/blue-topaz-vs-aquamarine/).

Three checks you can do at home, with their limits.

1. **Judge the color.** A dark, inky blue topaz is treated in practice. This tells you the treatment, not whether it is topaz.
2. **Feel the weight.** Topaz has a specific gravity of 3.4 to 3.6, so it feels solid for its size. You need a known stone of the same size to compare, so this is a hint, not a test.
3. **Look at the price.** London blue sells for roughly $10 to $30 per carat. An "aquamarine" at that price, or a "natural blue topaz" at a premium, deserves questions.

Three questions to ask the seller. Is it topaz, and was the color produced by irradiation and heating? Where was it treated? If it is sold as aquamarine, can you show a lab report? The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) turns names like London Blue and Swiss Blue into plain words.

## How to Care for London Blue Topaz

**Mineral Fact.** Topaz is hard, but it has low tenacity, which means it chips and cleaves easily. Vibrations and heat could split it, and some prongs stress the cleavage plane, so protective settings help.

- **Water.** OK. A soft brush, mild detergent and warm water. Keep soaks brief, since the reference care advice covers washing, not long soaks.
- **Ultrasonic and steam.** Avoid. Topaz can split, and liquid-filled inclusions can make a gem fracture when heated.
- **Heat.** Avoid. Keep it away from heat, and tell your jeweler it is topaz before any repair.
- **Sun.** Unknown for London blue itself (see below).
- **Chemicals.** Unknown. We found no reference source on chemicals and London blue. One jeweler suggests keeping it away from makeup and harsh chemicals.
- **Salt.** Unknown. We found no reference source on salt yet.
- **Storage.** Keep it apart from harder gems and away from knocks. No source gives storage advice for London blue itself.
- **Toxicity.** OK to wear and handle. Topaz has no known toxicity risk, and the NRC knows of no harm from wearing irradiated gems.

### Can London Blue Topaz Go in the Sun?

Our Care Checker shows Unknown for London blue, because no source covers London blue's light stability directly. The reference sources on treated blue topaz in general are reassuring. Nassau's 1985 study in GIA's *Gems & Gemology* says the blue from irradiation and heat treatment is "stable to light," and Geology.com says the treatment is permanent, "so there is no worry about the gems fading with exposure to light." Those statements cover treated blue topaz in general, not this trade name, so we keep Unknown until a reference source names London blue.

### Can London Blue Topaz Get Wet?

For a wash, yes. One jeweler's blog suggests holding the stone under running water for at least 45 minutes to "cleanse" it. Our care data covers washing, not long soaks, so keep it to a quick rinse. Check your own piece in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/).

## London Blue Topaz vs Look-Alikes

- **Swiss blue topaz.** Same stone and treatment, a brighter, lighter blue. London Blue is the market favorite and only slightly more expensive. More on our [blue topaz page](/stones/blue-topaz/).
- **Aquamarine.** A different species, often confused with treated topaz (see above).
- **Blue sapphire.** Corundum, harder at 9 against topaz's 8.
- **Mystic topaz.** Also colorless topaz, but colored by a surface coating rather than through the whole stone. See [mystic topaz](/stones/mystic-topaz/).

## Price Guide

### Is London Blue Topaz Expensive?

No. Before treatment was discovered, natural light blue topaz was rare and valuable while colorless topaz sold cheaply by the ton. Afterward, blue topaz prices fell, and these are now among the least expensive gems available. London blue typically retails for $10 to $30 per carat.

London blue costs a little more than Swiss blue, and more saturated shades generally hold higher value. The reason it stays affordable is abundance, since colorless topaz is plentiful and treatment works at large scale.

What drives price is size, cut and the setting, not rarity. GIA calls color the most important quality factor for topaz and notes that blue topaz rises very little in per-carat price as the size increases. A seller who prices London blue like a rare natural gem is either mistaken or hoping you are.

For a sense of scale, one listing we found, a 0.74-carat pear-shaped stone about 7 by 5 mm, was about $6, and another, a 6.10-carat stone about 12 by 10 mm, was $150. Both listings named irradiation as the treatment. Prices from US-dollar listings at online gem sellers in November 2026.
