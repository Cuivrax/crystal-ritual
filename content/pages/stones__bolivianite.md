# Bolivianite Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

Bolivianite is a trade name for ametrine, a quartz that is purple amethyst in one part of the crystal and golden citrine in another. It is named after Bolivia, whose Anahí Mine is the only commercial source. Crystal lovers treat it as a stone of balance and calm, a modern belief, and the International Gem Society discourages the name itself.

## What Bolivianite Actually Is

**The Stone.** Bolivianite is ametrine, and ametrine is quartz, the same mineral as amethyst and citrine. When one quartz gem shows the colors of both, it is called ametrine or amethyst-citrine, a name built from AMEthyst and ciTRINE. It belongs to the [quartz family](/stones/quartz/).

### Is Bolivianite a Real Mineral?

No. Bolivianite is a selling name, not a mineral species. The mineral is quartz, and bolivianite has no entry of its own on the IMA List of Minerals. You will meet the name especially in Bolivia, and it is best avoided, because it has been applied to other minerals before. According to the gemologist Hobart King, the name is a response to ametrine being designated the national gemstone of Bolivia.

### How Ametrine Gets Two Colors

Both colors come from traces of iron in the quartz, sitting in different oxidation states, meaning the iron atoms carry different electric charges. Purple is thought to form where natural radiation, from potassium-40 decaying in nearby rocks, changes the iron's charge. Golden yellow is thought to be iron left unchanged.

The abrupt switch from one color to the other reflects slight swings in temperature and pressure while the crystal grew. Sawn across, a well-formed crystal often shows purple and yellow wedges radiating from its center like slices of a pie, split by straight lines. That pattern comes from Brazil-law twinning, in which two quartz crystals of different color are intergrown. Exactly why each wedge takes its color was still unexplained in 1994, when the mine was studied in detail.

### Where Bolivianite Comes From

The only commercial source is the Anahí Mine in the Pantanal lowlands of southeastern Bolivia, near the Brazilian border. The quartz grew from hot fluids in fractures and cavities of a dolomitic limestone. A Bolivian company, Minerales y Metales del Oriente, has run the mine since 1989, and more than 100 tons of ametrine crystals came out between 1989 and 1994.

A crystal usually holds clear, purple and yellow zones, so only part of each becomes ametrine, and the rest is cut as amethyst, citrine or clear quartz. Ametrine was first reported from Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil, and non-commercial deposits are known from the United States, Canada, India and Mozambique.

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** Bolivianite has no ancient lore. George Frederick Kunz's 1913 book of gem lore never mentions it, and ametrine only reached the market in quantity in the 1970s.

- **Before Spanish rule.** **Tradition.** According to legend, the Ayoreo people of eastern Bolivia knew the two-colored crystals more than 500 years ago, and the native peoples of the region have long valued the stone.
- **Colonial-era legends.** **Tradition.** Crystal sellers tell a love story between a Spanish conquistador, Don Felipe de Urriola, and an Ayoreo princess named Anahí, whose two-colored stone stands for a heart split between her people and her husband. One of them adds a conquistador bringing the stones to the Spanish queen. Both stories are told without any historical source, and they date them differently. The documented record is plainer. The mine seemingly dropped out of commercial use after the 1600s and was rediscovered in the 1960s.
- **1925.** **Mineral Fact.** An issue of *American Mineralogist* described quartz crystals with alternating purple and yellow color sectors, perhaps the earliest formal record of such zoning.
- **1960s and 1970s.** **Mineral Fact.** Purple and yellow quartz reached the trade with vague origins, so some people suspected it was synthetic or treated. Cutters, inspired by bicolor tourmaline, favored emerald cuts split half purple, half gold.
- **1989 to 1994.** **Mineral Fact.** After a change in Bolivian mining law in 1989, the new owners invited geologists and gemologists to the mine, and a 1994 study concluded that the color combination is natural, not the result of lab treatment.

## What People Use Bolivianite for Today

### What Does Bolivianite Mean?

**Modern Association.** In modern crystal practice, bolivianite means balance, the calm associated with amethyst joined to the cheer associated with citrine. Modern crystal guides make it a Classic stone for [anxiety](/crystals-for/anxiety/), Widely Linked for confidence and protection, and Often Linked for [focus](/crystals-for/focus/). It is Sometimes Linked with money and Rarely Linked with sleep or love.

The "why" is the color split, which guides turn into a symbol for seeing both sides of a choice, so some suggest it for decisions, a new job or a hard conversation. Chakra charts put it on the crown, third eye and solar plexus, and zodiac lists disagree.

Some sites also promise help with headaches, hormones, digestion and detox. Gem references and medicine give no support to those claims.

## How to Use Bolivianite in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** The stone will not make a choice for you. It can give a decision a beginning and an end.

### A Two-Color Decision Stone

**Try This.** When you are stuck between two options, fold a sheet of paper in half. Hold the purple end of the stone and write on the left what you keep if nothing changes. Turn to the gold end and write on the right what you gain by changing. Sleep on it, then reread both columns in daylight before you decide.

### A Fresh-Start Card on the Desk

**Try This.** On January 1, a birthday or the first Monday of a month, write one small habit for the next four weeks on an index card. Set the stone on the card at your desk, away from the afternoon sun, and slide it along the card each day you keep the habit.

### A Gift That Says What It Is

**Try This.** Wrap an ametrine pendant with a card that says what it is (quartz, from the Anahí Mine if the seller confirms it), what people link it with (balance) and one care line, "warm soapy water, no steam, no sunny sill."

**Safety.** Small tumbled stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

**What Research Says.** In three archival field studies tracking Google searches for "diet," gym visits and goal commitments, Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis found that people started goal-directed behavior more often right after dates that feel like a fresh start, such as a new week, month or year ([*Management Science*, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901)). The studies were observational, so they show a pattern, not a cause, and none involved stones.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Bolivianite is often chosen for anxiety and stress. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If worry or stress lasts or feels heavy, talk to a doctor or a therapist. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/), any time, day or night, in the United States. In an emergency, call 911.

## Is My Bolivianite Real?

- **Natural, untreated.** Ametrine from the Anahí Mine, which the 1994 study found natural. Gem references list its usual treatment as none.
- **Natural, treated.** Lab experiments in 1981 showed that heat and irradiation can turn some amethyst into a two-colored look-alike. Doing it well is costly and is not known to have produced much, though heat-enhanced quartz is on the market.
- **Lab-grown.** Russian synthetic ametrine has been produced commercially since 1994, grown in hot alkaline solutions under pressure, and some is exported and sold as ametrine. Most of it has colors an experienced eye reads as synthetic, but the two match optically and physically, so only testing settles it.
- **Imitation.** No common imitation is documented. The look-alike that matters is synthetic quartz.
- **Trade names.** Bolivianite, trystine, golden amethyst and amethyst-citrine all mean ametrine.

### How to Check Bolivianite at Home

1. **Find the color boundary.** Good natural ametrine shows distinct purple and orangy yellow areas, which in a traditional cut meet along an abrupt line. Synthetic ametrine is zoned too, so this tells you it is two-colored quartz, not where it came from.
2. **Look at the color strength.** Very vivid, intense colors are a reason to ask about lab growth, since hydrothermal synthetics are often more intensely colored than natural ametrine. Price proves little, since even natural ametrine is inexpensive for its size and beauty.
3. **Do not judge by clarity.** Faceted ametrine generally has no eye-visible inclusions, so a clean stone is normal and says nothing about origin.

For an expensive stone, ask for a lab report. Labs separate Russian synthetics by growth features such as twinning and color zoning, by trace elements and by infrared spectra.

### What to Ask the Seller

1. Is this natural ametrine, and from which mine?
2. Has it been heated or irradiated?
3. Is it lab-grown? Under the Federal Trade Commission's Jewelry Guides, a lab-grown stone should carry a word like laboratory-grown or laboratory-created, and a treatment should be disclosed when it is not permanent, needs special care or significantly affects value. Ask for the answers in writing.

## How to Care for Bolivianite

**Mineral Fact.** Ametrine rates 7 on the Mohs scale of scratch resistance, where talc is 1 and diamond 10. With good toughness and no cleavage, it suits every kind of jewelry, rings included, with some care against scratches.

- **Water.** OK for a wash in warm, soapy water. A long soak is Brief Only, since only washing is covered.
- **Sun.** Brief Only. Some ametrine colors can fade with prolonged exposure to intense light.
- **Heat.** Keep Away From Heat. Abrupt temperature changes can make ametrine fracture. The International Gem Society calls it insensitive to temperature changes, but GIA says it should not be exposed to heat, so follow the cautious advice.
- **Chemicals.** No Chemicals. Hydrofluoric acid, ammonium fluoride and alkaline solutions can damage it.
- **Salt.** Not Enough Data. Leave salt bowls out.
- **Cleaning.** Ultrasonic cleaners are usually safe unless a stone is dyed or fracture filled. Steam cleaning is not recommended.
- **Toxicity.** Not Enough Data in our Care Checker. As background, the hazard listed for ametrine is silicosis, a lung disease from breathing quartz dust, which concerns cutting, not wearing.

### How to Clean Bolivianite at Home

1. Fill a small bowl with lukewarm water and one drop of mild dish soap.
2. Brush the stone gently with a soft toothbrush, including the back of the setting.
3. Rinse under lukewarm running water, never switching from cold to hot.
4. Pat it dry with a soft, lint-free cloth.
5. Skip the steam cleaner, and skip the ultrasonic unless you know the stone is untreated.

Do it whenever lotion or skin oil dulls the shine.

### Can Bolivianite Go in Water or Sun?

Water, yes, for a wash. Sun, only briefly, because some ametrine colors fade under intense light. Moonlight and sound are the gentle swaps. Check your piece in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/), and see [crystals that fade in the sun](/care/crystals-that-fade-in-the-sun/).

### How to Store & Wear It

1. Give it a soft pouch of its own, away from softer stones it can scratch. Sand and dust are mostly quartz, as hard as ametrine, so a pocket or a dusty bowl wears down the polish.
2. Take rings off for the gym, gardening and household cleaners.
3. Keep it off sunny windowsills, radiators and car dashboards.

## Bolivianite vs Look-Alikes

- **Amethyst and citrine.** Each half on its own. Heat can turn amethyst yellow, which is how a treated look-alike is made. See [amethyst](/stones/amethyst/) and [citrine](/stones/citrine/).
- **Ametrine.** The same stone under its usual name, on our [ametrine page](/stones/ametrine/).
- **Anahite.** A clear quartz with a very light lilac tint, also from the Anahí Mine. It is not ametrine.

## Price Guide

Ametrine is usually inexpensive compared with other stones of similar size and beauty, and certainly less expensive than bicolor tourmaline, even though supply comes from one mine. Value rises with a strong contrast between orange and purple, a fine or unusual cut and size, and large material is popular with carvers. A stone tagged bolivianite should cost what the same ametrine would.
