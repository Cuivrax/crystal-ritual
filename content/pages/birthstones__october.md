# October Birthstone Meaning, History & Honest Buying Tips

The October birthstones are opal and tourmaline, the two stones the American Gem Society lists for the month. Opal is silica with water locked inside that can flash every color of the rainbow, and tourmaline is a mineral group found in more colors than any other, with pink the shade many charts name. Opal has held October since the 1912 US jewelers' list, and it is also the birthstone that most needs careful handling.

## What Is October's Birthstone?

October has two, opal and tourmaline. The American Gem Society lists both, GIA's gem pages name both as October birthstones, and tourmaline counts in any color. Older lists gave October another stone altogether, the beryl.

| List | October stone | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Old lists Kunz attributes to the Jews, Romans, Arabians, Poles and Isidore of Seville | Aquamarine | Kunz 1913, page 315 |
| Russian and Italian lists | Beryl | Kunz 1913, page 315 |
| 15th to 20th century European usage | Beryl, opal | Kunz 1913, page 315 |
| US jewelers, Kansas City, August 1912 | Opal, with tourmaline as alternate | Kunz 1913, pages 319 to 320 |
| Jewelry Industry Council of America, 1952 | Pink tourmaline added | IGS |
| American Gem Society and GIA today | Opal, tourmaline | AGS, GIA |

### Modern vs Traditional October Birthstones

"Traditional" usually means opal, the main October stone of the list US jewelers adopted in Kansas City in 1912. "Modern" means tourmaline, the 1912 alternate.

Go back further and "traditional" means beryl, October's stone in all eight old lists Kunz compared, five times as aquamarine (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, pages 315 to 316). Aquamarine has since moved to [March](/birthstones/march/).

### Is the October Birthstone Pink Tourmaline?

On many charts, yes, but the American Gem Society list names tourmaline with no color given. The 1912 list said only "tourmaline." The Jewelry Industry Council of America added pink tourmaline in 1952, and the International Gem Society's October page still calls pink tourmaline the modern October stone. So a green or blue tourmaline is a correct October birthstone, and pink is the color the charts agree on.

### Why Does October Have Two Birthstones?

GIA's birthstone exhibit says tradition plays a part, but "the more significant reason is that some gemstones are too rare and expensive, and others just aren't as popular." For October the second stone also fixes a practical problem. Opal chips and scratches easily, while tourmaline is tough enough for a ring worn every day.

## Where the October Birthstone Comes From

**Tradition.** The old month lists follow the twelve foundation stones of the New Jerusalem in Revelation, counted from March, when the year was reckoned to begin (Kunz 1913, pages 307 to 308 and 319). The eighth foundation stone is the beryl, so the eighth month counted from March, October, got the beryl (pages 303 and 316). Kunz's calendar of the months keeps it, with this verse. "October's child is born for woe, / And life's vicissitudes must know; / But lay a beryl on her breast, / And Hope will lull those woes to rest" (page 330).

The 1912 jewelers broke that order and chose opal. Kunz objected that neither stone was "as appropriate as the beryl," and that tourmaline, known as a gem only since the 18th century, was "out of place" among birthstones (pages 320 to 321). The full story is on our [birthstone chart](/birthstones/).

## Opal, the Traditional October Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Opal](/stones/opal/) is hydrated silica, chemically close to quartz but holding 3% to 21% water, usually 6% to 10% in gem opal. With no crystal structure, it counts as a mineraloid. It formed when silica gel filled cracks in rock and left tiny silica spheres behind as the water evaporated. In precious opal the spheres are stacked like Ping-Pong balls in a box and split light into moving flashes called play-of-color. Common opal, or potch, lacks that order and shows no flashes.

Australia has been the leading source since its mines began commercial production in the 1890s. Ethiopia became a major producer after discoveries in the 1990s, and Mexico is known for fire opal, named for its yellow to red body color. Many Ethiopian opals are hydrophane, a porous opal that soaks up water and turns transparent while wet, which changes how you clean it. See [types of opal](/stones/family/opal/) for the rest.

**The Story.** "The opal, October's gem, recalls in its wonderful and varied play of color the glories of a bright October day in the country," Kunz wrote (1913, page 144).

- **Ancient Rome.** **Tradition.** Because opal shows the colors of other gems, the Romans thought it the most precious of all. Pliny saw in it "the piercing fire of carbunculus, the purple brilliancy of amethystos, and the sea-green of smaragdus" (*Natural History*, book 37, chapter 21).
- **1829.** Walter Scott's novel *Anne of Geierstein* gave opal an unlucky name. Kunz blamed a careless reading of the book, and gem cutters and setters who sometimes broke the brittle stone, often through no fault of their own, and came to see it as unlucky (pages 143 to 144 and 152). At bottom, the bad-luck legend is a fragility story.

## Tourmaline & Pink Tourmaline, the Modern October Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Tourmaline](/stones/tourmaline/) is a supergroup of more than thirty boron silicate species, and most cut gems are elbaite. It comes in more colors than any other mineral group, and Kunz traced the name to turmali, the name the people of Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, gave the stone (1915, page 52). Manganese produces the reds and pinks, and some pink stones may owe their color to radiation, natural or lab-induced. Saturated dark pink to red stones are often sold as rubellite, a trade name rather than a separate mineral, as our [pink tourmaline](/stones/pink-tourmaline/) page explains. Tourmaline is also the gem of the eighth wedding anniversary.

Brazil has led world production for nearly 500 years. Maine has produced pink and green tourmaline since an 1821 find near Paris, Maine. Southern California became the leading US source, and over 100 years ago tons of red tourmaline from Riverside and San Diego Counties were shipped to China for snuff bottles, carvings and jewelry.

**The Story.** Tourmaline charges with electricity when heated or rubbed, and its lore follows the spark.

- **1700s, Holland.** Dutch lapidaries called it Aschentrekker, the "Ash-Attractor" (Kunz 1915, pages 52 and 54).
- **1759.** Benjamin Franklin wore a tourmaline on a pivot in a ring and found the warmth of his finger enough to charge it (pages 57 to 58).
- **1915, China.** **Tradition.** Kunz describes the marriage sword carried at Chinese weddings, its sheath studded with pink tourmaline, rubellite or rose quartz, brought together with the sword as the ring goes on (pages 384 to 385).

## What October Birthstones Mean Today

**Modern Association.** Modern crystal guides tie opal most often to creativity, then to communication and intuition, and name the crown chakra most. Pink tourmaline is the love stone of the pair, tied to the heart chakra, self-love and compassion. In our rankings opal is Widely Linked for love, protection and money, pink tourmaline is Widely Linked for love, and tourmaline in general is a Classic protection stone. Some sites promise healing, which nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports.

### What Is the October Birthstone Color?

Every color, in practice, since opal flashes the whole spectrum and tourmaline comes in every color. If you want one, choose pink, the shade the charts give October's tourmaline.

October splits between [Libra](/zodiac/libra/), until about October 22, and [Scorpio](/zodiac/scorpio/). Kunz's zodiac verses give Libra the chrysolite, an old name for peridot, and Scorpio the beryl (1913, pages 346 to 347), while several modern guides pair opal with Libra.

## How to Choose an October Birthstone

Start with how it will be worn. On the Mohs scale of scratch resistance, from talc at 1 to diamond at 10, opal rates 5 to 6.5, and its toughness (resistance to chipping) is only very poor to fair. Tourmaline rates 7 to 7.5, has no cleavage (no tendency to split along flat planes) and suits everyday wear.

| Piece | Opal | Tourmaline |
|---|---|---|
| Ring worn every day | Only a triplet, or a solid opal in a protective setting | Good choice |
| Ring for special days | Fine, ideally in a protective setting | Good choice |
| Pendant or earrings | Good choice, the safest home for solid opal | Good choice |

- **A baby's first piece.** A small pendant kept in its box as a keepsake. Small stones are a choking hazard, so it stays out of reach until the child is much older.
- **A gift on a budget.** An opal doublet sells for a small fraction of a solid stone with a similar face-up look, which is fair value as long as it is sold as a doublet.
- **An inherited opal ring.** Look it over with a 10x loupe in daylight for fine cracks, and keep it for special days if you see any.

Opal's value rests on its play-of-color, pattern and body color. Bright flashes spread evenly across the face raise the price, and black opal commands the most per carat. Pink tourmaline's price depends on size, clarity, saturation and color.

### What to Ask Before You Buy

1. **Ask what it is, in full.** Natural, treated, lab-grown or imitation? Solid opal, doublet or triplet? Heat and irradiation are common, often undetectable treatments for tourmaline, and the International Gem Society goes further, saying most pink tourmaline is heated unless the listing states otherwise.
2. **Ask to see the side of an opal.** A thin glue line there means a doublet or triplet. A closed back hides it, so ask.
3. **Ask whether an Ethiopian opal is hydrophane.** It changes the care.
4. **Get the answers on the receipt.** Under the Federal Trade Commission's Jewelry Guides, a seller should disclose a treatment that is not permanent, creates special care needs or significantly affects value. A lab-made or imitation stone may be called a birthstone only with a word such as "laboratory-created" or "imitation" just as visible in front of it.

## Is My October Birthstone Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone, plus one for opal.

### Real or Fake Opal

- **Natural, untreated.** A solid opal, flashes and body in one piece.
- **Natural, treated.** Oil, wax or plastic can improve play-of-color and hide crazing, and smoke, sugar or dye can darken the body. Kunz recorded black opal faked with ink or burnt oil (1913, page 151).
- **Assembled.** A doublet is a thin slice of precious opal glued to a dark base, and a triplet adds a clear cap of quartz or another material. Both hold real opal, but neither is solid.
- **Lab-grown.** Real opal made in a lab, often with a mosaic of color patches and a cellular or chicken-wire pattern under magnification.
- **Imitation.** Plastic or glass, and most "opalite" in shops is glass. See [opal vs opalite](/compare/opal-vs-opalite/).

Three checks at home, with a 10x loupe and one lamp.

1. **Tilt it on a dark cloth.** Moving flashes mean precious opal, lab-grown opal or a good imitation. Even color with no flashes means common opal or something else.
2. **Look at the side.** A straight line between layers means a doublet or triplet.
3. **Look into the color.** Bubbles and swirl marks point to glass, a regular honeycomb to lab-grown opal.

Skip the needle and the glass of water. A needle can mark a real opal, water can change how hydrophane looks, and doublets and triplets are best kept out of water.

### Real or Fake Tourmaline

- **Natural, untreated.** Exists, but it looks exactly like a heated stone.
- **Natural, treated.** Heating gives stable, undetectable color, while irradiated color can fade with heat or bright light.
- **Lab-grown.** None is sold for jewelry.
- **Imitation.** Occasional, mostly multicolor glass or plastic layers glued between clear glass.
- **Trade name.** Rubellite, watermelon and Paraíba, a neon blue colored by copper, all name real tourmaline.

On a rough crystal, look for fine lengthwise lines and a rounded triangular outline from the end. Color proves nothing.

## How to Care for October Birthstones

These ratings come from the same data as our [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/), which shows OK, Brief Only, a warning that names the test such as Keep Dry, or Not Enough Data.

| | Opal | Pink tourmaline |
|---|---|---|
| Washing | Keep Dry. Sensitive to moisture | OK. Warm water, detergent, soft brush |
| Soaking | No Soaking | Brief Only |
| Sun and UV | Not Enough Data | Not Enough Data |
| Heat | Keep Away From Heat. Sensitive to temperature changes | Keep Away From Heat. Moderately sensitive |
| Chemicals | No Chemicals. Hydrophane opal absorbs them | Not Enough Data |
| Salt | Not Enough Data | Not Enough Data |
| Cleaning | Not Enough Data | OK. Warm water, detergent, soft brush |
| Knocks and scratches | Handle Gently. Chips and shatters more easily than most gems | OK. No cleavage, only slight brittleness |
| Toxicity | Not Enough Data | OK. None known |

Where our data is silent, GIA fills in. It calls warm, soapy water the only safe way to clean opal and warns that heat from intense light can cause fine fracture lines called crazing. Doublets and triplets are glued in layers, and one seller's care guide keeps them out of water altogether. Keep Dry means no tap, no soak and no bowl, so the routine below uses a cloth that is barely damp.

### How to Clean an Opal Ring at Home

You need two soft, lint-free cloths and a cup of lukewarm water with one drop of mild dish soap.

1. **Know your opal.** If it is a doublet or triplet, may be hydrophane, or you are not sure, use only a dry cloth and stop at step 2.
2. **Wipe it dry first.** A soft, dry cloth lifts most everyday film.
3. **Wipe with a barely damp cloth.** Dip a corner of the second cloth in the soapy water, wring it until almost dry, and wipe the stone and the metal around it. Never dip the ring.
4. **Rinse the cloth, not the ring.** Wipe again with a clean corner dampened in plain lukewarm water.
5. **Pat dry at once,** and let it rest on the cloth at room temperature, away from a hair dryer, radiator or sunny sill.
6. **Never use** an ultrasonic or steam cleaner, a jewelry dip, toothpaste, bleach or hot water.

Wipe it dry after each wear, and save the damp wipe for when you see a film.

### Everyday Opal Habits

1. **Put it on last,** after lotion, perfume and hairspray.
2. **Take it off** before washing hands or dishes, gardening, the gym, the pool and the hot tub.
3. **Keep it cool and steady.** No car dashboard in summer and no sunny sill, since heat and sudden temperature changes can craze or crack opal.
4. **Store it alone** in a soft pouch, away from harder gems and out of direct sunlight.

### Caring for Tourmaline

Wash it in warm, soapy water with a soft brush, then dry it. Skip ultrasonic and steam cleaners, because pink tourmaline is often flawed and can be damaged by vibration. GIA calls tourmaline generally stable to light and unaffected by chemicals, but high heat can change its color and sudden temperature changes can crack it.

**Where the beliefs and the care data disagree.** Several crystal guides cleanse opal under running water. Our care data marks it Keep Dry and No Soaking, so skip the tap. Moonlight on an indoor sill keeps it dry, as long as you move it before the morning sun reaches the sill. Our care data has no salt rating for either stone yet, so leave salt bowls out.

## Small October Rituals

**Try This.** Give your opal a home, a small dish near the sink but out of reach of splashes, and take the ring off there every time you wash your hands. The dish protects the stone, and the habit takes care of itself.

**Try This.** Franklin found that the warmth of his finger charged his tourmaline. Before a task you have been avoiding, hold a tumbled tourmaline in a closed hand for ten slow breaths, then start.

**What Research Says.** In a 2010 study, 96 volunteers repeated one everyday behavior in the same setting each day, and the time it took to feel automatic ranged from 18 to 254 days, with a single missed day doing little harm ([Lally and colleagues, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)). It was small, self-rated and not about stones, but it argues for keeping the dish for months.

**Safety.** Small stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Opal is often chosen for sleep and anxiety, and tourmaline for anxiety. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If poor sleep, worry or low mood lasts, talk to a doctor or a therapist. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/), any time, day or night, in the United States. In an emergency, call 911.
