# Golden Hills Turquoise Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

Golden Hills turquoise is a trade name for bright blue stone with a lavender undertone, sold as turquoise. The jewelry trade magazine JCK has described it as turquoise from Kazakhstan, and one seller places its original mine in the Altyn-Tyube area there. No gem lab we could find documents the name or the material's makeup, so treat Golden Hills as a seller's label. The meanings attached to it are those of turquoise in general.

## What Golden Hills Turquoise Actually Is

**The Stone.** "Golden Hills" is a name, not a mineral. Sellers use it, along with "Golden Hill," for turquoise they describe as bright blue with a lavender undertone.

The origin story comes from one turquoise seller. It says Altyn-Tyube means "Golden Hill" in Kazakh, and that the original Golden Hill mine lies in that mountain range, with a second "Lavender" mine about 200 miles farther south. The same seller says rough from the two mines gets mixed and is generally marketed as Golden Hill turquoise, and that first batches reached the US market some 30 to 35 years ago. Around 2015, it says, lavender turquoise from Kazakhstan caused a stir at the Tucson gem show, where skeptics called it dyed or doubted it was turquoise at all. It adds that lab testing "has since confirmed it's the real deal," without naming the lab or the results.

Here is what is documented. Turquoise does come from Kazakhstan. Sky-blue turquoise with chocolate-brown matrix is known from the Altyn-Tyube Mine, and turquoise also comes from the Zyryanovsk mine in eastern Kazakhstan. The Golden Hills name is used in the US jewelry trade too. In 2021 JCK described a one-of-a-kind ring set with "7.05 ct. Kazakhstan Golden Hills turquoise," and in 2026 National Jeweler listed two rings with Golden Hills turquoise among the pieces seen at the Las Vegas jewelry shows. These are jewelry captions, not lab reports.

So the honest label for a stone that has not been tested is "sold as Golden Hills turquoise, origin as stated by the seller." It also helps to know that some material sold as turquoise is actually another mineral of the turquoise group, since these minerals are so similar.

### Can Turquoise Be Lavender?

Turquoise usually runs from blue to blue-green to yellowish green. The International Gem Society notes that rare blue-violet turquoise contains strontium impurities. Whether that explains the lavender tone of Golden Hills material, no gem lab we could find has said.

Dye is the other road to an unusual color. Turquoise is porous and accepts dye, and dye can push its color far outside the natural range. That is why a lavender turquoise needs a treatment disclosure in writing.

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** The Golden Hills name is recent, and no tradition belongs to it. The beliefs people attach to it are turquoise's, and turquoise has a long record. More on the mineral in our [turquoise guide](/stones/turquoise/) and on the trade names in [types of turquoise](/stones/family/turquoise/).

- **13th century.** **Tradition.** Volmar's *Steinbuch* promised that whoever owned "the true turquoise set in gold" would not injure a limb in a fall, riding or walking (Kunz, *The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, page 109).
- **Old superstition.** **Tradition.** A turquoise was believed to lose its power if bought rather than received as a gift, the same belief told of the diamond (Kunz 1913, page 73).
- **Persia.** **Tradition.** Kunz calls turquoise the Persians' national stone and reports their saying that to escape evil and gain good fortune you should see the new moon reflected on a friend's face, a Koran or a turquoise (Kunz 1913, page 111).
- **Apache tradition.** **Tradition.** A medicine man needed a turquoise to be properly recognized (Kunz 1913, pages 113 to 114).
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** In modern crystal guides, turquoise is a Classic stone for [protection](/crystals-for/protection/). It is also the December birthstone on the modern US list.

## How to Use Golden Hills Turquoise in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** Turquoise is porous, so the most useful habits around it are the ones that keep it from soaking up whatever touches it.

### Last On, First Off

**Try This.** Put your turquoise ring or bracelet on after lotion, perfume and hairspray, and take it off before washing your hands or doing the dishes. Wipe it with a damp cloth when it comes off at night. That keeps skin oils and cosmetics, which turquoise can absorb, from sitting on the stone.

**Safety.** Small beads and chips are a choking hazard. Keep them away from small children and pets.

## Is My Golden Hills Turquoise Real?

Two questions hide here. Is it turquoise, and is it what the seller says it is? We use the same five labels on every stone, and treated is not fake. Treatments should be disclosed, though. The FTC's Jewelry Guides say a seller must disclose a treatment that is not permanent, creates special care needs or significantly affects the stone's value.

- **Natural, untreated.** Uncommon for turquoise in general. Only about 15 percent of the world's turquoise supply is natural, according to the US Indian Arts and Crafts Board, since most rough is too soft to cut or polish without help.
- **Natural, treated.** Turquoise is often stabilized with colorless resin, oiled or waxed to darken it, dyed, or pressed from chips and epoxy into "block" material. Any of these can sit under a mine name.
- **Lab-grown.** Synthetic turquoise exists, and Russia and China have made synthetic turquoise and simulants since the 1970s.
- **Imitation.** Dyed howlite and dyed magnesite are the usual stand-ins, along with plastic, glass and ceramic. See [howlite vs turquoise](/compare/howlite-vs-turquoise/).
- **Trade name.** Golden Hills is one. It names a claimed source and a look, not a tested material.

### How to Check It at Home

1. **Look for pooled color.** With a 10x loupe, check cracks, pits and the edges of the matrix. In dyed turquoise, color can gather in cavities and cracks.
2. **Look at the back, the drill holes and any chip.** Dye on howlite and magnesite usually does not go deep, so a white or pale interior showing through is a warning sign.
3. **Be wary of perfect color.** A wonderfully blue, very uniform color calls for caution, since dyed howlite and magnesite are everywhere in the bead market.
4. **Leave acetone to a gemologist.** Acetone lifts shoe polish dye from the webbing, but it is a destructive test, a last resort, and it goes against the No Chemicals rule for turquoise.

The limit is honest. Many treatments are confirmed only in a lab, with tools such as infrared spectroscopy, and a gemologist's refractometer separates turquoise (refractive index 1.61 to 1.65) from howlite, magnesite and variscite. No home check confirms a mine either. Some connoisseurs can tell the mine of a cut stone from its color and matrix, but the variation between stones is enormous.

### What to Ask the Seller

1. Which mine did this come from, and how do you know?
2. Is it natural, stabilized, dyed or blocked?
3. Has a gem lab tested this material, and can I see the report?

New Mexico's Indian Arts and Crafts Sales Act requires sellers of Indian arts and crafts to disclose the type of materials in writing, and the federal Indian Arts and Crafts Board recommends asking for the mine name and the treatment on every receipt. Keep that receipt with the piece. The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) turns a tag into questions like these.

## How to Care for Golden Hills Turquoise

**Mineral Fact.** Care for it as turquoise, which is also the cautious choice for a porous stone of uncertain makeup. Liquids that turquoise absorbs, from sweat to cleaning products, can damage it or change its color.

### How to Clean It

1. Wipe it with a soft cloth dampened with plain water.
2. If it needs more, use a very mild soap solution on the cloth, then wipe again with plain water. The International Gem Society says to skip soap entirely, so start with water.
3. Dry it at once and store it in a box, away from bright light and heat.

- **Water and soaking.** Brief only, and No Soaking.
- **Sun.** Brief only.
- **Heat.** Keep Away From Heat, including a jeweler's torch.
- **Chemicals.** No Chemicals. Keep cosmetics and household cleaners off it.
- **Cleaning machines.** No Ultrasonic or Steam.
- **Storage.** Handle Gently. It scratches easily, so keep it apart from other jewelry.
- **Salt and toxicity.** Not Enough Data. Turquoise contains copper, so the cautious choice is no salt cleanse, no elixirs, and washing your hands after handling rough.

## Price Guide

Natural turquoise is valued for the rarity of its color, its clarity and matrix, and its mine, and the reputation of the mine adds to the price. Stabilized turquoise should sell for less than untreated turquoise of similar quality, and dyed turquoise is always worth less than untreated material.

For Golden Hills, the price you pay should match what the seller will put in writing. A mine name with no written treatment disclosure is worth no more to you than plain turquoise.
