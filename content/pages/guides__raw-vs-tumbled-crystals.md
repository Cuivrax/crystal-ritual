# Raw or Tumbled Crystals & Which One to Buy

Raw crystals are stones left unpolished, as natural points, clusters or rough chunks, while tumbled stones are the same materials rolled in a barrel with grit and water for days and weeks until they come out smooth, rounded and shiny. Tumbling changes the shape and the surface, not the mineral, so hardness, care and safety stay the same. Pick tumbled for a pocket or a bowl you handle, and raw for a shelf where crystal faces and texture can show.

## Raw vs Tumbled at a Glance

| | Raw | Tumbled |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Unpolished points, clusters and rough chunks | Small, rounded, brightly polished pieces |
| Edges | Sharp points, edges and crystal tips | Points and edges worn off in the barrel |
| Mineral, hardness, care ratings | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| Best for | Display, collecting, home identification tests | Pockets, palms, bowls, starter sets |
| Clues it keeps | Crystal shape, color zoning, a rough spot to test | A polished window into clear stones |
| Common tricks | Heated amethyst sold as citrine geodes, copied moldavite texture | Dye, oil, wax, glass and man-made look-alikes |
| Price | Follows the stone and its size | Follows the stone and its size |

## What Tumbling Does to a Stone

**Mineral Fact.** Tumbled stones are small, rounded, brightly polished pieces of rocks and minerals, also known as polished stones, tumbled gems or baroque gems. A rotary tumbler turns a barrel holding the stones, abrasive grit and water for days and weeks at a time. In the first step the grit gets caught between the rocks and wears off sharp points and edges. Two more steps with finer silicon carbide smooth the surfaces, and a final polish such as micron-size aluminum oxide leaves the bright, lustrous finish. The first stages take about a week each and the whole process about a month, although the actual work is only a couple of hours.

Not every stone suits the barrel. The most popular tumbling materials have a Mohs hardness between 5 and 8, are durable and take a good polish, and quartz, agate and jasper are favorites. Agate is the most popular rough for producing tumbled stones. Every stone in a barrel needs the same hardness, because harder stones will scratch softer ones.

Polish also shows what was there all along, good and bad. It can reveal grain and pattern, as in tumbled petrified wood, and it shows flaws. Aventurine with coarse mica flakes comes out pitted, because the mica tends to pluck out during polishing. A few small pits on a tumbled aventurine are normal, not damage.

### Tumbling Copies What Rivers Do

Nature runs the same process. Miners recover many spinels from stream gravels, where, in the International Gem Society's words, "the rough-and-tumble action of the stream smoothed these gem crystals into rounded shapes and removed much of the included and fractured parts."

**Tradition.** People have always picked up the results. George Frederick Kunz called it "pebble-mania," "an inherent trait in all mankind," and described Redondo Beach pebbles in California, some of which were placed "in a bottle of water to bring out the beauty of their colors" (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, pages 19 to 20 and 30). Most ancient Mexican jadeite beads, he wrote, "appear to have been rounded pebbles of this material, assorted as to size and drilled" for necklaces (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, page 252).

### What Tumbling Does Not Change

A tumbled amethyst is still quartz with a hardness of 7, and its color can still fade with prolonged exposure to intense light, just as a raw cluster's can. Selenite is gypsum, both water soluble and very heat sensitive, whatever its shape. That is why the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/) rates stones, not shapes.

What a tumbled stone can pick up is a treatment. Many manufacturers treat tumbled stones with heat, dye, oil or wax, and oil and wax can conceal fractures, hide a bad polishing job or make a smooth rough stone look like it has been polished. Waxes and oils usually wear off with handling, water, soap or sunlight, and dyed stones are often not colorfast. On our ladder a dyed or waxed tumble is Natural, treated. That is not fake, but the seller should say so.

## Which Lasts Longer, Raw or Tumbled?

For hard stones, a tumbled piece takes daily handling better, because the points and thin edges that chip are gone. For soft or fragile stones, neither form is tough, and the care ratings are the same either way.

- **Raw points and clusters.** Crystal tips and edges are the parts that knock against each other in a bowl or a bag. Store them apart, and keep amethyst away from harder gems to avoid scratches.
- **Stones with cleavage.** Labradorite has perfect cleavage in two directions, which makes it prone to breaking on impact. A tumbled labradorite dropped on tile can still break.
- **Soft stones.** Selenite is about 2 on the Mohs scale, calcite 3 and fluorite 4, all below the 5 to 8 range of the most popular tumbling materials. The Care Checker marks selenite and fluorite Handle Gently for scratches and storage, and orange calcite Brief Only. Keep them out of pockets in either form.
- **Cleaning.** A smooth tumble rinses clean fast if its stone is rated OK for water. A cluster holds dust between its crystals, and a soft brush with warm water and mild detergent, the standard method for amethyst, reaches the gaps.
- **Toxicity.** The toxicity rating follows the mineral, not the finish. Bumblebee jasper is marked Handle With Care, and a retailer says it contains arsenic and sulfur minerals, and that no one should ingest it or let children mouth it, so a raw piece or a chipped tumble belongs on a high shelf. Malachite is rated Brief Only. Its grinding dust is toxic, though finished pieces should be safe to handle.

**Safety.** Small tumbled stones are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

## Which Form Suits Which Use

| Use | Pick | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A pocket or a [worry stone](/shapes/worry-stone/) | Tumbled | No points or edges to chip or poke |
| Holding during a routine | Tumbled | Smooth and comfortable in the hand |
| A shelf or a display | Raw | Crystal faces and texture show |
| Learning to identify stones | Raw | A rough spot for scratch and streak tests |
| A [crystal grid](/shapes/crystal-grid/) | Both | Points around the outside, tumbles in a ring |
| A starter set or a gift | Tumbled | Common stones are cheap and easy to handle |
| Selenite, calcite and other soft stones | Raw, on a shelf | Soft in any form, so handle them little |

Scratch testing is advised only as a last resort and never on a finished gem, and a streak test rubs powder off a rough corner onto unglazed porcelain. Both need a raw piece. Our [crystal identification guide](/identify/crystal-identification-guide/) walks through them in order.

## Are Fakes Easier to Spot Raw or Tumbled?

Neither form is safe from fakes. Each hides different clues, so ask which clues you can still check.

**What tumbling takes away.** The crystal shape goes, and so does the base of the crystal. That matters for citrine, because heated amethyst keeps its color concentrated at the top of each crystal and weaker at the base, a clue a tumbled stone no longer has. The rough spot for a scratch or streak test goes too, and oil or wax can hide fractures. Some common imitations come smooth and polished. Goldstone is a man-made aventurine simulant produced by mixing fine metal particles into a transparent glass, and "magnetic hematite" contains no hematite at all.

**Tradition.** Cutting and polish have helped disguise stones for a long time. Kunz described white quartz pebbles from US beaches that turned translucent once they were cut and were then passed off as moonstones (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, pages 19 to 20).

**What raw does not protect you from.** Almost all citrine geodes are discovered with amethyst inside, but are heat treated to turn them orange-brown. Fakers copy moldavite's etched skin too, and *Gems & Gemology* reports that an almost identical surface "can be created artificially, likely in hydrofluoric acid." Dye works on any form, and most of the spectacularly colored agates sold in the gem trade have been dyed.

**What a polish helps with.** On a clear stone, a polished face lets you look inside. Glass simulants of opal typically contain glass bubbles and swirl marks.

Whatever the form, ask the seller whether the stone is dyed, heated, waxed or coated, where it was mined, and whether it could be glass or a man-made material. The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) turns shop labels into plain answers, and [how to tell if a crystal is real](/real-or-fake/how-to-tell-if-crystals-are-real/) covers the checks stone by stone.

## Do Raw Crystals Have Stronger Energy?

That is a belief, and crystal sellers do not agree on it.

**Modern Association.** One shop writes that "Tumbled Flint has a gentler energy compared to the rough stones, more like a warm coal rather than a hot spark." A Spanish crystal shop recommends a tumbled milky quartz for the nightstand because its energy is softer. A crystal blog takes the other side and says a rough tumbled sapphire still carries the stone's power.

The honest note. Energy in this sense is not something geology measures, and the mineral is the same in both forms. Geology.com says of tumbled stones used in healing that "traditional medical research has not proven that any cure or healing occurs beyond a placebo effect," and warns that some people use them "instead of seeking genuine medical care."

**What Research Says.** We know of no study that compared raw and tumbled stones. In a 2025 randomized study of 138 adults, some who believed in crystal healing and some who did not, people used either a real rose quartz or a visually matched glass imitation for 14 days, and anxiety scores fell only among the believers, with the real stone doing no better than the glass ([Escolà-Gascón and colleagues, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1092852925100515)). It was one trial in adults whose anxiety was low to begin with, and it did not compare raw and tumbled forms. Our reading, which goes beyond the study, is that the finish of a stone matters less than what you do with it.

**Try This.** Hold a raw point and a tumbled piece of the same stone for a minute each, then keep the one your hand goes back to. If it will live in a pocket, the tumble wins on comfort alone.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Raw or tumbled, a stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If anxiety, low mood or a health worry lasts, talk to a doctor or a therapist, and never use a stone in place of treatment. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/), any time, day or night, in the United States. In an emergency, call 911.

## Are Raw Crystals Cheaper Than Tumbled?

Not reliably. For common stones both forms are cheap, and in the listings we found, the stone and its size moved the price more than the finish did.

Single tumbled stones of common materials cost little, and each figure here is one listing we found, about $1 for a rose quartz about 3/4 inch across, $3 for a red jasper about an inch across and $7 for a clear quartz of 3/4 to 1 1/4 inches. Raw chunks of a common stone were just as cheap, about $2 for an orange calcite chunk about 1.5 inches wide and $4 for one 1.5 to 2 inches across. A rarer stone costs more in either form. A tumbled pink opal of 3/4 to 1 inch was $10 in one listing, and raw citrine sold as natural and unheated ran about $30 for a 31 to 40 gram piece to about $59 for one about 2.3 inches long. Apart from the citrine range, these are single listings, not typical prices. The word natural on those citrine listings is the seller's claim. Prices from US-dollar listings at online crystal shops in November 2026.

Past a small piece, the work counts. The International Gem Society says of agate that "labor and artistry play a more significant role in pricing than the raw material's inherent value." Tumbling is light on labor, about a month in the barrel for only a couple of hours of actual work. Tumbled stones can be bought by the pound at prices that just about everyone can afford.

## Can You Tumble Raw Crystals Yourself?

Yes. Tumbling is the simplest and least expensive lapidary art to learn, and the only equipment you need is a tumbler and abrasives, often sold in kits, and the rough you want to polish. Load stones of one hardness, expect about a week per stage, and follow two warnings. Rinse stones outside, not in the sink, because tumbler waste can plug up a drain quickly, and open the barrel daily, because gas can build up and could blow the top off the barrel.

Leave anything the Care Checker flags for toxicity out of the barrel, since tumbling grinds stone away, and grinding dust is the hazard flagged for malachite. Even quartz, safe to handle, is a different matter as dust. The International Gem Society rates cutting and grinding quartz a high risk, with silicosis as the hazard, and says lapidary work needs a respirator and wet methods.

A tumbler also rounds off points and edges for good, so if a raw crystal has faces you love, keep it raw.
