# Pink Tourmaline Meaning, Properties & Real-Life Uses

Pink tourmaline is the pink color variety of tourmaline, a group of boron silicate minerals that rates 7 to 7.5 on the Mohs hardness scale. Modern crystal guides treat it as a love stone tied to the heart chakra, gentleness and self-love. Those meanings are modern beliefs. The stone's documented history takes in California mines and Chinese wedding swords.

| Mineral group | Tourmaline supergroup, more than 30 species. Most cut tourmaline is elbaite |

> **Is It Real? Quick Checks.** On a raw crystal, look for fine lines running along its length and a rounded triangle shape when you look at the end. Tourmaline has no obvious cleavage, so a piece that breaks cleanly along flat planes is probably something else. Ask the seller whether the color was treated. These checks can rule some stones out, but only a gem lab can confirm what you have. Heat and irradiation are common, often undetectable color treatments for tourmaline, while imitations are only occasionally seen ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)).

## What Pink Tourmaline Actually Is

**The Stone.** Tourmaline is not one mineral. It is a supergroup of more than 30 species that share the same trigonal crystal structure but have different chemical formulas, according to the [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tourmaline-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/). In 1890 the Victorian writer John Ruskin said its chemistry was "more like a medieval doctor's prescription than the making of a respectable mineral." The International Gem Society says the line still holds.

Pink is a color, not a species. Most cut tourmaline belongs to the species elbaite, and deep pink to red stones usually do too, though some turn out to be liddicoatite or olenite. A rare species called rossmanite also comes in pink to tan. For practical purposes gemologists simply call all of them tourmaline, and naming the exact species can take an advanced lab test called LA-ICP-MS ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tourmaline-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)).

The pink comes mainly from manganese in the crystal, according to the dealer guide at [Gem Rock Auctions](https://www.gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/pink-tourmaline). [GIA](https://www.gia.edu/tourmaline/gem-overview) confirms that manganese produces tourmaline's pinks, and adds that radiation, natural or lab-induced, might also color some pink stones.

### Where It Grows

Many tourmaline crystals grow in pegmatites, very coarse igneous rocks that form as a body of magma finishes cooling, where boron-rich fluids feed crystal growth ([Gem Rock Auctions](https://www.gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/pink-tourmaline)). The most spectacular crystals form when hot water and vapor carry the right elements into open pockets and fractures, says [Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml). Those pockets hold anything from crystals a millimeter long to prisms of more than 220 pounds (100 kg).

A single crystal can change color as it grows. [Watermelon tourmaline](/stones/watermelon-tourmaline/) has a pink or red core inside a green rind, so a thin slice looks like the fruit ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tourmaline-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)).

### Where It Comes From

Pink tourmaline comes from Brazil (especially Minas Gerais), Afghanistan, Pakistan, Madagascar, Mozambique and the United States, according to [Gem Rock Auctions](https://www.gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/pink-tourmaline). Brazil has been the world's leading source of tourmaline for nearly 500 years ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)).

The American chapter is a good one. The first commercial gemstone mine in the United States followed an 1821 tourmaline find near Paris, Maine, and Maine has produced pink and green tourmaline from dozens of localities since. Southern California, mostly Riverside and San Diego Counties, became the most important US source. Tons of red tourmaline were shipped from there to China for snuff bottles, carvings and jewelry, most of it more than 100 years ago ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)).

### A Stone That Holds a Charge

Tourmaline is piezoelectric, which means it produces electricity under pressure. It is also pyroelectric, so it produces electricity when heated, and it picks up a static charge when rubbed ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tourmaline-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). Keep that in mind for the ash story below.

### Pink Tourmaline or Rubellite?

Rubellite is a trade term, not a separate mineral. The International Gem Society uses it for reasonably saturated dark pink to red tourmaline in medium to dark tones. Lighter stones are usually sold as pink tourmaline, but the line is not strict, and two sellers can give the same stone different names ([Gem Rock Auctions](https://www.gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/pink-tourmaline)). Judge the stone in front of you by its color, clarity and cut, not by the word on the tag. More on the darker stones on our [rubellite page](/stones/rubellite/).

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** Pink tourmaline's meaning has changed more than the stone has. Here is what each era actually left in writing.

- **1500s, Brazil.** Portuguese explorers sent green and blue tourmaline home to Portugal thinking it was emerald and sapphire. Tourmaline was not recognized as its own mineral until 1793 ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)).
- **1700s, Europe.** **Tradition.** The first tourmalines to reach modern Europe came to Holland from Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, and the word tourmaline comes from turmali, the stone's name there. Dutch lapidaries called it Aschentrekker, or "Ash-Attractor," and the gem expert George Frederick Kunz favors the story that jewelers testing its hardness in the fire saw it attract or repel the ashes. The earliest printed note of its electric pull that Kunz knew of dates to 1707 (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, pages 52 to 56). That party trick is the pyroelectric effect, a real property of the mineral.
- **1915, China.** **Tradition.** Kunz describes the Chinese marriage sword carried by the priest at important weddings, 12 or 13 inches long, its sheath studded with pink stones cut as smooth domes. The favorites were "pink tourmaline, rubellite from the Shan Mountains, or rose-quartz." The groom holds the sword, the bride holds the sheath, and as the ring goes on her finger the two are brought together (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, pages 384 to 385).
- **1915, China.** Kunz adds an honest detail. The pink of these stones was often deepened with pink paste or foil set beneath them, and sometimes by dipping them in a pink aniline dye (same pages). Boosted pink is not a new trick.
- **1915, fashion.** Kunz notes a fashion fad of matching jewelry to the color of a gown, with pink tourmaline among the suggested stones (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, page 407).
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** The love and heart chakra meanings come from modern crystal books. One large crystal encyclopedia credits its pink tourmaline entry to the crystal authors Simmons, Raphaell and Melody, whose books include *The Book of Stones* (Robert Simmons with Naisha Ahsian, 2005), *Crystal Enlightenment* (Katrina Raphaell, 1985) and *Love Is in the Earth* (Melody, 1991).

### What About Roman & Egyptian Legends?

You will find pages saying the Romans used tourmaline for calm sleep, or that an Egyptian legend has it passing over a rainbow and taking all its colors. We found no old text behind either story. Since tourmaline was not recognized as its own mineral until 1793, and the Portuguese who found it in Brazil took it for emerald and sapphire, an ancient tourmaline tradition is hard to pin down.

## What People Use Pink Tourmaline for Today

**Modern Association.** In modern crystal guides, two links show up everywhere, love and the heart chakra. Self-love, calm and courage come right after. A smaller group of guides also ties it to the crown chakra. The love theme leans gentle rather than fiery, and one dealer guide describes it as a reminder to treat yourself with the same patience you would offer someone you care about ([Gem Rock Auctions](https://www.gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/pink-tourmaline)). Darker raspberry stones and rubellite get more passion and courage in some guides.

Most of the "why" is color. Pink is widely read as affection and gentleness, as the Gem Rock Auctions guide points out, and the chakra charts in modern crystal guides give the heart green as its main color with pink as its partner.

Crystal guides also mention real-life moments. People give it to mark a new relationship. Some keep it close through mourning, a separation or another tender stretch. Others keep it on a desk where frustration builds, or hold it before a hard conversation or a first date. It is often paired with [rose quartz](/stones/rose-quartz/) for a double dose of gentleness, or with black tourmaline, which is believed to protect while the pink one stays soft.

Some associations are rare and you can skip them, like lucky numbers, a feng shui "fame corner" or the claim that tourmaline did not form on Earth. That last one we can check, and the pegmatites of Maine and Minas Gerais say otherwise.

A few sites go much further and list heart, skin or hormone conditions. Nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports those claims, and even Gem Rock Auctions, which sells the stone, states that there is no scientific evidence it can treat or cure medical conditions.

## How to Use Pink Tourmaline in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** Whatever you believe about the stone, here is what the ritual does for sure. It gives you a pause you chose, at a moment you picked, with something in your hand to mark it.

### The Patience Stone on Your Desk

**Try This.** Put a tumbled pink tourmaline next to your keyboard. Before you answer the message that annoyed you, pick it up and let one slow breath out. Then answer. The stone is the cue, the pause is the point.

### Before a Hard Conversation

**Try This.** Keep it in your pocket on the day of the talk. Before you walk in, hold it and decide two things, the one sentence you need the other person to hear and the one thing you will listen for. Put it back in your pocket and go.

### A Gift for a New Chapter

**Try This.** For a new couple or a friend after a breakup, wrap a tumbled stone with a short card. Write what it is (a tourmaline, grown in a mineral pocket deep in the rock) and what people associate with it (gentleness, including toward yourself). The card does half the work.

**Safety.** Small tumbled stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

### An Evening Note

**Try This.** Keep a notebook and the stone on your nightstand. Before lights out, write one line about something you handled kindly today, even if the kind thing was stopping. Set the stone on the closed notebook.

**What Research Says.** In an exploratory remote randomized study of 108 adults, Melis Yilmaz Balban and colleagues found that five minutes a day of breathing exercises, especially cyclic sighing with its long exhales, improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than the same time spent in mindfulness meditation ([DOI 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). Volunteers knew which exercise they did and rated their own mood, and no stone was tested, so the slow breath in the desk ritual is the researched part, not the tourmaline.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Pink tourmaline is often chosen for grief, heartbreak and stress. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If sadness, anxiety or trouble sleeping lasts or feels heavy, talk to a doctor or a therapist. If you are in crisis in the US, reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline by calling or texting 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org).

## Is My Pink Tourmaline Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone. Here is where pink tourmaline stands on each, and where our data still has gaps.

- **Natural, untreated.** A pink stone straight from the ground. We found no reliable figure for how much of the pink tourmaline sold in the US is untreated. For the darker rubellite end, the [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/rubellite-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/) advises assuming every stone is treated, because irradiation can turn pale pink a stable red and cannot be detected, even though some stones are untreated.
- **Natural, treated.** Kunz recorded foil, paste and dye used to deepen pink stones in 1915. Today heat and irradiation are common, often undetectable color treatments for tourmaline (Geology.com), and irradiated color may fade with heat or bright light (International Gem Society).
- **Lab-grown.** None is sold for jewelry, and "simulated tourmaline" online usually means a fake (International Gem Society).
- **Imitation.** Occasional, mostly multicolor fakes made of a colored glass or plastic wafer glued between clear glass, with a glue line a loupe can spot at the edge (Geology.com).
- **Trade name.** Rubellite is a trade term for darker stones. It describes color, not a fake.

Three checks you can do at home, with their limits.

1. **Look along a crystal.** Tourmaline crystals often show fine lines running the length of the crystal and a rounded triangle or six-sided outline from the end ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)). This works on raw crystals only. A cut or tumbled stone loses these features.
2. **Look for cleavage.** Tourmaline has indistinct cleavage, so a piece that splits cleanly into flat planes is probably not tourmaline ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)). This rules stones out, it never proves one. Color does not help, because tourmaline comes in every color.
3. **Rub it.** Tourmaline picks up a static charge when rubbed ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tourmaline-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). That is a property, not a proof, since amber attracts light bits of paper too (Kunz 1915, page 54).

Three questions to ask the seller.

1. Is this tourmaline, and has the color been treated in any way?
2. Do you call it pink tourmaline or rubellite, and why?
3. Where was it mined?

## How to Care for Pink Tourmaline

**Mineral Fact.** With a hardness of 7 to 7.5, no cleavage and only slight brittleness, tourmaline makes a good jewelry stone for everyday wear ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tourmaline-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)).

- **Water.** OK. Warm water, detergent and a soft brush, keep soaks brief ([IGS care guide](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gemstone-care-guide/)).
- **Sun.** Unknown. Irradiated color can fade in bright light (IGS), so a drawer beats a sunny sill.
- **Salt.** Unknown.
- **Heat.** Avoid. Tourmaline is moderately sensitive to heat, and bicolor crystals sometimes split at color junctures (IGS). Being pyroelectric is a property, not a risk.
- **Cleaning.** No ultrasonic or steam. The pink variety is often flawed and vulnerable to vibration damage (IGS).
- **Toxicity.** None known for tourmaline in general (IGS).

Check your exact piece, tumbled stone, bracelet or ring, in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/). It shows OK, Brief only, Avoid or Unknown for water, sun and salt, with the reason and the source.

## Pink Tourmaline vs Look-Alikes

- **Rubellite.** Same mineral, darker color, different label (see above).
- **Watermelon tourmaline.** Same mineral, with a pink core and a green rind in one crystal.
- **Rose quartz.** A different mineral, quartz, usually with a cloudy translucence ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/rose-quartz/gem-overview)). The reliable test is a gemologist's, since rose quartz has a refractive index of 1.544 to 1.553 and a specific gravity near 2.65 ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/rose-quartz/)), against 1.624 to 1.644 and about 3.06 for tourmaline (GIA). The two have shared a job before, since Kunz lists both on the same Chinese wedding swords.
- **Morganite.** The pink to orange-pink variety of beryl, often light in tone ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/morganite/gem-overview)). Color alone will not settle it. A gemologist separates them by refractive index, 1.583 to 1.590 for morganite against 1.624 to 1.644 for tourmaline, and by specific gravity, 2.80 to 2.91 against about 3.06 (GIA).

## Price Guide

The value of tourmaline varies a lot with color, clarity and origin, and because good crystals turn up worldwide, tourmaline stays generally affordable ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/tourmaline-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). Prices typically exceed a few hundred dollars per carat only for large sizes or exceptionally rare colors (International Gem Society). At the top end, specimens with attractive color and crystal form can sell for thousands of dollars ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/tourmaline.shtml)).

For a pink stone, GIA's quality factors come down to color intensity and tone, clarity, cut and size. Pink to red tourmaline often shows more visible inclusions than green or blue stones, long crystals lead cutters to slender shapes, and how much size adds to value varies with the variety ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/tourmaline/gem-overview)). Treatment matters too, since heat and irradiation are common and often undetectable (Geology.com), so ask about it in writing. We give no price figures here, since the current listings we found did not make clear whether prices were per stone or per carat.
