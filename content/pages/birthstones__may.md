# May Birthstone Meaning, History & Honest Buying Tips

The May birthstone is emerald, the green variety of the mineral beryl and the only May stone on the modern US list from the American Gem Society. Older European lists also gave May agate, and some charts add chrysoprase as an alternate. The honest note is that most emeralds sold today have oil or resin in their cracks, which decides how you should wear and clean one.

## What Is the May Birthstone?

Emerald, alone, on both the American Gem Society list and the US jewelers' list adopted in Kansas City in August 1912 (Kunz 1913, pages 317 and 319). Older lists tell a different story.

### Does May Have Two Birthstones?

Not on the US list. Agate is the traditional second stone, from older European lists. Chrysoprase is an alternate on some charts, such as the Burke Museum's, but not on the 1912 or AGS lists. So when a jeweler says "traditional," ask which list, since 1912 means emerald alone and the older lists name agate at least as often. Every month is on our [birthstone chart](/birthstones/).

## How Emerald Got May

**Tradition.** The month lists follow the twelve foundation stones in Revelation, the first for March, the second for April and the third for May (Kunz 1913, pages 307 to 308). The third is chalcedony, the family agate belongs to. The fourth is emerald, so emerald began as June's stone (pages 311 to 313).

Kunz's tables still show it. Emerald is June's stone on four of his old lists, and across all eight, agate turns up for May five times and emerald four (pages 315 to 316). Later European usage put emerald first, and the 1912 list kept it alone.

In his calendar of the months, Kunz gave May the emerald, the hawthorn and a verse. "Who first beholds the light of day / In spring's sweet flow'ry month of May, / And wears an emerald all her life, / Shall be a loved and happy wife" (page 328).

## Emerald, the Modern May Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Emerald](/stones/emerald/) is the bluish green to green variety of beryl, the mineral of aquamarine. Chromium, vanadium or both make it green, and iron pushes it toward blue. It rates 7.5 to 8 on the [Mohs scale](/identify/mohs-hardness-scale-for-crystals/), which ranks scratch resistance from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond). Most emeralds now come from Colombia, Zambia, Brazil, Ethiopia and Zimbabwe, and Tiffany & Co. and later owners mined them in western North Carolina from 1894 to the 1990s.

Where green ends is debated. Labs and dealers call a stone [green beryl](/stones/green-beryl/) when it is too light for emerald, but they disagree on what too light means, and the name moves the price.

### Why Are Most Emeralds Oiled?

Almost every emerald has cracks or inclusions you can see without magnification, the "garden" dealers call jardin. Cracks that reach the surface are usually filled with colorless oil, wax or resin to hide them, and GIA cites estimates that 90 percent or more of emeralds are fracture-filled.

The trick is old. Andreas, bishop of Caesarea, whom Kunz places in the 10th century, wrote that the emerald "is nourished with oil, that its transparency and beauty may not change" (Kunz 1913, pages 311 to 312).

- **Natural fillers,** such as cedarwood oil, mineral oil and Canada balsam, are traditional. They can dry out over the years and leave milky spots in the cracks.
- **Resins,** such as epoxy, are more stable, though much of the trade still prefers oil because it counts as natural.
- **No filler** is rare. Some Ethiopian emeralds, mined since 2016, need no oil.

Fillers improve the look but often do nothing for strength. Labs grade the filling None, Insignificant, Minor, Moderate or Significant. Oil or resin, the home rules are the same. Heat, steam, ultrasonic cleaners and solvents can pull filler out, and then the cracks show again.

**The Story.**

- **Antiquity to the Middle Ages.** **Tradition.** Emerald was believed to foretell the future, defeat enchantments and strengthen its owner's memory (Kunz 1913, pages 76 to 78).
- **13th century.** **Tradition.** Albertus Magnus told of King Bela IV of Hungary, whose emerald ring broke in three when he embraced his wife, the stone being thought hostile to passion (page 78).

## Agate, the Traditional May Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Agate](/stones/agate/) is banded chalcedony, a microcrystalline quartz that grows layer by layer in cavities of igneous rock. It rates 6.5 to 7 and takes a fine polish. Dyeing is ancient and common, so the brightest colors are usually dyed.

**Tradition.** "Achates was a stone formerly in high esteem, but now held in none," Pliny the Elder wrote (*Natural History*, book 37, chapter 54). In the 11th century Marbodus, bishop of Rennes, said agates made their wearers agreeable and persuasive (Kunz 1913, page 51).

## Chrysoprase, an Alternate May Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Chrysoprase](/stones/chrysoprase/) is chalcedony colored apple green by nickel, mostly from Australia. It rates 6.5 to 7 with no cleavage, the tendency to split along flat planes, and is often called "Australian jade." Some chrysoprase fades in sun or heat, especially dyed and European stones, while untreated Australian ones hold their color.

**Tradition.** A nine-inch "emerald" in the chapel of St. Wenceslaus in Prague may have been chrysoprase, Kunz thought, since the chapel walls are inlaid with it (1913, pages 259 to 260).

## What May Birthstones Mean Today

**Modern Association.** Modern crystal guides put emerald on the heart chakra, with love and loyalty as its themes, and our rankings rate it Widely Linked for love and for calm. The love link is old, since Kunz notes emerald was commonly assigned to Venus (1913, page 78). Agate is a Classic stone for protection, and chrysoprase is Widely Linked for love, money and focus.

May splits between [Taurus](/zodiac/taurus/), until about May 20, and [Gemini](/zodiac/gemini/). Kunz's 1913 verses give Taurus the sapphire and Gemini the agate (page 346).

### What Is the May Birthstone Color?

Green, from emerald's bluish green to slightly yellowish green, with chrysoprase a brighter apple green. Some sites say emerald heals the eyes or the heart. Nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports those claims.

## How to Choose a May Birthstone

Hardness is resistance to scratching, and toughness is resistance to chipping. Emerald is hard, but its cracks make it chip more easily than its hardness suggests.

- **For every day.** Agate and chrysoprase are tough. An emerald is safest in earrings or a pendant. For an emerald ring, choose a bezel, a metal rim that guards the edge, and take it off for chores, the gym and the garden.
- **For a baby's first piece.** Buy it to keep, not to wear, since small stones are a choking hazard.
- **For an anniversary.** Emerald is the gem of the 20th and 35th wedding anniversaries.
- **For an inherited ring.** Assume it is oiled and keep it out of ultrasonic cleaners.
- **For a tight budget.** Green beryl, chrysoprase or agate. One listing we found priced a tumbled agate about half an inch across at $3 in November 2026.

**What drives an emerald's price.** Color first, with bluish green to pure green and vivid saturation at the top. Then clarity, where some garden is expected, and the enhancement grade, since a Significant stone is worth far less than a Minor one. Because emerald is less dense, a one-carat emerald looks bigger than a one-carat diamond.

### What to Ask Before You Buy

1. **Mined or lab-grown?** Under the FTC's Jewelry Guides, a lab-made emerald needs a word like "laboratory-grown," "laboratory-created" or "[maker]-created" right before its name, and never "natural" or "genuine." Get the answer on the receipt.
2. **Treated with what, and how much?** The same guides say a treatment that is not permanent or needs special care should be disclosed, and emerald fillers can dry out or seep.
3. **Is there a lab report?** Ask for the enhancement grade in writing.
4. **Can I look with a 10x loupe?** Expect inclusions. A big, flawless, cheap "emerald" is a warning sign.
5. **How do I care for it?** Ask for the care steps in writing.

## Is My May Birthstone Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone.

- **Natural, untreated.** Rare.
- **Natural, treated.** The usual emerald, filled with oil, wax or resin. Treated is not fake.
- **Lab-grown.** Grown by hydrothermal or flux methods, under maker names like Chatham and Gilson. Newer ones have natural-looking inclusions, so only a lab can tell.
- **Imitation.** Glass, YAG, green cubic zirconia, synthetic spinel and beryl triplets, layers glued together. Check a triplet from the side for a join line.
- **Trade name.** "African emerald" has meant green fluorite, and the FTC calls "yellow emerald" an incorrect name for golden beryl or heliodor. Many of the great "emeralds" of European cathedral treasuries, like the Three Magi treasury at Cologne, are really peridots (Kunz 1913, page 66). See [peridot vs emerald](/compare/peridot-vs-emerald/).

Agate's issue is undisclosed dye. Chrysoprase's is dyed green chalcedony sold under its name, often suspiciously even in color, so ask "is this dyed?" in writing.

## How to Use a May Birthstone

**Try This.** Medieval owners trusted emerald for memory. Before a toast or an interview, hold a green stone, breathe out slowly and say your first line aloud. The rehearsal does the work.

**Try This.** For a May birthday card, copy Kunz's verse and add the moon phase of the birth night from our [Birthday Moon](/moon/birthday-moon/) tool.

**Safety.** Small stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Emerald is often chosen for love and calm, and old lore promised a sharper memory. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If anxiety or low mood lasts, talk to a doctor or a therapist. ### How to Clean an Emerald at Home

1. **After you wear it, wipe it with a soft cloth dampened in lukewarm water.** For a stone graded Moderate or Significant, or of unknown grade, this is the only cleaning.
2. **Only when it looks dull, wash it.** Fill a small bowl with lukewarm water, not hot, and add one drop of mild dish soap.
3. **Brush gently with a soft brush,** such as a baby toothbrush, for a few seconds around and under the stone. Never leave it to soak.
4. **Rinse in a second bowl of lukewarm water,** never under a tap over an open drain.
5. **Pat dry with a lint-free cloth** and let it air dry before you put it away.

Sources differ on soap. GIA calls warm, soapy water the safest way to clean emeralds, while the International Gem Society's emerald enhancement guide says to avoid soapy water. That is why the damp cloth comes first.

### What an Emerald Should Never Meet

- **Ultrasonic cleaners,** which can weaken already fractured stones.
- **Steam and heat,** which can sweat out oil or extend cracks. Tell any jeweler it is a filled emerald before a repair or resizing.
- **Acetone, nail polish remover and alcohol,** which dissolve oils and some resins.
- **Lotion, perfume and cleaners.** Light and chemicals can affect fillers, so put the ring on last.

### How to Store It & What If It Looks Cloudy

Give each piece its own soft pouch, away from a sunny window. Diamonds and sapphires can scratch emerald, and emerald can scratch agate and chrysoprase.

If milky spots appear in the cracks, do not fix them at home. Dealers commonly re-treat emeralds, so ask a jeweler who works with them and get any re-treatment in writing. Any emerald can be refilled after its lab report, so an old report may not match the stone.

**Where the beliefs and the care data disagree.** Some crystal guides cleanse emerald under running water or in the sun. Our data keeps water Brief Only, has no sun rating and keeps chrysoprase out of the sun, so use moonlight or sound and bring the stones in before morning.
