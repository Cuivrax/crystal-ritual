# Crystal Care Checker for Water, Sun & Salt

The Crystal Care Checker answers the crystal care question people ask most, whether a stone can take a rinse, a soak, sunlight or salt before you cleanse it. Pick up to six stones and a method, and each stone gets OK, Brief Only, Avoid or Not Enough Data, with the reason and the source behind it. It looks at physical care only, never at what a method does for a stone's energy.

## How the Checker Works

1. **Choose your stones.** Type a name, or another name the stone goes by. "Tiger eye" finds tiger's eye, "rock crystal" finds clear quartz and "gypsum" finds selenite. You can add up to six stones, or tap one of the popular picks under the search box.
2. **Choose a method.** The first group is the contact methods, Rinse Under the Tap, Soak in Water, Leave in Sunlight and Bury in Salt or Salt Water. The second group is the dry methods, Moonlight, Sound, Smoke, Selenite Plate and Brown Rice.
3. **Read your results.** A short summary covers all your stones at once, then each stone gets its own card with the verdict, the reason in one or two sentences and links to the pages that reason comes from.

If a stone comes back Avoid or Not Enough Data, a Try Moonlight Instead button switches you to a dry method in one tap. Save as Image draws a tall care card with your stones and verdicts, handy to keep on your phone next to your shelf. The page address updates as you choose, so you can bookmark a result or send it to a friend who just got her first selenite lamp.

## What the Ratings Mean

| Rating | What it means |
|---|---|
| OK | Our sources say this kind of contact is fine |
| Brief Only | Our sources allow short contact only, so keep it quick |
| Avoid | Our sources warn against it |
| Not Enough Data | Our sources say nothing yet. Until they do, a dry method like moonlight or sound is the gentle choice |

The word always shows next to the color, so the rating reads the same on any screen and to anyone who cannot tell the colors apart.

Not Enough Data is an honest answer, not a warning. It means no source we trust has covered that stone and that kind of contact yet. Black tourmaline, for example, has no water rating in our sources, so the checker says so instead of guessing from its hardness or its chemistry.

## The Safety Rules Behind Every Rating

These rules decide what you see. They lean cautious on purpose, because a cautious answer costs you a few seconds and a wrong one can cost you a stone.

**Every rating rests on a quote.** Each one comes from a sentence we checked against the source page it links to. We never work a rating out from hardness or from what a stone is made of.

**Soak is Brief Only unless a source clears it.** Most reference guides describe washing a stone, warm water, mild detergent and a soft brush ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/amethyst-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)), and say nothing about leaving it in a bowl. So when a stone is OK for a rinse, the checker still shows Brief Only for a long soak, unless the source itself says soaking is fine. Today that is true for three stones, black agate, flower agate and grape agate, through a dealer's care table for agate in general ([GemSelect](https://gemselect.com/gem-info/cleaning.php)).

**A Wikipedia-only OK is not used.** Wikipedia is a useful lead, not a source we publish on its own. If Wikipedia is the only page saying a contact is fine, the rating drops to Not Enough Data. A warning that rests on Wikipedia alone stays, because caution is the safe side to err on, and the card says plainly that only Wikipedia backs it so far. Orange calcite and water is one of those ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcite)).

**A source has to fit the stone.** When the only source does not match the stone or the use, we leave the rating out. Our only sunlight source for rainbow moonstone was about orthoclase moonstone ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/moonstone-care-cleaning)), and rainbow moonstone is a labradorite. Our only salt source for clear quartz was a ritual page, not gem care advice ([Gem Rock Auctions](https://www.gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/clear-quartz)). Both show Not Enough Data.

**Dealer guides are labeled.** Some ratings come from shops that sell stones. Their links carry a small "dealer guide" tag, so you can weigh them yourself.

**Dry methods get the same line every time.** Moonlight, sound, smoke, a selenite plate and brown rice are practices people follow, and our sources document no physical contact risk for any of them. The checker shows each stone's water rating beside them for comparison, and it never says a method cleanses energy.

## Three Results Worth Knowing

- **Selenite and water.** Brief Only. Gypsum, the mineral behind selenite, is water soluble, and the International Gem Society cleans it by hand with warm water only ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gypsum-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). A quick wipe, then dry it.
- **Amethyst and sunlight.** Brief Only. GIA says some amethyst color can fade with prolonged exposure to intense light ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/amethyst-care-cleaning)). A morning on the sill is not the same as a month on it.
- **Fluorite and a soak.** Avoid. Fluorite is slightly soluble in water, so it should never be submerged ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/fluorite-jewelry-gemstone-information/)).

## What the Checker Does Not Cover

**Water you drink.** The checker rates contact with water, not whether water a stone sat in is safe to swallow, and our data clears no stone for that. The International Gem Society advises crystal healers to avoid making potions with toxic gems ([IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gemstone-toxicity-table)). Our [full water safety table](/care/crystals-that-cant-go-in-water/) lists the stones to keep out of drinking water, and [our moon water guide](/rituals/moon-water/) keeps the stone beside the jar.

**Dyed, coated or stabilized pieces.** Ratings describe the stone itself. A dye, a metal coating or a resin can change the answer, and a dyed stone should never go in water you drink. The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) helps you work out what a tag really means.

**Stones you cannot name.** If you do not know what a stone is, or the search cannot find it, treat it as Not Enough Data and keep it dry.

**Salt, mostly.** Salt is the thinnest part of our data. Gem references rarely discuss it, so most stones show Not Enough Data for salt. Sunlight is better covered, and water best of all.

Heat, chemicals, storage and toxicity live in the same care data and appear on each stone's own page. For the method by method picture, read [how to cleanse crystals without damaging them](/care/how-to-cleanse-crystals/).

**Try This.** Before your next cleansing night, put every stone you plan to use into the checker in one go and save the care card. Stick it inside the cupboard door where you keep the stones, and the question is answered before you reach for the tap.
