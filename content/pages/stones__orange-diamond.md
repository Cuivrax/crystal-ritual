# Orange Diamond Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

An orange diamond is a diamond, crystallized carbon, whose orange color comes from defects linked to nitrogen in its crystal. Diamonds of pure orange, with no second color, are among the rarest of all fancy-color diamonds. Orange is also easy to produce by lab growth and by treatment, so the report matters more than the name.

## What an Orange Diamond Actually Is

**The Stone.** A diamond is a mineral made essentially of pure carbon, crystallized in the cubic system. It comes in many colors. An orange diamond is a "fancy-color" diamond, the trade's name for diamonds with a distinct color. GIA grades natural orange diamonds from faint, very light and light orange up through fancy light, fancy, fancy intense, fancy vivid and fancy deep.

Most orange diamonds are not pure orange. They usually carry a second hue of yellow, brown or pink, and when orange contributes less than about a quarter of the color you will see the stone called "orangy." On a grading report with several hues, the last one named is the dominant color, so a "yellow-orange" diamond is mainly orange.

### What Makes a Diamond Orange?

Nitrogen, almost always. It is the most common impurity in diamond, and certain arrangements of nitrogen atoms absorb blue light. When the absorption covers only light below about 510 nanometers, the blue end of the spectrum, a diamond looks yellow. When it reaches to about 600 nanometers, taking in green light too, the diamond looks orange. Yellow and orange diamonds are close cousins, separated by a small shift in which light gets through.

In pure orange diamonds, about 86 percent owe their color to a broad absorption called the "480 nm band," named for the wavelength at its center. Scientists still do not fully understand what this defect is, and some authors have linked it to oxygen. Another 13 percent are colored by single nitrogen atoms scattered through the crystal. In 2024 GIA described two small orange diamonds whose color came from a combination of bands that appears to be a newly identified cause.

Heated to about 750 to 930 °F (400 to 500 °C), diamonds with the 480 nm band turn temporarily more orange. The same band is always present in chameleon diamonds, known for changing color.

### How Rare Is an Orange Diamond?

Very, in its pure form. Yellow is the most common fancy color, but over about a decade GIA examined several hundred thousand yellow and orange diamonds, and pure, unmodified orange made up 0.05 percent of them, fewer than 200 stones. Orange diamonds of every kind, mixed hues included, numbered more than 13,000 in the same period.

The largest share of the yellow and orange diamonds GIA sees, 40 percent, weigh one to two carats. They are most often cut as cushions or cut-cornered rectangles rather than rounds.

### Where Orange Diamonds Come From

Almost anywhere diamonds are mined. A diamond's yellow or orange color depends mostly on how long it sat at high temperature deep in the earth, not on where it later reached the surface, so GIA finds natural yellow and orange diamonds in nearly all mining areas.

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** Orange diamonds have little lore of their own, because so few were ever seen. Their story is mostly about diamonds in general and a handful of famous stones.

- **Ancient India.** **Tradition.** The gem treatise of Buddhabhatta reserved two classes of colored diamonds for kings alone, those "red as coral" and those "yellow as saffron" (Kunz, *The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, page 73). Orange is not among them.
- **Old superstition.** **Tradition.** A diamond's power was believed to vanish if it was bought, and to pass on freely when it was given as a pledge of love or friendship (Kunz 1913, page 73).
- **1882.** Edwin Streeter's book *The Great Diamonds of the World* is credited with the first use of "fire diamonds" for orange stones, a trade name still heard today.
- **1997.** The Pumpkin, a 5.54 carat Fancy Vivid orange diamond from the Central African Republic according to GIA, was bought the day before Halloween for $1.3 million. It later appeared in the Smithsonian's Splendor of Diamonds exhibition.
- **2013.** The Orange, a 14.82 carat Fancy Vivid orange of unknown origin, sold at auction for $35.5 million, about $2.4 million a carat, a record price at the time.

## What People Use Orange Diamonds for Today

**Modern Association.** One jeweler's guide says an orange diamond stands for energy and courage and suits an enthusiastic, adventurous wearer. Another describes orange as the color of vital energy, creativity, joy and renewal, and presents orange diamonds as heirloom pieces. In modern crystal guides, diamond in general is Widely Linked to confidence and money, and diamond is the April birthstone on the modern US list.

The "why" is color symbolism.

One correction is worth making. The first jeweler's guide also claims that boron gives orange diamonds their color. GIA's research ties orange to nitrogen, and boron is what makes most blue diamonds blue.

## How to Use an Orange Diamond in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** With an orange diamond, the habits that matter most are choosing it well and giving it with meaning.

### Check It Under Two Lights

**Try This.** Before you buy, look at the stone next to a window in daylight, then under a warm lamp indoors. Write down which light makes it look more yellow, more brown or more orange. Color drives the price of a fancy-color diamond, so judge it in person in good light, not from a trade name or a photo.

### Give It the Old Way

**Try This.** The old superstition said a diamond only worked when it was a gift. If you give one, for an engagement, an anniversary or an April birthday, add a card that says what it is (natural, treated or lab-grown, in plain words) and what it stands for to you.

**Safety.** Loose small stones and earring backs are a choking hazard. Keep them away from small children and pets.

## Is My Orange Diamond Real?

Three questions hide here. Is it a diamond? Did it grow in the earth or in a lab? Is its color natural or treated? We use the same five labels on every stone. The FTC's Jewelry Guides say sellers must disclose a treatment that is not permanent, needs special care or significantly affects value, and must describe a lab-grown stone with a word such as "laboratory-grown," "laboratory-created" or "[manufacturer]-created." The word "faux" is not enough.

- **Natural, untreated.** Rare and expensive. One useful fact for labs is that the 480 nm band has been seen only in natural diamonds, and no known commercial treatment or growth method creates it.
- **Natural, treated.** HPHT treatment, which squeezes and heats a diamond at high pressure and temperature, can turn a pale yellow diamond orange. Heavy irradiation followed by annealing (controlled heating) can turn most nitrogen-bearing diamonds orange to brown. These colors are stable. Cheaper treatments, a colored coating or a colored resin in cracks, are temporary and usually easy to see under magnification.
- **Lab-grown.** Most diamonds grown by the HPHT method come out yellow to orange, because nitrogen from the air gets into the growth chamber. CVD-grown diamonds, grown from carbon vapor, can be turned orange or pinkish orange by irradiation and annealing after growth. Lab-grown diamonds have the same composition, structure and optical properties as natural ones, and their color is often beautifully even.
- **Imitation.** Cubic zirconia and synthetic moissanite are made in colors and used as colored diamond substitutes. Glass comes in any color.
- **Trade names.** "Fire diamond" and "pumpkin" describe a color, not a grade. Trust the grading terms on a report instead.

### How to Check It at Home

1. **Read the report.** Look for three things, the word natural or laboratory-grown, whether the color is natural, and the full color grade. GIA's advice is that every fancy-color diamond should go to a lab for its color origin.
2. **Look for doubling with a 10x loupe.** Diamond is singly refractive and shows no doubling. Synthetic moissanite is doubly refractive, so you see a double image of the back facets, and the inside can look blurry.
3. **Watch the fire.** Moissanite has more than twice the fire of diamond, and strontium titanate over four times its dispersion. Huge rainbow flashes in an "orange diamond" deserve a second look.
4. **Check the surface and cracks.** A colored coating or a colored filler in a crack can usually be seen under magnification.

The limit is honest. A lab-grown orange diamond is real diamond and passes every one of these checks. Color treatment is often impossible to identify with standard gemological tools, so the lab report is the real test.

### What to Ask the Seller

1. Is this diamond natural or laboratory-grown?
2. Is the color natural or treated, and how was it treated?
3. Which lab graded it, and does the report state the origin of color?

Ask for all three answers on the receipt. The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) turns a tag into questions like these.

## How to Care for an Orange Diamond

**Mineral Fact.** Diamond is the hardest natural mineral, but it is not especially tough. It has perfect cleavage, a direction along which a crystal can split cleanly, in four directions, so a hard knock on an edge or a point can chip it. Ordinary chemicals such as scented soap and chlorine do not hurt it.

Our care data has no rating yet for water, sun, heat or salt. The International Gem Society says the color of orange diamonds is stable and that they withstand any conventional cleaning, including cleaning solutions, ultrasonic machines and steam. The cautious exception is a coated or crack-filled stone, since those treatments are temporary.

### How to Clean an Orange Diamond Ring

1. Soak the ring for a few minutes in warm water with a drop of mild dish soap.
2. Brush gently around and behind the stone with a soft toothbrush.
3. Rinse in warm water and dry with a lint-free cloth.
4. If the stone is coated or filled, or you do not know, skip ultrasonic and steam cleaners and keep it away from heat.

- **Chemicals.** OK for the diamond itself. Mountings and other stones in the piece may need more care.
- **Knocks and storage.** Handle Gently. Store it in its own pouch so its edges do not meet other hard stones.
- **Toxicity.** Not Enough Data in our care data.

## Orange Diamond vs Look-Alikes

- **Yellow diamond.** Same mineral and similar causes of color, with a slightly different absorption. See [yellow diamond](/stones/yellow-diamond/).
- **Moissanite and cubic zirconia.** Lab-made simulants, both available in colors. See [moissanite vs diamond](/compare/moissanite-vs-diamond/) and [cubic zirconia vs diamond](/compare/cubic-zirconia-vs-diamond/).
- **Orange sapphire and other orange gems.** Different minerals. On a gemologist's refractometer a diamond reads over the limit of the scale, which separates it quickly. See [orange sapphire](/stones/orange-sapphire/).

## Price Guide

Color sets the price of an orange diamond more than anything else. Fancy Vivid and Fancy Deep are the most desirable grades and command the highest prices, a pure orange with no second hue is far rarer than an orangy yellow or a brownish orange, and pure orange stones tend to be small. Origin matters too, since a lab-grown Fancy Vivid orange is far more affordable than a natural one.

Compare stones only when their reports match on natural or lab-grown, color origin and color grade.
