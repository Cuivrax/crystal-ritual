# Types of Opal & How to Tell Them Apart

The types of opal fall into two groups. Precious opal flashes rainbow play-of-color, as in black, white, crystal and boulder opal, while common opal, such as Peruvian blue and pink opal, shows none. Fire opal is named for its orange to red body color rather than for flashes, and opalite is usually man-made glass, not opal at all.

## What Opal Is

**Mineral Fact.** Opal is hydrated silica, an amorphous form of silicon dioxide chemically similar to quartz, with a Mohs hardness of 5 to 6.5. The figures for its water differ. GIA says up to 20% water in its structure, while the International Gem Society gives 3% to 21%, usually 6% to 10% in gem opal. With no crystal structure, it counts as a mineraloid rather than a mineral.

Opal formed when silica gel filled cracks in rock and left microscopic silica spheres behind as the water evaporated. Those spheres decide everything below.

**Tradition.** The flashes impressed people long before anyone saw a sphere. Pliny the Elder praised opal for showing "the piercing fire of carbunculus, the purple brilliancy of amethystos, and the sea-green of smaragdus" at once (*Natural History*, Book 37, [chapter 21](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D37%3Achapter%3D21), Bostock and Riley translation, via Perseus at Tufts). Today it is the [October birthstone](/birthstones/october/), and our [opal page](/stones/opal/) tells the full story.

## Precious Opal vs Common Opal

When the spheres are uniform and neatly stacked, like Ping-Pong balls in a box, they diffract light into flashes called play-of-color, and the stone is precious opal, also called noble opal. When they are random in size, shape and arrangement, the result is common opal.

Most opal is common opal, sometimes called potch, and most of it is plain enough to be overlooked in the field. Peruvian common opal is one colorful exception, in pastel blues, greens and pinks. Precious opal is rare, and most of it has been mined in Australia, with Ethiopia and Mexico as secondary sources.

**Try This.** Set the stone on a dark cloth under a lamp and tilt it slowly. Flashes of spectral color that move with the angle mean precious opal. Even color with no flashes means common opal, or something that is not opal at all.

## Types of Opal at a Glance

| Type | Group | What sets it apart |
|---|---|---|
| [Black opal](/stones/black-opal/) | Precious | A black or very dark body color behind the flashes. The name can also cover dark potch with a thin layer of crystal opal on top |
| [White opal](/stones/white-opal/) | Precious | A white, yellow or cream body color, the most common body color for precious opal |
| Crystal opal | Precious | Colorless and transparent to semi-transparent, with rich play-of-color in reflected light |
| [Boulder opal](/stones/boulder-opal/) | Precious | Precious opal still attached to its host rock, often thin seams in brown ironstone. Australia is the most famous source |
| [Ethiopian opal](/stones/ethiopian-opal/) and [Welo opal](/stones/ethiopian-welo-opal/) | Precious, often | Opal from Ethiopia, where discoveries began in 1994. Welo opal has clear, white, yellow, orange or brown body colors with vivid play-of-color, and many Welo opals are hydrophane, a porous opal that absorbs water |
| [Fire opal](/stones/mexican-fire-opal/) | Either | A yellow, orange or red body color, with or without play-of-color. The "fire" is the body color. Also called Mexican or sun opal |
| [Hyalite](/stones/hyalite-opal/) | Usually common | Colorless or white and glassy, with little to no play-of-color |
| [Blue opal](/stones/blue-opal/) | Common | Usually Peruvian blue opal, colored by tiny copper mineral inclusions |
| [Pink opal](/stones/pink-opal/) | Common | Pastel pink opal, best known from Peru, mixed with a fibrous mineral called palygorskite |
| [Green opal](/stones/green-opal/) | Common | Includes prase opal, green to yellowish green, which resembles chrysoprase |
| [Dendritic opal](/stones/dendritic-opal/) | Usually common | Opal with branching inclusions. Moss opal is white to brownish and opaque, with dendritic inclusions that resemble moss |
| [Girasol](/stones/girasol/) | Named for its glow | Semi-transparent to translucent, with a moving, billowy light that resembles moonstone |
| [Yellow opal](/stones/yellow-opal/) and [purple opal](/stones/purple-opal/) | Varies | Color names rather than defined varieties. Ask whether the stone is fire opal, precious opal with a colored body, a common opal or something else |

**Mineral Fact.** A 2004 lab study of translucent pink opals from Mexico and Peru found them made of opal with 10% to 40% palygorskite and linked the pink to organic compounds called quinones, a cause the authors proposed rather than proved ([Fritsch and colleagues, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1127/0935-1221/2004/0016-0743)).

## Lab-Grown, Treated & Assembled Opal

Not every opal in a shop came out of the ground in one piece, and not every one is opal.

- **Lab-Grown.** Synthetic opals are real opals grown in a laboratory. Their color often sits in a mosaic pattern, and high magnification can reveal a cellular or chicken-wire structure, though opals made by the process Pierre Gilson created look very natural.
- **Natural Treated.** Oil, wax or plastic can be pushed into opal to improve its play-of-color and hide fine cracks, and smoke, sugar or dye can darken the body color. Matrix opal is often dyed black. All of these enhancements should be disclosed before a sale.
- **Assembled.** A doublet is a thin layer of precious opal glued to a black base. A triplet adds a clear quartz cap, which protects the softer opal and makes a better ring stone. Both hold real opal, but neither is a solid stone, so ask before you pay a solid opal price.
- **Imitation.** Opal imitations are typically plastic or glass. Plastic ones are soft, and glass ones typically contain bubbles and swirl marks.

## Opal vs Opalite

Opalite is the name you will meet most often in crystal shops, and it is usually not opal. The International Gem Society defines it as "a trade name for a type of glass often used to simulate moonstone or opal," though a natural green, lavender or purple common opal sometimes goes by the name too. [Tiffany stone](/stones/tiffany-stone/), an opalized fluorite from Utah, is sometimes sold as purple opal or opalite too, both misnomers.

[Opal vs opalite](/compare/opal-vs-opalite/) shows the safe ways to tell them apart, and [how to tell if a crystal is real](/real-or-fake/how-to-tell-if-crystals-are-real/) covers any stone sold at a real opal price.

## Caring for Every Kind of Opal

Opal chips, scratches and shatters more easily than most popular gems, so skip opal ring stones unless the setting protects the stone, the stone is a triplet or the ring is for occasional wear.

Our care data rates water Avoid for opal, white opal and Ethiopian opal, since hydrophane opal absorbs water and Ethiopian hydrophane opal can crack. Black, boulder, blue, dendritic, green, hyalite, fire, pink, purple and yellow opal are rated Brief Only, so a damp cloth is fine and a long soak is not. Keep every opal away from heat and sudden temperature changes, and store each one in its own soft pouch.
