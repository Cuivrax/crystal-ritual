# Which Crystals Can Go in Water? Full Safety Table

Hard crystals such as amethyst, citrine, rose quartz and clear quartz can go in water for a quick rinse, but should not be left to soak. Fluorite, opal, halite, angelite and marcasite should stay out of water, while selenite, celestite, turquoise and lapis lazuli can take only brief contact. No crystal belongs in water you drink.

Every rating on this page comes from the same care data as our [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/), and each one rests on a quote we checked against its source page. **OK** means the cited source says the contact is fine. **Brief Only** means keep it quick. **Avoid** means the cited source warns against it. **Not Enough Data** means we have no sourced answer yet, and we would rather tell you that than guess from a stone's hardness.

## Water Safety at a Glance

| Stones | Rinse under the tap | Long soak |
|---|---|---|
| Amethyst, citrine, rose quartz, clear quartz | OK | Brief Only |
| Black agate, flower agate, grape agate | OK | OK |
| Selenite, celestite, turquoise, lapis lazuli | Brief Only | Brief Only |
| Fluorite, opal, halite, angelite, marcasite | Avoid | Avoid |
| Black tourmaline, labradorite, malachite, pyrite | Not Enough Data | Not Enough Data |
| Any stone, in water you drink | No | No |

## Crystals That Can't Go in Water

These stones are rated Avoid for water. Some dissolve, some soak it up and crack, and two react with it in ways you do not want near you.

| Stone | Why it stays out of water |
|---|---|
| Fluorite, including green, purple, rainbow and yttrium fluorite | Slightly soluble in water, so never submerge it |
| Opal and white opal | Sensitive to moisture. Hydrophane opals absorb water and chemicals and should be kept dry |
| Ethiopian opal | Hydrophane Ethiopian opal can absorb water and crack, so do not immerse it |
| Tiffany stone | Partly opal, so a dealer guide advises against submerging it. A slightly damp cloth is fine |
| Halite | Rock salt. It dissolves in water, especially warm water |
| Cobalto calcite and mangano calcite | Calcite is slightly soluble in water, and a dealer care table lists both as not safe to cleanse in water |
| Angelite | A blue-gray variety of anhydrite. Keep anhydrite dry, because it readily absorbs moisture and over time turns wholly or partly into gypsum. Dealers also advise avoiding water |
| Bumblebee jasper | A retailer advises against soaking or long water exposure for this soft, porous stone |
| Marcasite | Reacts with moisture and releases sulfuric acid. Store it below 60% humidity |
| Realgar | Water and steam can release toxic fumes. It contains arsenic |

**Safety.** Realgar is poisonous. Avoid breathing its dust or fumes, and wash up after handling it. It is a specimen for a shelf, never for a pocket, a bath or a bowl of water.

## Crystals That Can Take a Quick Rinse Only

These stones are rated Brief Only. A quick rinse or a damp cloth is fine, then dry the stone right away. None of them should sit in water.

| Group | Stones | Why to keep it quick |
|---|---|---|
| Soluble and soft minerals | Selenite, celestite, ulexite, aragonite, blue calcite, honey calcite, orange calcite, pink calcite, white onyx | Gypsum, the mineral behind selenite, is water soluble. A dealer guide calls celestite slightly soluble. Ulexite breaks down in hot water. Calcite and aragonite dissolve in acids, and the white onyx sold as onyx marble is calcite too |
| Porous stones | Turquoise (blue, green, white, purple, Kingman, Persian), lapis lazuli, lazurite, variscite | Porous, so water and other liquids soak in and can damage or recolor them. Wipe with a damp cloth and dry at once |
| Opals | Black, boulder, blue, dendritic, green, hyalite, Mexican fire, pink, purple and yellow opal | Opal is sensitive to moisture and to temperature change. Dealers disagree on fire opal, one says water only sparingly and never for doublets or triplets, another says avoid it |
| Amber, coral and ammolite | Amber, Baltic amber, blue amber, dark amber, coral, red coral, ammolite | Clean amber only with a damp cloth or mild warm soapy water, and coral only with a damp cloth, dried carefully. Frost and weather can crack ammolite's thin layer |
| Stones that rust or crumble | Magnetite, adamite | Magnetite can grow a yellow-brown rust coating if washed or kept damp. Adamite specimens sit in a crumbly matrix |
| Coated, dyed and druzy surfaces | Aqua aura quartz, aura quartz, pink agate, druzy | Dealers say to wipe aqua aura with a damp cloth and not to immerse it in liquid cleaners, and to dry aura quartz to avoid stains. Some dyed agate uses water-soluble dyes that wash out. A gentle rinse is fine for druzy, but water with chemicals or minerals in it can hurt it |
| Softer stones and copper minerals | Atlantisite, stichtite, golden lepidolite, azurite, chalcocite, cuprite, dioptase, purpurite | A dealer warns that long exposure could soften and mar stichtite. Another gives mixed advice on lepidolite. A third allows chalcocite a quick rinse, dried at once, never a soak. Azurite, cuprite and dioptase get only a gentle wash |
| A gentle wash, and nothing more | Andalusite, barite, blue kyanite, brookite, chicken blood stone, dalmatian jasper, dolomite, emerald, euclase, eudialyte, green sapphire, grossular, hematite, kammererite, Libyan desert glass, orbicular jasper, picasso jasper, pink amethyst, rutilated quartz, scheelite, snowflake obsidian, tektite, willemite | The cited sources describe a wash with warm water, mild soap and a soft brush, and say nothing about soaking. Emerald is washed lightly and only when needed |

Strictly, selenite is the name for colorless, transparent gypsum crystals, while satin spar is a massive, fibrous variety of gypsum that can be carved. A silky, fibrous wand or tower sold as selenite fits the second description. Either way it is gypsum, so the same quick, dry-it-at-once rule applies.

## Crystals That Can Go in Water for a Rinse

These stones are rated OK for water, so a rinse under the tap is fine. A long soak is a separate question. The Care Checker clears it for only three stones, black agate, flower agate and grape agate, and only through a dealer's care table for agate in general. For every other stone here the Care Checker shows Brief Only for a soak, because the sources describe washing a stone, not leaving it in a bowl.

**Mineral Fact.** [Amethyst](/stones/amethyst/), [rose quartz](/stones/rose-quartz/) and citrine are cleaned with warm water, mild detergent and a soft brush, and quartz is chemically inert with most substances. That is why they rinse well. It does not make them safe to drink from.

Three stones on the list wear a thin coating, titanium quartz, rainbow quartz and mystic topaz. Dealers advise mild soap at most and a dry cloth straight after, so the coating does not spot. Amazonite and vanadinite rinse fine but appear again in the drinking water table below.

## Why Water Hurts Some Stones

Water does damage in five different ways, and knowing which one applies tells you how careful to be.

**It dissolves them.** Halite is rock salt and dissolves in water, faster when the water is warm. Gypsum, the mineral behind selenite, is water soluble, and fluorite is slightly soluble. Water soluble gems need extra care.

**They soak it up.** Turquoise is porous and absorbs liquids that can damage it or change its color. Opal already holds water in its structure, 3% to 21% of it, and hydrophane opal takes in more. In Ethiopian hydrophane opal that can end in a crack.

**They react with it.** Marcasite reacts with moisture and releases sulfuric acid, and realgar can give off toxic fumes. Magnetite can rust. Pyrite has no care rating in our data yet, but it oxidizes when exposed to air and moisture and produces acids, which is why rock used for concrete must be free of it, and it tarnishes easily.

**The surface is not the stone.** Dyes, wax and metal films sit in or on the surface. Lapis lazuli is often dyed and waxed, gypsum takes dyes and coatings very easily, and some dyed agate bleeds in water. Not sure if yours is dyed? The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) lists the questions to ask the seller.

**Glue and thin layers.** An opal doublet is a thin layer of precious opal glued to a black base, which is why a fire opal dealer says never to wet doublets or triplets. Ammolite's thin layer can crack with frost and weather.

## Why People Put Healing Crystals in Water

**Modern Association.** Holding a stone under running water is one of the most common ways modern crystal guides suggest to cleanse it, next to moonlight, smoke and sound. Some guides go further. One crystal blog tells readers to drop citrine into a water bottle for energy, and a German stone encyclopedia marks citrine as suitable for direct contact with water. Those are beliefs about energy. They say nothing about what water does to the stone, and on that question the care data wins every time.

**Tradition.** Swallowing stones is far older than water bottles. George Frederick Kunz records the old belief that powdered malachite, dissolved in milk and drunk as a potion, would cure colic, and he adds that "if not very carefully used, the effect would have been anything but favorable." The physician De Boot was told that six grains of it worked as a purgative and, Kunz notes, never ventured to test it (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, pages 150 to 151). Malachite is a copper mineral, and it heads our list of stones to keep out of drinking water.

## Can You Put Crystals in a Water Bottle?

No stone should sit in water you drink, and that includes a water bottle. Our care data rates how a stone handles water, not whether the water that touched it is safe to swallow, and it clears no stone for that. Crystal healers should avoid making potions with toxic gems and avoid putting such gems in anyone's mouth.

**Try This.** Keep the stone beside your bottle instead, on the desk next to your keyboard or in the bag pocket next to the bottle. Every refill becomes the reminder, and nothing touches what you drink.

Some bottles hold a stone in a sealed compartment so it never meets the water. That is the indirect method, and we will only name stones for it once a reference source clears them for a long soak and our data clears them for toxicity. So far the Care Checker clears a long soak for three agates only through one dealer's table for agate in general, which is not enough for anything you drink, so we name no stone for a sealed bottle yet. Keeping the stone beside the bottle, as in the Try This above, works with any stone.

### Toxic Crystals to Keep Out of Drinking Water

Our house rule covers these stones whatever any other guide says. Wearing or displaying most of them is fine. The rule is about water you drink.

| Stone | Why it stays out |
|---|---|
| Malachite | A copper mineral, rated Brief Only for toxicity in the Care Checker. Dust from grinding it is toxic to breathe, and finished jewelry, carvings and specimens should be safe to handle. It reacts with acids, so keep it out of drinking water |
| Azurite | A copper mineral, rated Brief Only for toxicity in the Care Checker. Normal wearing or handling of a finished piece should pose no hazard, but the cutting dust is toxic |
| Chrysocolla | A copper mineral, colored by copper, soft and fragile in pure form |
| Pyrite | An iron sulfide that oxidizes with air and moisture and produces acids |
| Galena, cerussite, vanadinite | Rated Avoid for toxicity in the Care Checker. All three contain lead (vanadinite also vanadium and possibly arsenic). Galena is the main ore of lead, and many schools have taken it out of student mineral kits. For cerussite and vanadinite, normal handling or display of a finished stone is no problem and cutters need precautions |
| Realgar, adamite | Contain arsenic |
| Bumblebee jasper | A retailer says it contains arsenic and sulfur minerals and must not be ingested |
| Chicken blood stone | Rated a high toxicity risk for mercury. Finished pieces pose no health risk in normal handling, and cutting needs precautions |
| Amazonite | Rated Avoid for toxicity in the Care Checker. Its green color is thought to be caused by trace amounts of lead |
| Baltic amber, dark amber, red coral, jet, ammolite, pyromorphite, wulfenite, halite, Tiffany stone | Each is rated Avoid for toxicity in the Care Checker. The International Gem Society table lists amber, jet, coral and ammolite as high risk without saying why, pyromorphite and wulfenite contain lead, raw Tiffany stone is said to contain beryllium, and the sources for halite discourage tasting minerals |
| Cinnabar | Contains mercury. Its toxicity risk is rated High, and it can react dangerously if accidentally swallowed |
| Orpiment | An arsenic sulfide, like realgar. Both are toxic, and contact with them should be avoided |
| Stibnite | Named in our house rule for drinking water, like every stone in this table |
| Any dyed or stabilized stone | You cannot know what the dye or resin is |

**Safety.** Small stones are a choking hazard for children and pets. If someone swallows a stone, or drinks water a stone from this table sat in, call the [Poison Help line](https://poisonhelp.hrsa.gov/) at 1-800-222-1222 right away. It is a toll-free number that connects you to your local poison center, 24 hours a day. For a pet, call your vet.

## How Long Should You Cleanse Crystals in Water?

As long as a rinse, and no longer. The cited sources describe washing a stone, not leaving it to soak, so the Care Checker shows Brief Only for a long soak on every stone except those three agates. Here is the rinse the cited sources support for stones rated OK.

1. **Check the stone first.** Put it in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/). If it says Avoid or Not Enough Data, skip water and pick a dry method.
2. **Use lukewarm water, not hot.** Abrupt temperature changes can fracture citrine.
3. **Add a drop of mild soap and use a soft brush.** That is the recommended method for amethyst, citrine and rose quartz.
4. **Rinse, then pat it dry with a soft cloth.** Do it straight away, before you set the stone back on the shelf.
5. **For Brief Only stones, use a damp cloth instead of the tap.** Wipe and dry at once, the usual advice for turquoise.

Prefer to keep a stone dry? Moonlight, sound, smoke, a selenite plate and brown rice are the dry methods the Care Checker lists, and it records no physical contact risk for any of them. Planning a jar under the full moon? Read [how to make moon water without ruining your stones](/rituals/moon-water/), where the stone sits beside the jar.

## Stones with No Water Rating Yet

Black tourmaline, labradorite, tiger's eye, carnelian, smoky quartz, howlite, lepidolite, malachite and pyrite have no sourced water rating yet, so the Care Checker shows Not Enough Data for them. That does not mean water will ruin them. It means no reference we trust has said, and until one does, a dry method is the gentle choice.

The same goes for any stone you cannot name. If a gift or a market find has no label, treat it as Not Enough Data, keep it dry and keep it out of anything you drink. For the full method by method picture, including sun and salt, see [how to cleanse crystals without damaging them](/care/how-to-cleanse-crystals/).
