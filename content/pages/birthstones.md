# Birthstones by Month & Where the Lists Come From

A birthstone chart pairs each month of the year with one or more gems. The modern US chart is the American Gem Society list, which grew out of a list US jewelers adopted in Kansas City in 1912, itself built on much older European lists. The chart below shows all three, so you can see which stones are new and which go back centuries.

## Where Birthstone Lists Come From

**Tradition.** The idea starts with the twelve stones on the breastplate of the Jewish high priest. In the 1st century the historian Josephus wrote that those twelve stones could stand for the months or for the twelve signs of the zodiac, and St. Jerome repeated the link in the early 400s (Kunz 1913, pages 307 to 310). Later writers swapped in the twelve foundation stones of the New Jerusalem from the book of Revelation, which is why the oldest lists start the year in March (pages 307 to 308 and 314).

Wearing the stone of your own birth month came much later. Kunz traced it to Poland in the 18th century (page 307). Until then, he says, people picked a stone for the virtue they wanted, not for the month they were born in.

### The 1912 List & Its Updates

In August 1912 the National Association of Jewelers met in Kansas City and adopted a single list for the US trade. It moved the ruby from December to July and gave December turquoise instead. Kunz printed the list the next year and objected loudly, writing that "there should be no duplication or triplication of birth-stones for any given month" (Kunz 1913, pages 317 to 321).

The trade kept adding stones anyway.

- **1952.** The Jewelry Industry Council of America modified the list, adding alexandrite to June, citrine to November, pink tourmaline to October and zircon to December.
- **2002.** Tanzanite became a modern December birthstone, joining turquoise and zircon.
- **July 2016.** The American Gem Trade Association and Jewelers of America named spinel a birthstone for August.

The sources we checked do not list every change between 1912 and 1952.

## Modern vs Traditional Birthstones

"Modern" means the current US trade list, the first column above. "Traditional" is used two ways. Most jewelers mean the 1912 list, the second column. Older writers like Kunz meant the European lists before it, the third column, which is why a December "traditional birthstone" can be turquoise on one site and ruby on another. When a page says traditional, check which list it means.

## Why Some Months Have Several Birthstones

GIA's birthstone exhibit notes that differing traditions are often cited, but says "the more significant reason is that some gemstones are too rare and expensive, and others just aren't as popular." A second or third stone gives buyers another price and another look. June has pearl, moonstone and alexandrite, December has three blue stones, and January stays with garnet, which already comes in almost every color.

## Birthstones vs Zodiac Stones

**Tradition.** Zodiac stones are a separate list, and mixing the two is the most common birthstone mix-up. Kunz calls them "astral" or "zodiacal" stones, tied to the signs rather than the months. The signs overlap the months, so Aquarius runs from January 21 to February 20 in his table, not January 1 to 31 (Kunz 1913, pages 321 to 322).

His zodiac rhymes even pick different stones. Sagittarius gets topaz and Capricorn gets ruby, while the 1912 December birthstone is turquoise (pages 345 to 347). Kunz also lists talismanic gems for the twelve guardian angels, so in his day a person could choose from three different stones (pages 322 to 323). Modern crystal guides have built yet another layer, which our [zodiac crystals](/zodiac/) pages trace sign by sign, starting with [Sagittarius](/zodiac/sagittarius/).

## How to Pick Your Birthstone

Start with the month, then pick from whichever list you like. All of them are real traditions, just from different centuries. Then think about wear. A soft or brittle stone, like turquoise or tanzanite, does better in earrings or a pendant than in a ring you wear every day. Ask the seller three things. What is the mineral name? Has it been treated? Is it natural or lab-grown?

**Try This.** Giving a birthstone? Add a card with its line from the chart, the modern stone and the older one. It turns one gift into two stories.
