# How to Make Moon Water Without Ruining Your Stones

Moon water is water left out overnight under the moon, usually a full moon, then used as part of a personal ritual. Any effect is a matter of belief. If you want a crystal involved, set it beside the jar rather than in it, because some stones dissolve in water and some contain lead or copper.

| What you need | A clean glass jar with a lid, water, and a spot the moonlight reaches |

## What Moon Water Is & Where the Idea Comes From

**The Story.** Moon water is simple. You fill a jar, leave it where the moon can reach it, and bring it in the next morning. What people do with it varies. Crystal sites suggest everything from drinking it to adding it to a bath or using it as a face toner, sometimes with stones dropped straight into the water. That last habit is where things go wrong for some stones, and we will get to it.

**Modern Association.** The idea sits inside a bigger one. Leaving stones out in moonlight to "cleanse" or "charge" them is the method that turns up for nearly every stone in the modern crystal guides we have collected. Some of those guides split the month in two, the full moon for release and reflection and the new moon for setting intentions.

**Tradition.** Stones and the moon have been linked in European writing for centuries, though not through water.

- **1500s.** Kunz reports a story told by Antoine Mizauld of a "selenite or moonstone" with a white mark that grew and shrank as the moon waxed and waned (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, page 98).
- **1500s.** Kunz also tells of a stone said to belong to Pope Leo X that grew brighter as the moon waxed, and he adds that such stories were "undoubtedly fables that were circulated intentionally, or more probably through pure love of exaggeration" (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, pages 386 to 387).
- **1500s.** In Cornelius Agrippa's occult philosophy, as Kunz summarizes it, the high priest of Monday's mysteries wore a triple necklace of pearls, crystals and selenites (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, pages 336 to 337).
- **Folk tradition.** Kunz records that lovers held a moonstone in the mouth at the full moon to read their future together (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, pages 97 to 98). Please do not try that one with any stone.

Moon water itself does not appear in either of Kunz's books on gem lore. [TO CHECK where the modern moon water practice comes from, with a dated source.]

## How to Make Moon Water

**Try This.** The whole method fits on a sticky note.

1. **Pick your night.** Check the phase on [Tonight's Moon](/moon/full-moon-calendar/). The full moon is the usual choice.
2. **Fill a clean glass jar and close it.** If there is any chance you will drink it, start with water that is safe to drink. The lid keeps out dust and insects.
3. **Set it where moonlight reaches.** A windowsill is fine. So is a porch or a balcony.
4. **Put your stone beside the jar, not in it.** Against the glass is as close as it needs to get.
5. **Add an intention if you like.** Write one line on a card and tuck it under the jar. Label the lid with the date and the moon's name.
6. **Bring it all in the next morning.** Some stones fade with long exposure to light, fluorite among them ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/fluorite-jewelry-gemstone-information/)), so do not leave your stone on a sunny sill all day.

What to do with the water is up to you. Water a plant you care about, rinse your hands as a way to start the day, or wipe down your desk or altar. [TO CHECK public health guidance on how long covered drinking water stays fine at room temperature, before suggesting drinking it.]

## Can You Put Crystals in Moon Water?

Some sturdy stones can take a quick rinse, but no stone needs to be inside the jar, and a stone never goes in water you drink. Our care data rates how a stone handles water, not whether water it sat in is safe to swallow, and it clears no stone for that, so the answer for anything you drink is no.

### The Safe Way, Beside the Jar

This is the indirect method. The stone sits next to the jar, or on its lid, and never touches the water. It still shares the night and the ritual, and nothing can dissolve into what you drink.

Some guides go one step closer and put the stone in a small sealed glass inside the jar. We will list the stones that suit that version once our care data clears them for both water and toxicity. So far our sources clear a long soak for only three stones, black agate, flower agate and grape agate, through one dealer's care table for agate in general ([GemSelect](https://gemselect.com/gem-info/cleaning.php)), which is not enough for anything you drink [TO CHECK with a reference source before listing any stone for the sealed glass].

### Never in Water You Drink

Our house rule covers these stones, whatever any other guide says. Where we have a sourced reason, it is below.

| Stone | Why it stays out | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Malachite | A copper mineral, rated Brief Only for toxicity in the Care Checker. Dust from grinding it is toxic to breathe, and it reacts with weak acids | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/malachite-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/), [Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/malachite.shtml) |
| Azurite | A copper mineral, rated Brief Only for toxicity in the Care Checker. Its cutting dust is toxic | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/azurite-jewelry-gemstone-information/) |
| Chrysocolla | A copper mineral, colored by copper, and soft and fragile in pure form | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/chrysocolla-chalcedony-gem-information/), [Gemstones.com](https://www.gemstones.com/gemopedia/chrysocolla) |
| Pyrite | An iron sulfide that oxidizes with air and moisture and produces acids as it does, which is why rock used for concrete must be free of it | [Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/pyrite.shtml) |
| Galena | Lead sulfide, rated Avoid for toxicity in the Care Checker. Many schools have removed it from student mineral kits |
| Cerussite, vanadinite | Lead minerals, rated Avoid for toxicity in the Care Checker. The International Gem Society says normal handling or display of a finished stone is no problem, and cutters need precautions | [IGS cerussite](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/cerussite-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/), [IGS vanadinite](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/vanadinite-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/) | [Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/galena.shtml) |
| Realgar | Contains arsenic, and water or steam can release toxic fumes | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/realgar-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/) |
| Cinnabar, stibnite, orpiment | [TO CHECK sourced reasons, no stone sheets yet] | |
| Any dyed or stabilized stone | You cannot know what the dye or resin is. Porous stones such as lapis lazuli take and hold dye | [Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/lapis-lazuli/) |

The rule here is only about water you drink. The [full water safety table](/care/crystals-that-cant-go-in-water/) lists more stones to keep out of drinking water.

### Stones That Water Damages

These are not dangerous to you. Water is dangerous to them.

| Stone | What water does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Selenite | Selenite is a clear form of gypsum, which is water soluble. Clean it by hand with warm water only and keep contact brief | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gypsum-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/) |
| Fluorite | Slightly soluble in water, so never submerge it | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/fluorite-jewelry-gemstone-information/) |
| Celestite | Slightly soluble in water, per a dealer guide. The International Gem Society cleans it only with a soft brush, mild detergent and warm water | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/celestite-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/), [Gemstones.com](https://www.gemstones.com/gemopedia/celestite) |
| Angelite | Dealers advise avoiding exposure to water [TO CHECK with a reference source] | [Gemstones.com](https://www.gemstones.com/gemopedia/angelite) |
| Opal | Sensitive to moisture. Hydrophane opal absorbs water | [IGS](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/opal-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/) |
| Turquoise | Often porous and absorbs liquids | [Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/turquoise.shtml) |
| Lapis lazuli | Slightly porous and often dyed | [Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/lapis-lazuli/) |
| Aqua aura and other coated quartz | The metal film is fragile. No soaking in liquid cleaners | [Skyjems](https://skyjems.ca/pages/encyclopedia-aquaaura-quartz) |

### Stones Usually Fine to Rinse

Reference guides clean amethyst, [rose quartz](/stones/rose-quartz/) and moonstone with warm water, mild detergent and a soft brush ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/rose-quartz/)). Quartz, the mineral behind amethyst, rose quartz and [clear quartz](/stones/clear-quartz/), is chemically inert with most substances ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml)). That supports a quick rinse. It is not a green light for an overnight soak or for a drink. Our care data rates all four OK for a rinse and Brief Only for a long soak, because the sources describe washing, not soaking.

## Five Minutes on the Night of the Full Moon

**When to use it.** The night of the full moon, after dinner, before the evening disappears into your phone.

**You need.** A glass jar with a lid, water, a small card and a pen. A stone you like, if you have one. If you do not, the ritual works the same.

**Steps.**

1. **1 minute.** Fill the jar, close it and set it on the windowsill. Put your stone beside it.
2. **1 minute.** Turn off the main light. Look at the moon, or the brightest patch of sky, and take three slow breaths, letting each one out longer than you took it in.
3. **2 minutes.** On the card, write one thing you are done carrying this month and one thing you are keeping. Slide the card under the jar.
4. **1 minute.** Write the date and the moon's name on the lid. Leave everything until morning.

**In the morning.** Water a plant with it. Read the card once more, then keep it or throw it out.

**Why it helps.** Whatever you believe about the stone, here is what the ritual does for sure. It gives you five quiet minutes, one written thought and a date you will meet again next month. **Tradition.** Modern guides tie the full moon to release and the new moon to fresh intentions, so the card fits the night. **What Research Says.** [TO CHECK a peer-reviewed study with DOI on expressive writing or slow breathing, and say plainly that it did not test moon water or stones.]

**Safety.** Stone beside the jar, never inside. Keep jars and small stones out of reach of small children and pets. If you light a candle, never leave it burning unattended.
