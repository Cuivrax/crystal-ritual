# Milky Quartz Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

Milky quartz is the cloudy white variety of quartz, made white by countless tiny cavities and bubbles of water or carbon dioxide trapped as the crystal grew. Crystal sellers tie it to calm, purity and clear thinking, and those are beliefs. The honest note is that it is little used as a gem, and to prospectors it meant a place to look for gold.

> **Is It Real? Quick Checks.** Real milky quartz is cloudy white through the body of the stone, with a glassy to slightly greasy shine. Where it is chipped, it breaks in curved, shell-like surfaces, not flat planes. White calcite and feldspar can look similar, but both are softer, and feldspar splits along flat cleavage. Glass needs a closer look (see below).

## What Milky Quartz Actually Is

**The Stone.** Quartz is silicon dioxide, the most abundant and widely distributed mineral at Earth's surface, rating 7 on the Mohs scale ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/minerals/quartz.shtml)). The [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/quartz-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/) groups milky quartz with amethyst, smoky quartz, citrine and rose quartz as the crystalline varieties, the ones that grow in distinct, visible crystals.

The definition is simple. [Amethyst Galleries' Mineral Gallery](https://galleries.com/minerals/silicate/milky_qu/milky_qu.htm) calls milky quartz "any quartz crystal or cluster that is white in color and cloudy," and says that cloudy white look gave the variety its name.

### Why Is Milky Quartz White?

Because it is full of tiny bubbles. "The milkiness is due to myriad tiny cavities and bubbles filled with CO2 or water," explains the International Gem Society. Amethyst Galleries adds that these fluid inclusions have been sealed in the crystal since it first grew, and that they can give milky quartz a greasy luster unlike the other quartz varieties.

So the white is not a dye, a stain or a coating. It is the same clear mineral as [clear quartz](/stones/clear-quartz/), packed with trapped fluid. Amethyst Galleries puts it bluntly. "From a cynical point of view," the inclusions have ruined the crystal for gem and optical work.

Would breaking a piece open reveal clear crystal inside? Usually not, since the inclusions were trapped as the crystal grew. Skip the hammer anyway. Broken quartz has sharp edges, and the IGS rates quartz dust from cutting and grinding as a high risk to breathe.

### Milky Quartz or White Quartz?

For most shoppers, the same thing. Any white, cloudy quartz fits the definition above, and the IGS notes that vein quartz is often white. The bead dealer [Fire Mountain Gems](https://www.firemountaingems.com/resources/encyclobeadia/gem-notes/gmstnprprtssnwq) sells the same cloudy white material as snow quartz and lists white quartz, milk quartz and even quartzite as other names. Quartzite is a different thing, a rock of tightly packed quartz grains formed by metamorphism (IGS). Our [snow quartz page](/stones/snow-quartz/) covers the trade name.

### Where Milky Quartz Forms

The crystalline quartz varieties generally grow in pegmatites and veins, deposited from water solutions over a long time (IGS). Milky quartz is a massive variety available in large pieces, and the IGS names California and Colorado among its occurrences.

It also shows up inside other crystals. Milky quartz is often the cloudy phantom inside otherwise clear rock crystal, amethyst, citrine or smoky quartz, a ghostly crystal within a crystal that formed early and was later covered by clear growth (Amethyst Galleries). Bands of milky quartz and amethyst make the stone sold as [chevron amethyst](/stones/chevron-amethyst/), and the ghost effect gives [phantom quartz](/stones/phantom-quartz/) its name.

## Where the Meaning Comes from

**The Story.** Milky quartz has no grand legend. What it has is a long record of white stones kept, buried and carried, and one very practical meaning.

- **Ancient Rome.** **Tradition.** Pliny the Elder took crystal for ice frozen hard by winter cold, writing that "crystal is only to be found in places where the winter snow freezes with the greatest intensity" (*Natural History*, Book 37, chapter 9, [Perseus Digital Library, Tufts University](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D37%3Achapter%3D9)). His translators read crystallus as clear rock crystal, so the ice story belongs to clear quartz, not to its cloudy cousin.
- **Old graves.** **Tradition.** White stones were often placed in graves, sometimes in the mouth of the dead. George Frederick Kunz passes on the suggestion that the sparks quartz gives off when struck were believed to light the dark path of the departed (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, page 25).
- **Ancient Persia.** **Tradition.** In 1901, near Susa, Henry de Morgan uncovered a bronze sarcophagus that he dated to between 350 and 330 BCE. One necklace in it held 400 stone beads and 400 gold beads, among them "hyaline and milky quartz," clear and white side by side (Kunz 1915, pages 323 to 324).
- **14th century, Scotland.** **Tradition.** The Stone of the Banner of Clan Donnachaidh was a polished ball of white quartz about 1¾ inches (4.5 cm) across. A chief on his way to join Robert Bruce before the battle of Bannockburn spotted it glittering in a clod of earth stuck to his flagstaff, and the clan treated it as a talisman in battle. Tradition said it was the same white stone St. Columba had once used (Kunz 1915, pages 24 to 25).
- **North America.** **Tradition.** Kunz describes unworked white quartz pebbles from the Etowah Mound in Georgia and worked white quartz pebbles from the Pueblo region of the Southwest, some found in graves and called "fire stones" or "charms." He tied their appeal to purity, the old link between whiteness and spotless cleanliness, and to the sparks of light quartz gives off (1915, page 27).
- **The white stone of Revelation.** **Tradition.** Kunz also points to the verse "To him that overcometh ... I will give a white stone, and in the stone a new name written" (1915, page 25).
- **The gold country.** **Tradition.** Prospectors searching for gold-laden ore look for outcrops of milky white quartz veins, and many of the finest gold specimens are lacy gold in pure white milky quartz (Amethyst Galleries). For miners, white quartz meant one thing, and it was not calm.
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** Crystal sellers call it the "observation crystal" and a stone of calm, purity and fresh starts, linked with the crown chakra.

## What People Use Milky Quartz for Today

**Modern Association.** In modern crystal guides, milky quartz is Often Linked with easing anxiety and with protection from "negative energies," and Sometimes Linked with focus and confidence. Guides call it a stone for memory and wisdom, for listening and public speaking, and a meditation stone. Some describe purity, innocence and a fresh start.

The "why" is mostly the white. A cloudy white stone reads as snow, a blank page and a clean slate, which is the same purity Kunz saw behind white pebbles in old graves. The crown chakra, shown in violet or white on today's chakra chart, follows from the color, and some guides simply assign it to all seven.

### Is Milky Quartz Different from Clear Quartz?

As a mineral, only in its inclusions. Both are quartz, and the clear kind simply lacks the trapped bubbles. In the crystal world they get different jobs. Guides call [clear quartz](/stones/clear-quartz/) a master healer and amplifier, while milky quartz gets the gentler words, calm, purity and observation. Fire Mountain Gems sums up the seller view as clear quartz, "yet gentler."

Some pages promise far more, from balancing hormones and easing breastfeeding pain to strengthening bones and teeth, healing burns and even protecting against the sun's radiation. Gem science and medicine do not back those claims, and no stone replaces sunscreen.

> **When a Stone Is Not Enough.** Milky quartz is often chosen for stress, anxiety and a sense of calm, and some shops sell it for hormones, nursing pain or bone health. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If anxiety or low mood lasts or feels heavy, talk to a doctor or a therapist, and for pain while nursing, a doctor, midwife or lactation consultant. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/), any time, day or night, in the United States. In an emergency, call 911.

## How to Use Milky Quartz in Everyday Life

**The Ritual.** These routines work whether or not you believe in crystal energy. The stone is a physical reminder you can see and hold, and the habit does the rest.

### The One-Minute Window Break

**Try This.** Keep a rough piece of milky quartz on your desk. When a long task starts to blur, pick it up, step to the window and turn the stone in the light for one minute. Find one detail you had not noticed, a glint, a crack, a ghostly edge. Guides call it the observation crystal, so let it make you observe. Then sit back down.

### A Pocket Stone Before You Speak

**Try This.** Guides link milky quartz with public speaking. Carry a small tumbled piece into the meeting or the toast. Before you stand up, close your hand around it and say your first sentence silently, once. The stone gives your hand something to do. The rehearsal is the part that helps.

### A White Stone With a New Name

**Try This.** For a graduation, a new job or a new last name, give a white quartz pebble with a card. Write what it is, quartz clouded by tiny bubbles of water and carbon dioxide trapped when it grew, then the line from Revelation about a white stone with a new name written in it, and the new name or title underneath.

**Safety.** Small stones are not toys, so keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them. Rough or broken quartz can have sharp edges.

**What Research Says.** In a lab study of a long, dull vigilance task, people who briefly switched to a second task now and then kept their performance steady, while those who stayed on the main task without a break got steadily worse (Ariga and Lleras, 2011, *Cognition*, [doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.12.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2010.12.007)). It was a short lab task, the break was another task rather than a rest, and no stone or window was involved, so applying it to a desk stone is our extrapolation. The break is the part that may help.

## Is My Milky Quartz Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone. Milky quartz is common, and its look comes from its own inclusions, so there is little reason to fake it.

- **Natural, untreated.** Most likely what you have. Gem references describe color treatment for gem forms of quartz such as citrine and amethyst ([minerals.net](https://www.minerals.net/mineral/quartz)), but no reference describes treated milky quartz.
- **Natural, treated.** No reference describes heated, dyed or coated milky quartz. The metal-coated quartz minerals.net describes is sold under "aura" names.
- **Lab-grown.** Quartz was one of the first gems grown on a large scale, with major development during World War II to supply crystals for radios, and the IGS notes that synthetic quartz is always clean. Cloudiness is the opposite of what labs grow it for.
- **Imitation.** Glass is the usual quartz imitation, though no reference describes glass sold as milky quartz in particular.
- **Trade names.** "Snow quartz" and "white quartz" describe the same material. "Quartzite" on a milky quartz tag is a loose use of the word, since quartzite is a rock (IGS).

### Three Checks, With Their Limits

1. **Look at the body.** Milky quartz is white and cloudy through its body. The limit is that milky phantoms also sit inside clear quartz, amethyst, citrine and smoky quartz (Amethyst Galleries), so a cloudy patch alone does not name the stone.
2. **Compare the hardness.** Quartz is 7, while calcite is much softer at 3 and feldspar softer at 6 (minerals.net). Skip scratch tests on a piece you love, since they leave a mark.
3. **Look at a break.** Quartz has no cleavage and typically breaks with a curved, conchoidal fracture (Geology.com), while feldspar has perfect cleavage (minerals.net). This rules out feldspar, not glass. Glass tends to feel warmer to the touch than the gems it imitates and can show mold marks ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/glass)).

Ask the seller two questions. Is this natural quartz or glass? Where was it found?

## How to Care for Milky Quartz

**Mineral Fact.** Quartz is durable at 7 on the Mohs scale and chemically inert in contact with most substances (Geology.com), but it dissolves in hydrofluoric acid (minerals.net).

- **Water, soaking and cleaning.** Unknown. Care guides cover quartz in general, but none covers milky quartz itself, so water, soaking and cleaning stay unrated here and the page gives no soaking advice.
- **Sun.** Unknown.
- **Heat.** Brief only. Heat can microfracture quartz stone, as after a fire. One jeweler's care guide ([Gemporia](https://www.gemporia.com/en-gb/gemology-hub/article/922/)) warns that inclusions can expand at a different rate from the stone and crack it under extreme heat or cold, especially tiny pockets of moisture, and milky quartz is full of them.
- **Chemicals.** Brief only. Inert with most substances, but keep it away from anything containing hydrofluoric acid.
- **Salt.** Unknown.
- **Scratches and storage.** Brief only. Hard at 7 and durable, but brittle, and it breaks with curved, conchoidal fractures. No care source gives storage advice for it.
- **Toxicity.** OK to wear and handle. The [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/gemstone-care-guide/) rates wearing and handling intact quartz as negligible risk, but cutting and grinding dust as high risk, which calls for a respirator, ventilation and wet methods.

### Can Milky Quartz Go in Water?

That is still an open question. Quartz itself is chemically inert, but no care source covers milky quartz as a variety, so water, soaking and cleaning are rated Unknown and this page gives no advice on them either way. A soft dry cloth is enough for dust. Keep every stone out of drinking water, and read our guide to [moon water without ruining your stones](/rituals/moon-water/). ## Milky Quartz vs Look-Alikes

- **Clear quartz.** The same mineral without the bubbles. See [clear quartz](/stones/clear-quartz/).
- **Snow quartz.** A seller's name for the same cloudy white quartz. See [snow quartz](/stones/snow-quartz/).
- **Quartzite.** A metamorphic rock of tightly packed quartz grains, not a crystal variety (IGS).
- **White calcite.** Much softer at 3 (minerals.net). See [white calcite](/stones/white-calcite/) and [calcite vs quartz](/compare/calcite-vs-quartz/).
- **Feldspar.** Softer at 6, with perfect cleavage (minerals.net).
- **Glass.** Softer than quartz, warmer to the touch, sometimes with mold marks (IGS). See [quartz vs glass](/compare/quartz-vs-glass/).

## Price Guide

Milky quartz is a common, massive variety available in large pieces, and the IGS says it is little used in gems, except as cabochons of milky quartz with yellow gold specks. Amethyst Galleries notes that the trapped fluid has ruined the crystal for gem and optical work, though milky quartz is used in many fine ornamental carvings. Gem references do not price milky quartz, so the list below covers what moves the price instead.

What drives the price.

- **Use.** The inclusions that make it white have ruined the crystal for most gem and optical work, leaving ornamental carvings and the occasional cabochon (IGS, Amethyst Galleries).
- **Size.** It comes in large pieces (IGS), so a bigger specimen is simply more material.
- **Gold.** Many of the most beautiful gold specimens are lacy gold in pure white milky quartz (Amethyst Galleries), so a piece with visible gold is the exception.
- **Name on the tag.** The same cloudy white quartz also sells as snow quartz or white quartz, so compare listings by size and finish, not by name.
