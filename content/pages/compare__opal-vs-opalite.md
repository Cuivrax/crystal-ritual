# Opal vs Opalite, What Really Sets Them Apart

Opal and opalite are different materials. Opal is a natural gem, silica with water locked inside, and the October birthstone. The opalite sold in shops is usually man-made glass, made to look like opal or moonstone. It is pretty and low in value, but it is not opal and not a natural stone.

## What Each One Really Is

### Opal, Silica with Water Inside

**Mineral Fact.** Opal is hydrated silica, SiO₂ with a variable amount of water, up to about 20% ([GIA](https://www.gia.edu/opal/gem-overview)). It has no crystal structure, so [Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/opal/) calls it a mineraloid rather than a mineral. It formed when silica gel filled cracks in rock and, as the water evaporated, left the silica behind as tiny spheres (GIA).

When those spheres are uniform and neatly stacked, they split light into flashes of color. That is precious opal, and only opal shows true play-of-color. When the spheres are random, you get common opal, with no flashes ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/opal-jewelry-and-gemstone-information/)). Both are natural. Our [blue opal](/stones/blue-opal/) and [pink opal](/stones/pink-opal/) pages cover two popular common opals, and the [types of opal](/stones/family/opal/) guide covers the rest. For the stone itself, see [opal](/stones/opal/).

### Opalite Is Man-Made Glass

**Mineral Fact.** The [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/glass) defines opalite as "a trade name for a type of glass often used to simulate moonstone or opal." [Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/opal/opalite.shtml) adds that it "is often sold as 'opalite' without any disclosure of its man-made origin," which it says could be considered confusing, misleading or fraud.

The glow comes from the glass itself. Opalescent glass gets its milky translucence from tiny crystals that scatter light, in some cases produced by cooling the molten glass very slowly. Some of it shows the Tyndall effect, looking blue when light hits its surface and orange when light passes through it ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/opal/imitation-opal.shtml)). If your opalite glows blue on a dark cloth and orange against a window, this is why.

### Is Opalite a Real Stone?

It is real glass, but not a natural stone and not a kind of opal. Nothing is wrong with buying it, as long as you know what it is. Our [opalite guide](/real-or-fake/opalite/) goes further, and [moonstone vs opalite](/compare/moonstone-vs-opalite/) covers the other stone it often stands in for.

### Other Things Called Opalite

The name has wandered, so a tag that says opalite deserves a follow-up question.

- **Natural common opal.** The International Gem Society notes a natural common opal in green, lavender or purple that is sometimes called opalite, and advises against the name because of its "strong association with simulants." Geology.com uses natural opalite for common opal without play-of-color.
- **A plastic with play-of-color.** In 1988, a plastic imitation opal with true play-of-color was offered under the trade name "Opalite" at the Tucson show. Testing showed it was virtually identical to a Japanese plastic imitation already described in the gem literature ([Gems & Gemology, Spring 1989](https://gia.edu/gems-gemology/spring-1989-plastic-opal-koivula)).
- **Tiffany stone.** Geology.com describes Tiffany stone as a rare opalized fluorite from a single mine at Spor Mountain, Utah ([Geology.com](https://geology.com/gemstones/tiffany-stone/)). One gem dealer lists "purple opal" and "opalite" among its names and calls both misnomers ([Gem Rock Auctions](https://www.gemrockauctions.com/learn/a-z-of-gemstones/tiffany-stone-information)). See [Tiffany stone](/stones/tiffany-stone/).

## How to Tell Opal from Opalite

Use your eyes first and a loupe second. Skip any test that scratches, soaks or heats the stone, because the opal is the one that gets hurt.

### Watch the Light

Lay the stone on a dark cloth under a lamp, then hold it up to a window. A milky blue glow that turns orange with light behind it is the Tyndall effect that opalescent glass can show (Geology.com). It is a strong hint, not a proof.

Now tilt it. In precious opal, the colors you see change with the angle of viewing (International Gem Society). Flashes like that point away from plain milky glass, but not away from every imitation. Slocum stone, a glass, can mimic play-of-color (International Gem Society), and some play-of-color opalites are "difficult to distinguish from natural opal by eye or even with a microscope" (Geology.com).

### Use a Loupe

Glass simulants typically contain bubbles and swirl marks (International Gem Society), and molded glass may show mold marks, rounded facet edges and concave facets ([International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/glass)). Lab-grown opal, which is real opal made in a laboratory, can show a cellular, scaly or chicken-wire pattern under high magnification (International Gem Society).

### Tests to Skip

- **The scratch test.** Opal rates 5 to 6.5 (GIA) and glass 5 to 6 (International Gem Society), so a scratch proves nothing and leaves a mark.
- **The water test.** Hydrophane opal absorbs water, which can change how it looks (International Gem Society). Never dunk a stone you think may be opal.
- **The hot needle.** A hot probe that gives off a "burnt plastic" smell exposes plastic, but it is a destructive test (Geology.com). The same goes for poking a stone with a needle.
- **Weighing at home.** Glass simulants usually have a higher specific gravity and refractive index than natural opal (International Gem Society), but measuring them is a job for a gemologist with the right instruments.

An experienced gemologist or a gem lab can tell common opal, imitation opal and lab-grown opal apart (Geology.com). That is worth it before you pay opal prices.

### Three Questions for the Seller

1. Is this natural opal, lab-grown opal or glass?
2. If the tag says opalite, do you mean man-made glass or natural common opal?
3. Is it priced by the piece or by the carat? Glass is sold by the piece (International Gem Society).

Geology.com notes that the Federal Trade Commission gives guidance on how imitation and synthetic materials should be described. The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) explains the words you may meet on a tag, from "lab-created" to "genuine."

## Caring for Opal & Opalite

**Opal.** Our care data rates water, long soaks, heat and harsh chemicals Avoid. The International Gem Society calls opal sensitive to moisture and temperature changes, notes that hydrophane opal absorbs water and chemicals, and warns that opal shatters more easily than other popular gems. It advises against opal ring stones unless the setting protects the stone, the stone is a triplet or the ring is for occasional wear. Kunz noted that most opals slowly lose their water over the years (1913, page 146).

**Opalite.** "Mild soap and warm water should be safe for most non-treated and non-backed pieces," says the [International Gem Society](https://www.gemsociety.org/article/glass) about glass. Household dust is harder than most glass gems, so wipe opalite with a lint-free cloth instead of rubbing grit across it. Opalite is not in our care data yet.

Neither one belongs in water you drink. Look up any opal before a cleansing ritual in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/).

## What People Believe About Each

**Tradition.** Because opal shows the colors of other gems, the Romans thought it the most precious gem of all, and the Bedouins believed opals held lightning and fell from the sky in thunderstorms (GIA). In the Middle Ages it was the eyestone, believed to make its wearer invisible, hence the nickname "patron of thieves" (Kunz 1913, pages 146 and 148). Walter Scott's 1829 novel *Anne of Geierstein* gave opal its unlucky reputation (GIA). Kunz blamed a careless reading of the book, and cutters who broke the brittle stone and came to see it as unlucky (pages 143 to 144 and 152). Queen Victoria opposed the idea and helped make opals fashionable again (*The Magic of Jewels and Charms*, 1915, page 375).

Glass opals have a long history too. Kunz notes that Pliny the Elder praised the Indian imitations of the opalus in glass and called it the stone imitated most successfully, and Pliny's chapter on opal's flaws gives a sunlight test for glass fakes (*Natural History*, Book 37, chapters [21](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D37%3Achapter%3D21) and [22](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0137%3Abook%3D37%3Achapter%3D22), Bostock and Riley translation, via Perseus). Kunz took that as a reason to doubt that the opalus was opal at all, "for it is well known that no stone is more difficult to imitate" (1913, pages 145 to 146).

**Modern Association.** Opal is the October birthstone on the modern American list ([American Gem Society](https://www.americangemsociety.org/birthstones/)), and creativity is the link modern crystal guides make most often. Opalite has no old lore of its own, and the name appears in neither of Kunz's books. Any meaning on an opalite tag is a modern association, written for glass.

## Which to Choose

- **For a birthday.** Opal, for October. See our [October birthstone guide](/birthstones/october/).
- **For a budget.** Opalite, as long as it is labeled as glass. Glass has low value and is sold by the piece (International Gem Society).
- **For daily wear.** Neither is a tough ring stone. Opal chips and scratches easily, and household dust can scratch most glass.
- **For the look.** Love that blue-to-orange glow? That is a glass effect, and opalite does it well. For flashes of rainbow color, look for precious opal, and for soft color with no flashes, a common opal such as blue or pink opal.

**Try This.** Giving opalite? Say what it is on the card, "Man-made opalescent glass, the kind of opal copy Pliny already wrote about." An honest gift is still a lovely one.
