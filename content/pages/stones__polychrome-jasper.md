# Polychrome Jasper Meaning, Properties & What It Really Is

Polychrome jasper is a many-colored jasper, an opaque, fine-grained form of quartz, mined in Madagascar and named for its earthy reds, browns, yellows and creams, since polychrome means "many colors." It is also sold as desert jasper. Crystal lovers use it for grounding, calm and creative stamina, which are modern beliefs, and it is affordable and easy to care for.

> **Is It Real? Quick Checks.** Expect an opaque stone in earthy reds, browns, yellows, grays and cream that stays dark against a flashlight. Neon or bright blue patches are a reason to ask about dye. The name does not prove the stone came from Madagascar, so ask where it was mined.

## What Polychrome Jasper Actually Is

**The Stone.** Jasper is an opaque, solid or patterned form of quartz made of crystals far too small to see, colored by other minerals. Opacity is what separates it from agate, its translucent cousin. Our [jasper page](/stones/jasper/) covers the whole family and its many names.

"Polychrome" describes the look, not a mineral. One lapidary supplier, which sells it as tumbling rough, explains that the name means "many colors," that the same rock is also sold as desert jasper because it matches the hues of the desert where it is found, and that the original source is mines in Madagascar. The same supplier warns that some retailers use the word polychrome for colorful rocks from other places. Its exact makeup has not been described by a gem lab we could find, so the honest label is multicolored jasper from Madagascar, sold as polychrome or desert jasper.

### When Was Polychrome Jasper Found?

Recently, by sellers' accounts. One Madagascar dealer says geologists found it in 2006 while looking for more ocean jasper, and a Swiss seller dates the find to 2008. The context fits. Ocean jasper, an orbicular jasper from Madagascar's northwest coast, came on the market in 2000, and its mine reserves were exhausted in 2006. The lapidary supplier does not name the mine, so ask your seller where it was dug.

### Why So Many Colors?

Jasper is almost always multicolored, and its colors come from the other minerals mixed into the quartz. One gem dealer names iron and manganese among the impurities that add its colors and patterns. Each jasper has its own color and pattern, so no two pieces match.

### Polychrome Jasper Eggs, Palm Stones & Tumbles

Jaspers take an excellent polish and are usually cut as cabochons, sometimes carved, and seldom faceted. The lapidary supplier says this rough tumbles easily and takes a highly reflective polish, and shops sell it as eggs, palm stones, spheres and tumbled stones. When you choose an egg, turn it under a lamp and pick one whose bands you like from every side.

## Where the Meaning Comes From

**The Story.** Polychrome jasper is too new to have lore of its own. What it inherits is the long story of jasper, with a catch, since the ancient name did not always mean our stone.

- **77 CE, Rome.** **Tradition.** Pliny the Elder described iaspis as green and often transparent and wrote, "Throughout all the East, it is the custom, it is said, to wear iaspis by way of amulet" (*Natural History*, Book 37, chapter 37, Bostock and Riley translation). He also called the magicians' claims about it falsehoods. His iaspis may include other chalcedony.
- **Ancient Egypt.** **Tradition.** The buckle amulet placed on a mummy's neck was often red jasper, and heart amulets were made of green jasper among other stones (Kunz, *The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, 1913, pages 226 to 227).
- **4th century.** **Tradition.** Jasper was praised as a rain-bringer, credited with driving away evil spirits and with protecting its wearer from venomous bites. Kunz notes that this was a green, translucent stone (1913, pages 90 to 91).
- **Middle Ages.** **Tradition.** Bartholomaeus Anglicus wrote that jasper "hath as many vertues as dyvers coloures and veines." Kunz explains that color mattered as much as chemistry to old writers, so jasper's many colors and markings pointed to many uses (1913, page 91). It is a neat fit for a stone named for its many colors.
- **2006 to 2008, Madagascar.** The stone is found, according to sellers, and sold under its new names.
- **Today.** **Modern Association.** Grounding, calm and steady creative energy.

## Is My Polychrome Jasper Real?

- **Natural, untreated.** The usual case, a natural rock cut and polished.
- **Natural, treated.** Possible. Sources disagree on how often jasper is dyed. The International Gem Society calls dyeing and color-changing heat common for jasper in general, while Minerals.net says most jasper is untreated and only occasionally dyed.
- **Lab-grown.** None documented.
- **Imitation.** Fakes are scarce, though one gem dealer warns of plastic or glass knockoffs.
- **Trade name.** Polychrome and desert jasper are trade names for the same Madagascar material, and polychrome is sometimes used for rocks from elsewhere.

### How to Check Polychrome Jasper at Home

You need a flashlight and a 10x loupe.

1. **Shine the flashlight through a thin edge.** In a dim room, jasper stays dark. Impurities can let a little light through at the very edge, so a faint glow there is normal.
2. **Judge the colors.** Earthy reds, browns, ochres, grays and cream are what this stone looks like. Electric blue, purple or neon patches are a reason to ask about dye.
3. **Look into cracks with the loupe.** Dyes tend to collect along fine cracks, where they show as dark lines.
4. **Leave the swab test to a gemologist.** A cotton swab with alcohol or acetone can sometimes lift a dye, but it goes against the No Chemicals rule for this stone, and a clean swab proves nothing.
5. **Feel the weight.** Dense quartz feels heavy and stays cool in the hand, according to one gem dealer, so a light, warm-feeling piece deserves a question. It is a hint, not proof.

Three questions for the seller. Is it from Madagascar? Has it been dyed or heated? Will you write both on the receipt? The [Label Decoder](/tools/crystal-label-decoder/) helps with trade names like this one.

## How to Care for Polychrome Jasper

**Mineral Fact.** Jaspers are trouble free to care for and hardy enough for all jewelry uses. Dyed pieces should avoid harsh chemicals, so treat any stone with suspicious colors gently.

- **Water.** OK. Warm soapy water and a soft brush clean it easily.
- **Soaking.** Brief Only. Wash it, do not leave it in a bowl.
- **Sun.** Brief Only. One gem dealer suggests an hour of sun to dry it after cleaning, and our rating goes no further.
- **Heat.** Brief Only. No prolonged heat and no steam cleaners.
- **Chemicals.** No Chemicals. Skip household cleaners.
- **Salt.** Brief Only. The same dealer says to avoid salt for some jasper types.
- **Knocks and storage.** OK. Store it in a fabric-lined box or soft cloth, apart from softer and harder stones.
- **Toxicity.** OK to handle. Jasper's toxicity is rated low, and its silicosis hazard concerns cutting dust only.

### How to Clean Polychrome Jasper at Home

1. Add one drop of mild dish soap to a bowl of warm water.
2. Dip the stone and brush it gently with a soft toothbrush for about 30 seconds, never a scouring pad.
3. Rinse under running water of the same temperature.
4. Pat it dry with a towel and let it finish drying on the towel.
5. Store it in its own pouch.

### Can Polychrome Jasper Go in Water, Sun or Salt?

A wash, yes. A long soak, a salt bowl or a summer on the windowsill, no, since our ratings for soaking, salt and sun are Brief Only. Moonlight, sound and smoke, the methods shop guides suggest, are all gentler. Check your piece in the [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/).

## Polychrome Jasper vs Look-Alikes

- **Desert jasper.** The same material under another sale name. See [desert jasper](/stones/desert-jasper/).
- **Ocean jasper.** Also from Madagascar, but an orbicular jasper with small round "eyes," formed from a volcanic rhyolite that was completely silicified. See [ocean jasper](/stones/ocean-jasper/).
- **Picture jasper.** Jasper with scenic, picture-like patterns. See [picture jasper](/stones/picture-jasper/).
- **Agate.** Same quartz family, but translucent. See [agate vs jasper](/compare/agate-vs-jasper/).

## Price Guide

Jaspers in general are very common, so most of the value lies in the strength of the color, the beauty of the pattern and the skill of the cutting, and only a few rarer types command premium prices. This guide gives no figures. For an egg or a sphere, pay for crisp bands, an even polish and no cracks, and ask where it was mined before paying extra for the name.
