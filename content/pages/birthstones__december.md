# December Birthstone Meaning, History & Honest Buying Tips

The December birthstones are turquoise, tanzanite and zircon, the three stones on the modern US list published by the American Gem Society. Turquoise is the traditional one, on the jewelers' list since 1912, and tanzanite joined in 2002. All three come in blue, and most stones sold in each have been treated or heated.

## December Birthstones at a Glance

| | Turquoise | Tanzanite | Zircon |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | An opaque copper aluminum phosphate mineral | The blue to violet variety of zoisite | A zirconium silicate mineral, not cubic zirconia |
| On the US jewelers' list | Since 1912 | Since 2002 | Since 1952 |
| Colors | Sky blue to green, often veined with matrix | Violet blue to bluish violet | Many colors. Blue is the most popular |
| Mohs hardness | 5 to 6 | 6 to 7 | 6 to 7.5 |
| Usual treatment | Most is stabilized, waxed or otherwise treated | Heated, assume nearly all | Heated, for almost all blue and colorless |
| Lab-grown | Exists (Gilson, 1972) | None known | Made for research, sources differ on jewelry use |
| Best for | Pendants and pieces that avoid knocks and lotions | Earrings and pendants | Pendants, earrings, protective ring settings |

## What Is the December Birthstone?

There is no single answer, because there is no single list. Today's US lists give tanzanite, turquoise and zircon. Older lists mostly gave December the ruby. Here is what each list actually says.

| List | December stone | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Old lists Kunz attributes to the Jews, the Romans, the Arabians and Isidore of Seville | Ruby | Kunz 1913, page 315 |
| Polish list | Turquoise | Kunz 1913, page 315 |
| Russian list | Turquoise, chrysoprase | Kunz 1913, page 315 |
| 15th to 20th century European usage | Ruby, bloodstone | Kunz 1913, page 315 |
| US jewelers, Kansas City, August 1912 | Turquoise, with lapis lazuli as alternate | Kunz 1913, pages 317 to 320 |
| American Gem Society and GIA today | Tanzanite, turquoise, zircon | AGS, GIA |
| International Gem Society guide | Blue topaz, blue zircon, tanzanite as modern, turquoise and lapis lazuli as traditional | IGS, updated April 10, 2025 |

### Modern vs Traditional December Birthstones

When jewelers say "traditional," they usually mean turquoise, the December stone of the 1912 list, with lapis lazuli as its alternate. GIA calls turquoise the traditional December birthstone. "Modern" covers tanzanite and zircon. The International Gem Society also counts blue topaz as a modern option, though the AGS and GIA lists leave it out.

Go back further and "traditional" points somewhere else. **Tradition.** In 1913 the gem writer George Frederick Kunz printed a rhyme that tells December babies to "place on your hand a ruby true" (*The Curious Lore of Precious Stones*, page 331). The 1912 list moved the ruby to July and gave its place to turquoise (page 320).

### Why Does December Have Three Birthstones?

GIA's birthstone exhibit says that tradition is one reason, but "the more significant reason is that some gemstones are too rare and expensive, and others just aren't as popular." A third stone gives buyers a different price and a different look. Kunz objected to the idea. In 1913 he wrote that "there should be no duplication or triplication of birth-stones for any given month" (pages 320 to 321).

## Where Birthstone Lists Come From

**Tradition.** Month stones go back to the twelve stones on the breastplate of the Jewish high priest. In the 1st century the historian Josephus tied them to the twelve months and the twelve zodiac signs, and St. Jerome repeated the link in the early 400s (Kunz 1913, pages 307 to 310). Wearing your own month's stone came much later, probably in 18th century Poland, Kunz says (page 307).

In August 1912, the National Association of Jewelers met in Kansas City and adopted the list that later US lists grew from. Kunz printed it the next year and protested. Jewelers argued that pale turquoise suited a winter month "when the sun's rays are feeble," and Kunz answered that "it is in winter that we seek for warmth" (page 320).

The list has been updated since. In 1952 the Jewelry Industry Council of America modified the list, adding alexandrite to June, citrine to November, pink tourmaline to October and zircon to December. Tanzanite was designated a modern December birthstone in 2002. It joined turquoise and zircon, the latter on the list since 1952. The full story, month by month, is on our [birthstone chart](/birthstones/).

## Turquoise, the Traditional December Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Turquoise](/stones/turquoise/) is an opaque mineral in shades of blue, bluish green, green and yellowish green, and the most prized color is a sky blue or robin's egg blue. Chemically it is a hydrated copper aluminum phosphate. Copper gives the blue, and iron pushes it toward green. It forms in dry regions where acidic, copper-rich groundwater seeps down and reacts with rocks that hold phosphorus and aluminum. Most of today's supply comes from North America and China.

**The Story.** Turquoise has the longest written history of the three.

- **About 3500 BC, Egypt.** A necklace from an Old Empire grave carries a turquoise pendant shaped like an ibex, which Kunz read as a talisman (Kunz 1913, pages 37 to 38). Turquoise appears on the funerary mask of Tutankhamun.
- **From the 1200s, Europe.** **Tradition.** Writers said turquoise protected its wearer from falls, especially from horseback, and Turkish riders tied it to their horses' bridles (Kunz 1913, pages 108 to 109).
- **Gift, not purchase.** **Tradition.** A turquoise was believed to work only if it was given, never bought, because the spirit in the stone "was thought to take offence at the idea of being bought and sold" (Kunz 1913, page 73).
- **The American Southwest.** At Pueblo Bonito in New Mexico, excavated in 1896, nearly nine thousand turquoise beads and pendants lay with a single burial (pages 112 to 113).

## Tanzanite, the Newest December Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Tanzanite](/stones/tanzanite/) is the blue to violet variety of the mineral zoisite. Tiffany & Co. named it after Tanzania, and every stone comes from one place, the Merelani Hills near Mount Kilimanjaro. Small amounts of vanadium cause the blue. Tilt one and you see three colors in three directions, a property called pleochroism.

**The Story.** There is no ancient tanzanite lore. The identification of blue zoisite from Tanzania dates to 1962, the find at Merelani to 1967 and Tiffany's advertising campaign to 1968. Its meanings were all written in the last few decades, so you can watch a birthstone tradition being built.

## Zircon, the Brightest December Birthstone

**The Stone.** [Zircon](/stones/zircon/) is a natural zirconium silicate mineral and has been used as a gem for about 2,000 years. It is not cubic zirconia, which is made in a lab from a different compound. Most natural zircon is yellow, red or brown. Blue is the most popular color, and about 80 percent of the zircon sold today is blue. Most of that blue comes from heating brown zircon to about 1,650 to 1,830 °F (900 to 1,000 °C). Well-cut colorless zircon is bright enough to outshine a mediocre diamond.

**The Story.**

- **India.** **Tradition.** A Sanskrit name for zircon, then called hyacinth, is râhuratna, the jewel of the dragon blamed for eclipses, and it was believed to avert misfortune (Kunz 1913, pages 344 to 345). Hindu poets gave the mythical Kalpa Tree foliage "entirely formed of zircons" (page 238).
- **Middle Ages.** **Tradition.** Zircon was thought to bring deep sleep and scare off evil spirits.
- **1880s England.** Victorians had a fondness for blue zircon, and fine specimens turn up in English estate jewelry from the 1880s.

## What December Birthstones Mean Today

**Modern Association.** GIA's exhibit sums up the popular meanings in a line each. "Turquoise imparts peace upon the wearer," and "tanzanite is said to give its wearers balance."

Modern crystal guides go further. For turquoise, protection and communication are the themes they share most, and the throat chakra is the link most guides make. The protection theme echoes the old amulet against falls. Tanzanite's guides center on intuition, change and calm, with the throat, third eye and crown chakras. Zircon's guides disagree with each other on almost everything, from chakra to zodiac sign, which is what a young modern tradition looks like.

So what is the December birthstone color? Blue, in practice. Turquoise runs from sky blue to green, tanzanite from blue to violet, and zircon comes in many colors, with blue the favorite. Some crystal guides pair turquoise and tanzanite with [Sagittarius](/zodiac/sagittarius/), the sign that covers most of December.

Some sites list health benefits for these stones. Nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports them.

## How to Choose a December Birthstone

Start with how it will be worn, then the budget, then the color.

- **For a ring worn every day.** None of the three is ideal. Zircon is the hardest, but it is brittle and chips at the facet edges, so pick a setting that protects them. Tanzanite's cleavage and brittleness make daily wear risky.
- **For earrings or a pendant.** All three are good choices, since they take fewer knocks.
- **For a casual, earthy look.** Turquoise, opaque and often veined, set in silver or worn as beads.
- **For sparkle on a budget.** Blue zircon. The International Gem Society's December guide gives average retail prices for blue zircon of about $227 per carat for 1 to 2 carat stones and about $337 per carat for 2 to 5 carats. Its figure for London blue topaz, the option the AGS list leaves out, is $10 to $30 per carat.

**Try This.** The old belief says a turquoise works only as a gift, so lean into it. Wrap the stone with a card that says what it is and the one line of Kunz's lore you like best.

**Try This.** For your own birthday, set the stone on your nightstand the night before. In the morning, before you reach for your phone, hold it and write one sentence about the year you want.

**Safety.** Small stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

## Is My December Birthstone Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone. Natural untreated, natural treated, lab-grown, imitation and trade name. Treated is not fake, and for December it is the norm.

### Real or Fake Turquoise

- **Natural, treated.** Most turquoise on the market has been treated. All but the highest grades may be stabilized with wax, oil, resin or epoxy, and even top stones often get a paraffin wax coat.
- **Reconstituted.** Ground turquoise mixed with plastic. Gemologists still debate whether it counts as a synthetic, a hybrid or a simulant.
- **Lab-grown.** Synthetic turquoise exists. Pierre Gilson made the best-known kind in 1972.
- **Imitation.** Blue-dyed howlite is common and sometimes sold without disclosure. Dyed magnesite, plastics, ceramics and glass turn up too.
- **Trade name.** "White turquoise" is often natural howlite, a misleading term. "Persian blue" names an even sky blue color in the trade, not proof the stone came from Iran.

Check the underside for an epoxy backing, the sign of a doublet. Under strong magnification natural turquoise looks smooth, while Gilson synthetics show tiny blue spheres in a pale base. Acetone lifts shoe polish dye, but dye tests are destructive, a last resort. Many treatments need a lab.

### Real or Fake Tanzanite

Assume your tanzanite is heated. It is a stable treatment, and synthetic tanzanite is unknown, though many imitations exist. Tilt the stone under one lamp and watch the color shift between blue and violet. Heating can weaken that effect, so a weak shift does not rule tanzanite out. "Fancy color tanzanite" for green, yellow or pink zoisite is a misuse of the name.

### Real or Fake Zircon

Almost all blue and colorless zircon is heated, and the treatment cannot be detected. Crystalline zircon has been made for research, and the International Gem Society knows of no jewelry use, so a "synthetic zircon" listing may really be cubic zirconia. Watch for old trade names too, since colorless zircon was sold as "Matara diamonds" and blue as "Siam aquamarines." With a 10x loupe, look down through the table at the pavilion (lower) facet junctions. In zircon they appear doubled, while diamond does not show doubling.

Three questions for any seller. What is the mineral name? Has it been treated, and how? Is there a lab report, and from which lab?

## How to Care for December Birthstones

These ratings come from the same data as our [Care Checker](/tools/crystal-care-checker/), which shows OK, Brief Only, a warning that names the test such as Keep Dry, or Not Enough Data with the reason and the source.

| | Turquoise | Tanzanite | Zircon |
|---|---|---|---|
| Washing | Brief only. Porous, liquids can damage or recolor it | OK. Warm water, mild detergent | OK. Warm water, mild detergent, soft brush |
| Soaking | Brief only | Brief only | Brief only |
| Sun and UV | Brief only. Store away from bright light | Unknown | Avoid. UV can turn heated blue zircon brown |
| Heat | Avoid | Unknown | Unknown |
| Chemicals | Avoid. Absorbed cleaners can damage or recolor it | Unknown | Unknown |
| Salt | Unknown | Unknown | Unknown |
| Cleaning | Brief only. Damp cloth with very mild soap, plain water, dry | OK. Soft brush, mild detergent, warm water | Avoid ultrasonic and mechanical cleaners |
| Knocks and scratches | Brief only. Lower hardness | Brief only. Cleavage and brittleness | Brief only. Brittle, chips at facet edges |
| Toxicity | Unknown | Unknown | OK. Wearing it poses no significant risk |

Turquoise can be damaged by acids and discolored by cosmetics and skin oils, so put it on after lotion and perfume, and none of the three belongs in a steam or ultrasonic cleaner. GIA warns that tanzanite can crack under very high temperatures or sudden temperature changes, a caution our care data does not cover yet. Take blue zircon off at tanning and nail salons, where minutes of UV can turn it brown.

**Where the beliefs and the care data disagree.** Some crystal guides cleanse turquoise in sunlight or under running water. Our care data keeps both brief, because turquoise is porous and is best stored away from bright light. Blue zircon should skip the sun altogether. For a cleansing ritual, sound or moonlight keeps the stone dry, as long as you bring it in before the morning sun reaches the sill. No source in our data covers salt for these three yet, so leave salt bowls out.
