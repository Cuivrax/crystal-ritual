How to Make Moon Water Without Ruining Your Stones

By Iris Holloway

Moon water is water left out overnight under the moon, usually a full moon, then used as part of a personal ritual. Any effect is a matter of belief. If you want a crystal involved, set it beside the jar rather than in it, because some stones dissolve in water and some contain lead or copper.

Moon Water at a Glance
What it is Water left out under the moon overnight, then used in a personal ritual
What you need A clean glass jar with a lid, water, and a spot the moonlight reaches
When Most guides pick the full moon. Some use the new moon for intentions
Time One night, plus 5 minutes to set it up
Stones Beside the jar, never inside it
Never in water you drink Malachite, azurite, chrysocolla, pyrite, galena, cinnabar, stibnite, realgar, orpiment, and any dyed or stabilized stone
Keep out of any soak Selenite, fluorite, celestite, angelite, opal, coated aura quartz, and porous stones like turquoise and lapis lazuli
Drinking it Only if you started with drinking water, kept the lid on, kept it out of direct sun and no stone touched it
Tonight's phase Tonight's Moon

Tradition

What Moon Water Is & Where the Idea Comes From

The Story. Moon water is simple. You fill a jar, leave it where the moon can reach it, and bring it in the next morning. What people do with it varies. Crystal sites suggest everything from drinking it to adding it to a bath or using it as a face toner, sometimes with stones dropped straight into the water. That last habit is where things go wrong for some stones, and we will get to it.

Modern Association The idea sits inside a bigger one. Leaving stones out in moonlight to "cleanse" or "charge" them is the method modern crystal guides suggest for nearly every stone. Some of those guides split the month in two, the full moon for release and reflection and the new moon for setting intentions.

Tradition Stones and the moon have been linked in European writing for centuries, though not through water.

  • 1500s. Kunz reports a story told by Antoine Mizauld of a "selenite or moonstone" with a white mark that grew and shrank as the moon waxed and waned (The Curious Lore of Precious Stones, 1913, page 98).
  • 1500s. Kunz also tells of a stone said to belong to Pope Leo X that grew brighter as the moon waxed, and he adds that such stories were "undoubtedly fables that were circulated intentionally, or more probably through pure love of exaggeration" (The Magic of Jewels and Charms, 1915, pages 386 to 387).
  • 1500s. In Cornelius Agrippa's occult philosophy, as Kunz summarizes it, the high priest of Monday's mysteries wore a triple necklace of pearls, crystals and selenites (The Curious Lore of Precious Stones, pages 336 to 337).
  • Folk tradition. Kunz records that lovers held a moonstone in the mouth at the full moon to read their future together (The Curious Lore of Precious Stones, pages 97 to 98). Please do not try that one with any stone.

Moon water itself does not appear in either of Kunz's books on gem lore, and we found no dated source for when the practice began. Treat it as a modern practice, not an ancient one.

How to Make Moon Water

Try This The whole method fits on a sticky note.

  1. Pick your night

    Check the phase on Tonight's Moon. The full moon is the usual choice.

  2. Fill a clean glass jar and close it

    If there is any chance you will drink it, start with water that is safe to drink. The lid keeps out dust and insects.

  3. Set it where moonlight reaches

    A windowsill is fine. So is a porch or a balcony.

  4. Put your stone beside the jar, not in it

    Against the glass is as close as it needs to get.

  5. Add an intention if you like

    Write one line on a card and tuck it under the jar. Label the lid with the date and the moon's name.

  6. Bring it all in the next morning

    Some stones fade with long exposure to light, fluorite among them, so do not leave your stone on a sunny sill all day.

What to do with the water is up to you. Water a plant you care about, rinse your hands as a way to start the day, or wipe down your desk or altar.

If you plan to drink it, borrow what the CDC advises for stored drinking water. Use a clean container with a lid that closes tightly, keep your hands off the water and the inside of the container, and keep it out of direct sunlight. That advice was written for emergency water supplies, and we found no public health guidance written for moon water itself, so the plant is the easy choice.

Can You Put Crystals in Moon Water?

Some sturdy stones can take a quick rinse, but no stone needs to be inside the jar, and a stone never goes in water you drink. Our care data rates how a stone handles water, not whether water it sat in is safe to swallow, and it clears no stone for that, so the answer for anything you drink is no.

The Safe Way, Beside the Jar

This is the indirect method. The stone sits next to the jar, or on its lid, and never touches the water. It still shares the night and the ritual, and nothing can dissolve into what you drink.

Some guides go one step closer and put the stone in a small sealed glass inside the jar. We will list the stones that suit that version once a reference source clears them for a long soak and our care data clears them for toxicity. So far the Care Checker clears a long soak for only three stones, black agate, flower agate and grape agate, and only through one dealer's care table for agate in general, which is not enough for anything you drink. So we list no stone for the sealed glass yet, and beside the jar is the version we suggest.

Never in Water You Drink

Our house rule covers these stones, whatever any other guide says. Where we have a sourced reason, it is below.

Stone Why it stays out
Malachite A copper mineral, rated Brief Only for toxicity in the Care Checker. Dust from grinding it is toxic to breathe, and it reacts with weak acids
Azurite A copper mineral, rated Brief Only for toxicity in the Care Checker. Its cutting dust is toxic
Chrysocolla A copper mineral, colored by copper, and soft and fragile in pure form
Pyrite An iron sulfide that oxidizes with air and moisture and produces acids as it does, which is why rock used for concrete must be free of it
Galena Lead sulfide, rated Avoid for toxicity in the Care Checker. Many schools have removed it from student mineral kits
Cerussite, vanadinite Lead minerals, rated Avoid for toxicity in the Care Checker. Normal handling or display of a finished stone is no problem, and cutters need precautions
Realgar Contains arsenic, and water or steam can release toxic fumes
Cinnabar Contains mercury. Its toxicity risk is rated High, and it can react dangerously if accidentally swallowed
Orpiment An arsenic sulfide, like realgar. Both are toxic, and contact with them should be avoided
Stibnite Named in our house rule for drinking water, like every stone in this table
Any dyed or stabilized stone You cannot know what the dye or resin is. Porous stones such as lapis lazuli take and hold dye

Wearing or displaying finished malachite or azurite is considered safe, which is why the Care Checker rates both Brief Only for toxicity and not Handle With Care. The rule here is only about water you drink. The full water safety table lists more stones to keep out of drinking water.

Stones That Water Damages

These are not dangerous to you. Water is dangerous to them.

Stone What water does
Selenite Selenite is a clear form of gypsum, which is water soluble. Clean it by hand with warm water only and keep contact brief
Fluorite Slightly soluble in water, so never submerge it
Celestite Slightly soluble in water, per a dealer guide. Clean it only with a soft brush, mild detergent and warm water
Angelite A variety of anhydrite, which readily absorbs moisture and over time turns wholly or partly into gypsum, so keep it dry. Dealers also advise avoiding exposure to water
Opal Sensitive to moisture. Hydrophane opal absorbs water
Turquoise Often porous and absorbs liquids
Lapis lazuli Slightly porous and often dyed
Aqua aura and other coated quartz The metal film is fragile. No soaking in liquid cleaners

Stones Usually Fine to Rinse

Reference guides clean amethyst, rose quartz and moonstone with warm water, mild detergent and a soft brush. Quartz, the mineral behind amethyst, rose quartz and clear quartz, is chemically inert with most substances. That supports a quick rinse. It is not a green light for an overnight soak or for a drink. Our care data rates all four OK for a rinse and Brief Only for a long soak, because the sources describe washing, not soaking.

Not sure about your stone, or about a bracelet on elastic? Check it in the Care Checker, or see the full table of which crystals can go in water.

Five Minutes on the Night of the Full Moon

When to use it. The night of the full moon, after dinner, before the evening disappears into your phone.

You need. A glass jar with a lid, water, a small card and a pen. A stone you like, if you have one. If you do not, the ritual works the same.

Steps.

  1. 1 minute

    Fill the jar, close it and set it on the windowsill. Put your stone beside it.

  2. 1 minute

    Turn off the main light. Look at the moon, or the brightest patch of sky, and take three slow breaths, letting each one out longer than you took it in.

  3. 2 minutes

    On the card, write one thing you are done carrying this month and one thing you are keeping. Slide the card under the jar.

  4. 1 minute

    Write the date and the moon's name on the lid. Leave everything until morning.

In the morning. Water a plant with it. Read the card once more, then keep it or throw it out.

Why it helps. Whatever you believe about the stone, here is what the ritual does for sure. It gives you five quiet minutes, one written thought and a date you will meet again next month. Tradition Modern guides tie the full moon to release and the new moon to fresh intentions, so the card fits the night. What Research Says A 2023 randomized study of 108 adults found that 5 minutes a day of structured breathing, especially cyclic sighing with its long, slow exhale, improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than the same time spent in mindfulness meditation (Balban and colleagues, Cell Reports Medicine). It tested breathing exercises over one month, not moon water or stones, and participants knew which exercise they were doing.

Safety. Stone beside the jar, never inside. Keep jars and small stones out of reach of small children and pets. If you light a candle, never leave it burning unattended.
