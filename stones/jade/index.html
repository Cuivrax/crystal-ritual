A cultural and trade name for two tough materials

Jade Meaning, Properties & Real-Life Uses

By Iris Holloway

Jade stone is the name shared by two different minerals, jadeite and nephrite, tough enough to have been carved into tools and jewelry for over 5,000 years. In China jade has long stood for rank, love and long life. Jadeite can be bleached, resin-filled or dyed, often without disclosure, and many stones sold under jade names are other minerals entirely.

Modern Association

What People Use Jade for Today

In modern crystal guides, luck, prosperity and abundance lead, with protection, harmony and calm close behind. On our Crystals for Money page, jade is a Classic, our label for the strongest link in modern guides. Some guides add wisdom, friendship, love and long life, echoes of the Chinese amulets above, and several call jade a dream stone to keep by the bed. The heart chakra link is the most common, and a few guides add the solar plexus or third eye. Zodiac signs vary, with Taurus and Libra named most often.

The "why" is color and history. Green reads as growth and money, and the rank, love and long-life meanings come straight from Chinese tradition.

One crystal shop repeats the Chinese advice that jade should be worn, never boxed, "lest it turn white and die," and says a chipped piece is believed to have absorbed an attack in your stead. Those are beliefs. A chip is also a good reason to check the setting.

Some sites list kidney, skin, fever or anti-aging benefits. Nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports those claims.

How to Use Jade in Everyday Life

The Ritual. Whatever you believe about the stone, jade was made to be handled, and its toughness lets a small piece live in a pocket for years.

Daytime

A Pocket Stone You Actually Hold

Try This Carry a small polished piece in your coat pocket, as Chinese jade lovers did in Kunz's account. Before a meeting or a tense phone call, run your thumb across it three times and decide the one sentence you need to say. Give it a pocket of its own, away from keys, because jade is tough but can still scratch.

A Weekly Money Check-In

Try This On Sunday evening, set your jade on top of your budget notebook or latest statement, the way one crystal shop suggests placing it on a business card or financial document. Spend 10 minutes on three numbers, what came in, what went out and what you will set aside this week. Write the third number on a sticky note under the stone and leave it there until next Sunday.

For Someone Else

A Gift with an Old Meaning

Try This Borrow from the Chinese amulets Kunz describes. A jade butterfly for an engagement, a phoenix for a coming-of-age birthday, two figures for a best friend. Write the tradition on the card, and add whether the piece is jadeite or nephrite if the seller told you. More ideas on our crystal gifts page.

Safety. Small tumbled stones and bead bracelets are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them.

When a Stone Is Not Enough

Jade is tied to the kidneys by its very name, and some guides add fevers, skin and sleep. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If symptoms last or worry you, see a doctor, and never delay treatment because of a stone.

Mineral Fact

What Jade Actually Is

The Stone. For centuries all jade was thought to be one material. In 1863 the Frenchman Alexis Damour showed that it was two, jadeite and nephrite. Both are metamorphic rocks made of tiny interlocking mineral crystals, which is what makes them exceptionally tough. That toughness is why jade was first used for ax heads, weapons and tools, and only later for gems and talismans.

Mineral Fact Jadeite is an aluminum-rich pyroxene, and jadeite is an approved mineral species on the IMA list. Nephrite is a magnesium-rich amphibole, a name used for material made of the minerals tremolite and actinolite. So nephrite is the name of a rock, not of a single mineral species.

Jade vs Jadeite

Jade is the umbrella name and jadeite is one of the two minerals under it, so every jadeite is jade but not every jade is jadeite. The other is nephrite. Here is how they compare.

Jadeite Nephrite
Mineral family Pyroxene, sodium and aluminum Amphibole, calcium and magnesium
Mohs hardness 6.5 to 7 6 to 6.5
Specific gravity 3.3 to 3.5 3.0 to 3.3
Refractive index 1.66 to 1.68 1.60 to 1.63
Luster Vitreous to sugary Vitreous, greasy, silky or waxy
Colors White to dark green, also lavender, pink, red, yellow, orange, blue, black Usually white, cream or green, more muted
Clarity Semi-transparent to opaque Translucent to opaque

To the eye the two look much alike, which is why science took until 1863 to separate them. Chinese craftsmen got there first. A few hundred years ago they noticed that some jade from Burma was harder, denser, easier to work and took a higher polish, and it became the jade most prized in China. The rare fine-grained, bright, evenly green kind was named Imperial Jade and once reserved for the emperor. Kunz gives its Chinese name as fei ts'ui, "Kingfisher plumes" (The Curious Lore of Precious Stones, 1913, page 83).

Is Nephrite a Crystal?

Nephrite does not form single crystals with flat faces that you could buy as points. It is a rock, a tight mass of tiny interlocking crystals. Those tiny crystals are monoclinic, but the stone in your hand is a solid, translucent to opaque mass. "Nephrite crystal" on a shop label simply means a piece of nephrite.

Where Jade Comes From

Jadeite came to China from Burma, now Myanmar, and became the jade Chinese craftsmen prized most only a few hundred years ago. Nephrite's Chinese story is far older. Kunz named Khotan in Turkestan, today's Xinjiang, and New Zealand as the chief sources in 1913 (page 382). Kunz also secured an enormous 7,000-pound (3,175 kg) block of New Zealand jade, found in 1902, for the Museum of Natural History in New York (page 254).

Tradition

Where the Meaning Comes From

The Story. Jade's written history is long, and most of it is Chinese.

The Timeline, Neolithic China to Today

  1. Neolithic China Tradition

    The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds a nephrite cong, a ritual object of the Liangzhu culture dated to about 2400 BCE, and the cong probably signified wealth and social status.

  2. Early Chinese writing Tradition

    The old character for "precious" shows jade beads inside a house, and the oldest form of the character for "king" appears to be a string of jade beads, still insignia of high rank in Kunz's day (1913, pages 83 to 84).

  3. Chinese custom Tradition

    People who could afford it carried small pieces of jade to handle, believing that "something of the secret virtue of the substance is absorbed into the body." Struck jade was said to sound like the voice of the loved one, and jade was called "the concentrated essence of love" (Kunz 1913, page 84).

  4. Chinese amulets Tradition

    Kunz lists jade gifts with set meanings. "Two Brothers of Heavenly Love" went to friends, a phoenix to a girl coming of age, a man riding a unicorn to newlyweds as a sign an heir would come, and a butterfly from a groom to his fiancée as a symbol of successful love (1913, page 84).

  5. Tombs Tradition

    "Mouth-jades" were placed in the mouths of the dead for protection (Kunz 1913, pages 86 to 87). In ancient Mexico, a green stone, possibly jade, was placed in a dead noble's mouth and called his heart (pages 85 to 86).

  6. Maori New Zealand Tradition

    A carved neck pendant called hei-tiki, figuring an ancestor, was believed to pass "something of the very being of those ancestors" to the descendants who wore it (Kunz 1913, pages 87 to 88).

  7. 16th century Tradition

    The Spanish called jade piedra de hijada, "stone of the flank," from the belief that it cured kidney ailments. The word nephrite comes from the same idea. Kunz suspected that returning Spanish sailors invented the cure to raise the value of their jade (1913, pages 264 to 265 and 382 to 383).

  8. Turkey Tradition

    Kunz found jade talismans prized as heirlooms among the Turks, and a sect whose members carried a flat piece of jade for life as protection "against injury or annoyance of every kind" (1913, page 246).

  9. Today Modern Association

    Luck, prosperity and protection, described below.

Is My Jade Real?

You cannot fully confirm jade at home. Only trained observers with significant experience can reliably tell jadeite from nephrite without testing equipment, and Type B treatment often takes a gemological laboratory to detect. Here is where jade stands on the five labels we use for every stone.

Natural, Untreated

Type A is jadeite that is natural or polished with wax only.

Natural, Treated

Heat, wax, dye, bleach, acid and polymer injection have all been used on jadeite and nephrite. The Type A, B and C system below covers jadeite.

Lab-Grown

General Electric grew jadeite in the lab as an experiment, and a 1987 study reported no plans for commercial production. GE has not released synthetic jadeite commercially to date, and nephrite has not yet been synthesized. We found no reference source describing lab-grown jade sold in the US.

Imitation

Glass, soapstone and serpentine are sold as jade, as is green-dyed calcite.

Trade Name

Dozens of "jade" names belong to other minerals (see the table below).

Type A, B & C Jade

  • Type A. Finished jadeite is boiled clean, dipped in molten wax and buffed, the most traditional treatment, expected by serious buyers and generally no drag on value.
  • Type B. Jadeite bleached by soaking in hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, then impregnated under pressure with wax or a clear polymer resin. It can start to deteriorate within a few years, because acid makes some jadeite brittle and resin can discolor with time, heat and sunlight. It should always be disclosed but often is not, since it sells for a small fraction of the price of Type A.
  • Type C. Usually everything done to Type B plus dye. The dye can fade over time or with heat and light, so pay a price that assumes only a few years of use. Dye can often be detected with a microscope, spectroscope or gemological filters.

Trade Names That Are Not Jade

Trade Name. In July 2018 the US Federal Trade Commission's jewelry guides stated that an incorrect varietal name can be "misleading," "unfair" and "deceptive." Names such as Mexican jade and Transvaal jade generally refer to other minerals.

How to Tell If Jade Is Real

To know for sure whether jade is real you need a lab, but three checks at home can rule out a lot.

  1. Read the label

    Any jade with a place name or another mineral's name in front of it deserves a question. Check it against the table above.

  2. Feel the weight

    Jade is dense. Jadeite runs 3.3 to 3.5 and nephrite 3.0 to 3.3, against 2.5 to 2.6 for serpentine and about 2.6 to 2.7 for aventurine quartzite and chalcedony. A jade bead should feel heavier than a quartz bead of the same size. The difference is easiest to notice side by side, and it rules nothing in on its own.

  3. Read the price and the paperwork

    A bright green, glowing piece at a low price is a reason for caution, given how little Type B and C sell for. For an expensive item, lab confirmation usually costs a tiny fraction of the price, and any GIA report can be checked against GIA's online report database.

Folk tests you will see online, such as how cold the stone feels or how it rings when tapped, are not in the gemological sources behind this page, so we do not offer them as proof.

Three questions to ask the seller.

  1. Is it jadeite or nephrite?
  2. Is it Type A, B or C, and will you put that on the receipt?
  3. Does it come with a lab report, or can I return it after a lab test?

Mineral Fact

How to Care for Jade

Jadeite rates 6.5 to 7 and nephrite 6 to 6.5, so take some care against scratches, but both have exceptional toughness and resist breaking or chipping.

  • Water. OK. Clean with warm water, detergent and a soft brush. Soaking is rated Brief only in the Care Checker, because only washing has been confirmed safe, not long soaks.
  • Cleaning machines. Ultrasonic and steam cleaners are safe for untreated jade, but because jade may be treated, warm soapy water is always the safe choice. Acid treatments may weaken some pieces, so have a gemologist look at anything valuable.
  • Sun. Avoid long exposure. Type B resin can discolor and Type C dye can fade with heat and sunlight. Jade itself is stable to light, but you rarely know which type you own, so keep it off sunny windowsills.
  • Heat. Avoid. Heat from a jeweler's torch can harm jade, and treated jadeite is sensitive to heat. Skip hot water.
  • Chemicals. Avoid. Warm acids can affect jade.
  • Salt. Unknown. The Care Checker has no confirmed salt rating for jade yet.
  • Toxicity. Unknown. The Care Checker has no confirmed rating for jade dust or ingestion yet, so no elixirs.
  • Storage. Keep it apart from harder stones such as quartz, which rates 7.

Here the care data overrules some crystal guides. One German guide suggests jade water heated with herbs as a tea, and a US shop soaks jade in water under sun or moonlight for a face toner. Heat and sun are what treated jadeite tolerates worst, and our data clears jade for neither drinking nor long soaks. Check your piece in the Care Checker and see which crystals can go in water.

Jade vs Look-Alikes

Aventurine

A variety of quartz with abundant shiny inclusions of minerals such as mica, sold as "Indian jade." Tilt it under a lamp and look for sparkle. It is also lighter.

Chrysoprase

A translucent green chalcedony colored by nickel that may resemble fine jade. It is lighter, at about 2.6. More on chrysoprase.

Serpentine

Fine green serpentine is easily mistaken for jade by inexperienced buyers. It is softer, at 3 to 6, with lower density and refractive index than either jade.

Green Jasper

The confusion is ancient. Kunz notes that the scholar Abel Remusat, writing in 1820, saw jade in the green jasper of the Greeks and Romans (1913, pages 299 to 300). Red jasper has its own naming muddle.

Dyed Calcite

Sold as "Mexican jade" for vases, chess pieces and desk sets.

Price Guide

Jade prices span an enormous range. The best Imperial Jade can cost more per carat than high-quality diamonds, while Type B sells for a small fraction of a similar Type A piece and Type C for a small fraction of Type B. Among nephrites, white "mutton fat" jade is a traditional favorite and the most popular nephrite in Chinese markets.

Several factors drive the price of jadeite. Color comes first, ideally a very specific green. The best jadeite is semitransparent, so text read through it looks slightly blurred. Texture runs from fine to coarse, and value rises with size, all else being equal.

Color, origin and size are the main value factors for jade. Carvings are valued by the piece rather than by the carat, with artistry and provenance counting most, and the usual advice is to buy only Type A. We give no dollar range here, because the November 2026 US shop listing we found for plain "jade" did not say whether it was jadeite or nephrite, and jadeite commands higher prices. Ask which one you are buying before you compare prices.
