Labradorite Meaning, Properties & Real-Life Uses

By Iris Holloway

Labradorite is a plagioclase feldspar known for labradorescence, a flash of blue, green and gold that shows only at certain angles. Modern crystal guides tie its meaning to protection, intuition and change, ideas drawn from that hidden flash. The honest note is that its recorded history starts in 1770, so all of its lore is young.

Modern Association

What People Use Labradorite for Today

In modern crystal guides, labradorite is a Classic stone for protection, often described as a shield for the aura. Intuition and transformation come right behind, then calm and creativity. Guides also tie it to real moments, a new job, a move, the end of a relationship or a first day somewhere new, and some say artists and writers keep a piece on the desk.

The "why" is mostly the flash. A gray stone that hides its color until the light hits it right reads as hidden depths, which explains intuition. A flash that shifts as you turn it reads as change. The aurora-like colors feed the "magic" nicknames and the legend, and protection follows the idea of a stone that keeps its light inside.

Guides agree less on the details. The throat and third eye chakras come up most, with the crown close behind. Signs scatter across Sagittarius, Pisces, Leo, Scorpio and others, so treat any single sign as one guide's choice. Water is the element guides name most.

What Does Labradorite Do?

As a mineral, labradorite reflects light from layers inside it. Its uses are cabochons, custom jewelry and polished building stone. Everything else is belief about the person holding it.

Some guides go further and list blood pressure, rheumatism, gout, period pain, breathing or immunity. Nothing in the mineral sources behind this page supports those claims, and one crystal seller says plainly that labradorite "doesn't replace medical or mental-health support."

Try This

How to Use Labradorite in Everyday Life

The Ritual. The flash is the whole point of this stone, so the best rituals use it. Each one is a cue to slow down for a few seconds.

The Tilt Before a Hard Task

Try This Keep a palm stone on your desk. Before you open the task you keep avoiding, tilt the stone slowly until the color appears. It only shows at one angle. Then ask yourself one question about the task from a different side, like who would find it easy, and start there.

A Threshold Stone by the Door

Try This Set a polished piece in the dish where your keys go. When you come home, put the keys down, touch the stone and say what you are leaving at the door, a meeting, a commute, a bad mood. Guides call it a shield. Here it marks the line between work and home.

Daytime

A Pocket Stone for a New Start

Try This In the first week of a new job or a new city, carry a small tumbled piece. Each time your hand finds it, name one thing that went right today. Change is the theme guides give it, and noticing small wins is the realistic version.

For Someone Else

A Gift for Someone Starting Over

Try This Wrap a labradorite with a card that says what it is, a feldspar first found in Labrador in 1770, and why you chose it. It looks plain gray until the light hits it right. Most people going through a hard change will get the point.

Safety. Labradorite cleaves easily, so a drop onto tile can split it. Small tumbled stones are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets.

What Research Says. In a small pre-registered experiment, 48 mostly undergraduate volunteers repeated a made-up ritual at home for a week, and an EEG then showed a smaller brain response to their own mistakes on a task, with no change in how well they did it (Hobson, Bonk and Inzlicht, 2017, PeerJ, doi.org/10.7717/peerj.3363). Their self-reported feelings did not change and no stone was involved, so tying it to the tilt before a hard task is our extrapolation. Treat these as personal practices.

When a Stone Is Not Enough

Labradorite is often chosen for anxiety, stress and feeling drained by other people. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If anxiety, low mood or exhaustion lasts or feels heavy, talk to a doctor or a therapist, and never use a stone in place of treatment. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org, any time, day or night, in the United States.

Mineral Fact

What Labradorite Actually Is

The Stone. Labradorite is a feldspar of the plagioclase series. It is most often found in mafic igneous rocks such as basalt, gabbro and norite, and it is the main mineral of a rock called anorthosite. Its formula, (Na,Ca)(Al,Si)₄O₈, carries about 30 to 50 percent sodium and 70 to 50 percent calcium.

That makes labradorite less a single mineral than a stretch of a series, and the IMA List of Minerals does not give it a species entry of its own. Without a visible flash, telling it from other plagioclase takes X-ray diffraction, chemical analysis, optical tests or specific gravity on pure specimens.

What Causes the Flash?

Labradorescence is not a surface shine. Light enters the stone, strikes a twinning surface inside it and reflects back, and different surfaces reflect different colors. A 2025 gemological review traces the color to alternating layers of calcium-rich and sodium-rich feldspar, far too thin to see, known as the Bøggild intergrowth. The color tracks how thick the layers are, and a pair of layers about 150 nanometers thick in total gives blue. The International Gem Society calls the color play "iridescent like the feathers of a peacock," in blue, green, gold, yellow and purple.

Not every labradorite flashes. Labradorite is the only plagioclase with strong labradorescence, yet many specimens show none. Iridescent labradorite forms in plutonic rocks, those that crystallized deep underground, that cooled extremely slowly or went through high-grade metamorphism, which may be why some stones never grew the layers.

Why Is My Labradorite's Flash Weak or Off-Center?

It is usually normal. The quality of the flash varies from stone to stone and even within one stone. Cutting matters too. The flash shows best when the flat back of a cabochon runs parallel to the layers inside, and a stone cut at another angle shows its flash off to one side. A dull, gray-looking piece is not a fake for that reason alone.

Where Labradorite Comes From

Labradorite is named for where it was found, the Isle of Paul near Nain in Labrador, Canada, where a Moravian missionary came across it in 1770. Crystals there reach 2 feet long but are badly cracked.

  • Finland. A few deposits give superb flash, and the best was named spectrolite.
  • Madagascar and Russia. Much of the gray to black labradorite with good flash.
  • India. Small amounts of transparent stones with an inner flash.
  • Oregon. Transparent orange, yellow, red, green and clear labradorite with no labradorescence. Some holds aligned copper platelets and sells as Oregon Sunstone.

Labradorite also sparkles on buildings. Anorthosite rich in labradorite is cut into countertops, tiles and facing stone sold as "blue granite," and a building faced with it glitters when the sun hits at the right angle.

Tradition

Where the Meaning Comes From

The Story. Labradorite has no ancient or medieval lore. It does not appear in either of Kunz's books on gem lore (1913 and 1915), which makes sense for a stone first found by a missionary in 1770. What it has is a modern story, a scientific puzzle and a trade name or two.

The Timeline, 1770, Labrador to Today

  1. 1770, Labrador

    A Moravian missionary finds the stone on the Isle of Paul near Nain, and it takes its name from the region.

  2. 1920s

    Scientists take up the flash. The 2025 review cites a 1923 paper by the physicist Robert Strutt, fourth Baron Rayleigh, in its discussion of iridescent feldspar, and it cites Bøggild's 1924 work on how the layers lie, layers now known as the Bøggild intergrowth.

  3. Finland

    The best labradorite from a few Finnish deposits was given the name spectrolite by the director of the Geological Survey of Finland.

  4. 1960s, India

    Rainbow moonstone, a milky labradorite with a blue or multicolored sheen traced to southern India, was mislabeled "water opal" when first reported.

  5. Today Modern Association

    Protection, intuition and transformation lead, with nicknames like "Stone of Magic" and "frozen northern lights." These associations come from modern crystal books and seller guides.

Is the Inuit Northern Lights Legend Real?

We could not confirm it. It is a story told by crystal sellers and crystal books, and we found no written source for it older than modern crystal guides. Many of them retell it. An Inuit warrior found the northern lights trapped in the rocks along the coast and freed most of them with his spear, and the light that stayed behind is the flash you see today. One German crystal seller itself points out that the stone was only described in 1770, and one crystal encyclopedia's claim of a thousand years of use by the Red Paint People of Maine comes with no source.

That does not make the story false. Oral traditions often go unrecorded by outsiders. It does mean we present it as a story told by crystal sellers and books, not as documented Inuit tradition.

Is My Labradorite Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone. Gem references say little about labradorite fakes.

Natural, Untreated

Most labradorite you will see. Labradorite that shows labradorescence has no known treatments, and no enhancements are reported to alter the color or clarity of the milky "rainbow moonstone" type.

Natural, Treated

Transparent feldspars may be heated or diffusion treated, and lattice diffusion is listed as a typical treatment for labradorite. We found no reference source on coated or dyed labradorite sold in the US.

Lab-Grown

We found no reference source describing lab-grown labradorite.

Imitation

The same goes for glass or other materials sold as labradorite.

Trade Names

Spectrolite is fine labradorite, rainbow moonstone is milky labradorite, much Oregon Sunstone is labradorite, and "blue granite" is anorthosite rich in labradorite. None of these is a fake.

Three checks you can do at home, with their limits.

  1. Tilt it under one light

    The flash should switch on and off with the angle and come from inside the stone. The limit is that real stones can show little or no flash.

  2. Look for straight lines

    Plagioclase often shows twinning as fine parallel lines or striations. A loupe helps. This hints at feldspar, it does not prove labradorite.

  3. Look at any chip

    Labradorite breaks along two flat cleavage directions. Never chip a stone to test it.

Three questions to ask the seller. Is the color natural, with no coating? Where was it mined? Is this labradorite, or a rock like larvikite? The Label Decoder turns the words on a tag into what they usually mean.

Mineral Fact

How to Care for Labradorite

At 6 to 6.5 on the Mohs scale, labradorite scratches more easily than agate and jasper, and its two directions of perfect cleavage make it prone to breaking on impact. It is a poor choice for jewelry that takes knocks, and in the International Gem Society's words, the cleavage "is always worth minding."

  • Water, sun, heat, chemicals and salt. Unknown. We found no reference source that covers them for labradorite yet.
  • Knocks and storage. Avoid knocks. That makes pendants and earrings a safer choice than rings, and since it scratches more easily than agate, store it apart from quartz-family stones.
  • Toxicity. OK to wear and handle, with no known toxicity.

Can Labradorite Go in Water or Salt?

Our data cannot clear it yet. Guides split. One cleans it with lukewarm water and mild soap, others say to skip long soaks and salt, and one warns that salt water can wear a stone this soft. Our Care Checker rates water and salt as Unknown because no reference source we use covers labradorite yet, and our care data wins over the guides. So skip soaks and salt until one does, and treat long sun the same way. Check your piece in the Care Checker.

Labradorite vs Look-Alikes

Rainbow Moonstone

It is labradorite. The International Gem Society calls rainbow moonstones "technically a variety of labradorite," and GIA's lab reports them as labradorite known in the trade as rainbow moonstone. It looks milky because it lacks the ilmenite that turns Labrador and Finnish material dark gray. See rainbow moonstone.

Moonstone

True moonstone is orthoclase feldspar with a soft blue or white glow called adularescence, while labradorite flashes in many colors. Compare them in moonstone vs labradorite.

Spectrolite

The name for the finest Finnish labradorite, now used loosely for exceptional stones from elsewhere. See spectrolite vs labradorite.

Larvikite

A feldspar-rich Norwegian rock, a monzonite, with a blue sheen and silver tones when polished, sold as "Norwegian Pearl Granite" or "Royal Blue Pearl Granite," according to one dealer guide. It is a rock, not labradorite.

Sunstone

Some sunstone is labradorite with copper platelets that glitter, which is a different effect from the color flash.

Price Guide

Labradorite is rarely seen in mass-merchant jewelry and is mostly used by designers doing custom work. Flash drives value, since its quality, hue and brilliance vary from stone to stone. Finnish cabochon material is uncommon and fairly expensive for a feldspar, faceted gems over 20 carats are exceptional, and Oregon material is abundant from 2 to 10 carats. Natural color and schiller make feldspar more valuable, while plain gold and champagne material, sold in the trade as labradorite, is the most common.
