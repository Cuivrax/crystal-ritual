Watermelon Tourmaline Meaning, Properties & Real-Life Uses

By Iris Holloway

Watermelon tourmaline is a single tourmaline crystal that grew pink or red at its core and green around the outside, so a thin slice looks like the fruit. Modern crystal guides tie it to the heart, love and the meeting of opposites. In 1915 it was called the Peace Stone. It rates 7 to 7.5 on the Mohs scale.

Modern Association

What People Use Watermelon Tourmaline for Today

Modern crystal guides place watermelon tourmaline on the heart chakra, a fit one guide traces to chakra color symbolism. Love and courage come up most, with compassion, joy and confidence close behind. A German encyclopedia site adds understanding, joy and a feeling of being safe.

Real-life uses in the guides are gentle. People give it between partners and close friends, or keep it close during a stretch when they want to treat themselves more kindly. For love, one guide pairs it with pink tourmaline to double the tenderness.

Zodiac links are thin. Gemini, the sign of the twins, is the one that repeats, fitting for a two-colored stone. One dealer adds Virgo, plus Saturn and Venus, and one shop lists Libra, Chinese zodiac signs and the number 2. Treat those as marginal.

Some sites go much further. One dealer lists immune support, metabolism and pain relief, and one shop calls it a talisman for serious heart and nervous system conditions. Nothing in the mineral or medical sources behind this page supports those claims. One crystal guide states that it is not a substitute for medical or mental health care, and we agree.

Try This

How to Use Watermelon Tourmaline in Everyday Life

The Ritual. A slice is easy to read at a glance, two colors and a line where they meet. That makes it a good cue for small moments of choosing.

The Peace Stone on the Table

Try This Keep a slice on the kitchen table or your desk, wherever the hard conversations happen. Before you answer in a disagreement, look at the colorless band and name, out loud or to yourself, one thing you and the other person both want. Then answer. Kunz's peace symbol, put to work.

Pink & Green at Dinner

Try This Pass the slice around at dinner. Whoever holds it answers two short questions. Pink, who was kind to you today? Green, what is growing, a plant, a skill, a plan? It works with kids, roommates and tired couples alike.

A Window Slice

Try This Stand a thin slice in a window where light comes through it. Each morning when you open the blinds, let the color be your cue to pick one thing to do with care that day. Tourmaline is generally stable to light, so the sill is fine for most pieces, but skip a spot over a radiator.

For Someone Else

A Gift for Two

Try This For an October birthday, a pair of best friends or a couple, give a slice with a card. "Pink and green in one crystal. In 1915 it was called the Peace Stone." One dealer also names tourmaline as the 8th wedding anniversary stone.

Safety. Small slices and tumbled stones are not toys. Keep them away from small children and pets who might swallow them, and watch thin edges, which can be sharp.

What Research Says. In three randomized studies, two with college students (192 and 157 of them) and one with 65 adults living with neuromuscular disease, people asked to list what they were grateful for reported better well-being than comparison groups on several measures, and the clearest gain was in positive mood (Emmons and McCullough, 2003, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377). The results were self-reported, the gains did not show on every measure, and no stones were involved. Treat the habits above as personal practices.

When a Stone Is Not Enough

Watermelon tourmaline is often chosen for heartbreak, old hurts and low moods, and some sites make claims about heart and nerve conditions. A stone can be a comfort to hold, but it is not care. If sadness, anxiety or a health worry lasts, talk to a doctor or a therapist. If you are in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org, any time, day or night, in the United States.

Mineral Fact

What Watermelon Tourmaline Actually Is

The Stone. Watermelon tourmaline is pink at the center and green around the outside, and the crystals are typically cut in slices to show it off. Some have a colorless ring between the two colors. It is a pattern, not a species of its own.

Tourmaline is a supergroup of more than 30 species with the same trigonal structure and wildly different chemistry. In 1890 John Ruskin said its chemistry was "more like a medieval doctor's prescription than the making of a respectable mineral." Most gem tourmaline is elbaite, rich in sodium, lithium and aluminum, and watermelon stones usually are too.

How a Crystal Grows Two Colors

The colors come from trace elements. Iron, and possibly titanium, gives tourmaline its green and blue, while manganese produces pinks and reds. A watermelon crystal changed its recipe while it grew, starting pink and finishing green, so each slice is a record of that change.

Most of this happens in granite pegmatites, rare igneous rocks with scattered open pockets. Different pockets in one pegmatite can hold tourmaline of very different colors. The most spectacular crystals form when hot water and vapor carry the right elements into those spaces.

Tourmaline can also be zoned along its length instead of from the center out. Those stones are sold as bi-color, tri-color or parti-color. A pink rind around a green core also exists, and dealers call it reverse watermelon.

Where It Comes From

Brazil has been the world's leading tourmaline source for nearly 500 years, and its Minas Gerais pegmatites have produced much bicolor material. The American chapter is older than you might think. The first commercial gemstone mine in the United States followed an 1821 tourmaline find near Paris, Maine, and southern California later became the country's most important source. Afghanistan, Madagascar, Namibia, Nigeria and others round out the list.

Is Watermelon Tourmaline Rare?

It depends on quality. Tourmaline in general is abundant and affordable. George Frederick Kunz called the watermelon kind "rare" but "not especially costly" in 1915 (The Magic of Jewels and Charms, page 59). Today one dealer says quality material is rare, that prices have climbed as mines are depleted and that gems over 4 carats are scarce.

Tradition

Where the Meaning Comes From

The Story. Watermelon tourmaline has no ancient lore, because tourmaline was not recognized as its own mineral until 1793. Its story is modern, and some of it is surprisingly scientific.

The Timeline, 1500s, Brazil to Today

  1. 1500s, Brazil

    Portuguese explorers sent green and blue tourmaline home thinking it was emerald and sapphire.

  2. Holland Tradition

    Kunz tells of Dutch jewelers who put tourmaline in the fire and saw it attract and repel the ashes. Lapidaries nicknamed it Aschentrekker, the "Ash-Attractor." The name tourmaline comes from turmali, used in Ceylon (1915, pages 52 to 56). That trick is the pyroelectric effect, a real property.

  3. 1759 Tradition

    Benjamin Franklin wore a tourmaline in a ring on a pivot, and noted that the warmth of his finger was enough to charge it (Kunz 1915, pages 57 to 58).

  4. 1910 and 1911

    The name "watermelon tourmaline" was coined by the naturalist George Robley Howe of Norway, Maine, and appeared in a local paper in 1910, by one dealer's account.

  5. 1915 Tradition

    Kunz calls the green and red crystal with a white zone between them the Peace Stone, "sometimes called the 'Watermelon Stone,'" found in Paris, Maine, San Diego County and Brazil. He counts it among the stones of sentiment, a symbol of "universal peace" (1915, pages 58 to 59). He even explains the white band. Manganese makes the red, iron makes the green, and where they meet they cancel out. Modern gemology names the same two elements.

  6. 1915, fiction Tradition

    Kunz retells "My Tourmaline," a story by Saxe Holme (Helen Hunt Jackson) about a Mount Mica crystal, red at one end, green at the other, with a white band, that comforts a girl laid up with a sprained leg (1915, pages 51 to 52). A tale, not a test, and an early sign of the gentle reputation.

  7. Today Modern Association

    Crystal guides read the pink core as love and tenderness and the green rind as growth, and the meeting of the two as harmony between differences. Kunz's Peace Stone said much the same thing a century earlier.

Is My Watermelon Tourmaline Real?

We use the same five labels on every stone.

Natural, Untreated

We found no reference source giving the share of watermelon tourmaline on the US market that is untreated, and natural tourmalines frequently receive enhancements, so ask.

Natural, Treated

Heating and irradiation are the two most important tourmaline treatments. Heat changes are stable and undetectable, and irradiation color can fade with heat or bright light. Both are common, and irradiation can produce red, deep pink and parti-colors. One dealer adds laser drilling and fracture filling for clarity, which lower value.

Lab-Grown

Not on the market. No commercially available lab-created tourmaline exists for gem or jewelry use, since it is expensive and difficult to make. A "simulated" tag online often means a fake.

Imitation

Fakes are usually glass, plastic or cheaper stones.

Trade Name

"Watermelon" is an honest pattern name. "Reverse watermelon" is the same idea turned inside out.

Three checks you can do at home, with their limits.

  1. Look at the boundary

    Natural stones usually fade from color to color, while imitations tend to show a sharp line. The limit is real, since some natural stones have a crisp colorless ring and parti-colored tourmaline can have striking boundaries.

  2. Look for inclusions

    Almost all watermelon tourmaline shows eye-visible inclusions, so a flawless stone is suspect. Flawless natural stones are not impossible.

  3. On a crystal or slice, look at the shape

    Tourmaline crystals often have triangular or six-sided cross-sections with rounded edges and fine lines running their length. Its cleavage is indistinct, so a piece that splits cleanly along flat planes is probably not tourmaline. Tumbled and faceted stones lose these clues.

A price far below the market is one more warning sign. Ask the seller three questions. Is this natural tourmaline? Has it been heated, irradiated or filled? Where was it mined? The Label Decoder explains trade names and gives you the questions to copy.

How to Care for Watermelon Tourmaline

With a hardness of 7 to 7.5, no cleavage and only slight brittleness, tourmaline makes a good stone for everyday jewelry. Its toughness is rated fair.

Water

Not Enough Data

Unknown for soaking. The reference advice covers washing only.

Cleaning

OK

Warm, soapy water is the best way to clean tourmaline, and ultrasonic and steam cleaners are not recommended.

Sun

OK

Tourmaline is generally stable to light. The exception is color created by irradiation, which can fade with bright light or heat.

Heat

Keep Away From Heat

High heat can alter the color, and sudden temperature change can crack the stone.

Chemicals

OK

Tourmaline is not affected by chemicals. A mild wash is still all it needs.

Salt

Not Enough Data

We found no reference source on salt and tourmaline, so skip it.

Storage

OK

OK for everyday wear, with one caution for slices. Bi-colored crystals sometimes split where the colors meet, and pink tourmaline is often flawed. Store slices flat in their own pouch.

Toxicity

None known.

Can Watermelon Tourmaline Go in Water or Sun?

A wash in warm soapy water, yes. A long soak or a monthly session under running water, as one German site suggests, has no reference guidance behind it, so our Care Checker rates water Unknown. Sun is the easier one. Tourmaline is generally stable to light, so a few hours on the sill is fine unless the color came from irradiation. Skip salt, which is also Unknown, and skip drinking elixirs too. Check your exact piece in the Care Checker, which shows OK, Brief Only, a warning that names the test such as Keep Dry, or Not Enough Data, with the reason and the source.

Price Guide

The most detailed published figures come from one marketplace dealer, in a guide updated January 25, 2026. Rough runs about $3 to $8 per carat, faceted stones about $20 to $55 per carat (roughly $10 to $80 at the extremes), and cabochons about $15 to $40 per carat. The most coveted look, a green rind fading into a hot pink core, costs more than other patterns, and cat's-eye stones cut as cabochons fetch more too.

At the top end, tourmaline specimens with attractive color and crystal form can sell for thousands of dollars. For slices, tumbled stones and pendants we found no current US shop listings to quote, so judge them by what drives tourmaline prices in general. Those are color, clarity and origin, with a premium for clean stones, particularly those over 10 carats. Treatment matters too, so ask whether a piece was heated, irradiated or filled.
